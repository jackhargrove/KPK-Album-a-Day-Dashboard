# Write-up

Agalloch is a folk metal band from Portland, Oregon that formed in 1995 as part of the Cascadian black metal scene of the Pacific Northwest but became pioneers of the post-metal genre. At the time of this album, their lineup was John Haughm (bandleader, vocalist, guitarist, drummer), Don Anderson (guitar, keyboard), and Jason Walton (bass). The name Agalloch comes from the scientific name of the tree that grows agarwood, *Aquilaria agallocha*They released their debut album, *Pale Folklore*, in 1999, which featured a folksy black metal sound that was primarily influenced by Norwegian black metal band Ulver (who have an album on this list). After releasing a few EPs, they began recording their second album in 2001. At this time, Agalloch were heavily influenced by post-rock bands, particularly Godspeed You! Black Emperor. The resulting album, 2002’s *The Mantle*, combined black metal and folk metal with post-rock to create an important entry in the post-metal genre. The album was successful enough for Agalloch to receive media attention, and they began performing live shows for the first time. They released their third album, *Ashes Against the Grain*, in 2008, losing a lot of the folk sound for more traditional metal elements. Their fourth album, *Marrow of the Spirit*, was released soon after in 2010 and featured their most experimental sound to date. Both albums were very critically successful. They released their 5th album in 2014 an broke up not long after in 2016. In 2023, Agalloch reunited, and they’ve been performing together ever since.

*The Mantle* incorporates significant influence from both the post-rock and the neofolk genres into its baseline black metal sound. Neofolk, an underground genre in the 90s that Swans was a major part of, takes the sound of industrial and replicates it with the instruments of folk. Neofolk music contributed the strummed acoustic guitars to *The Mantle*. Post-rock, which you’re all familiar with and also curiously featured Swans, contributed the long, dynamic, and winding song structures to this album, as well as certain elements of the guitar sound. There was also influence taken from singer-songwriters Tom Waits and Nick Cave, who both have an album on the list. While the black metal origins are still heavily apparent in the songs with the pounding double bass drum and some of the harsher electric guitar riffs, it would probably be wrong to call this album black metal. It also takes its growling vocals from doom metal. The album’s cover is a photograph of the Thompson Elk Fountain in Portland, Oregon.

# Hargrove's review

3/3 on the metal albums! This album is really cool, it almost sounds like emo but half the vocals are growled. The guitar work is immaculate, with the electric, acoustic, and classical guitars all being used to perfection. The strumming of the acoustic guitar sets a tender but hopeless base upon which the sound is built, the electric guitar brings the heaviness and the harshness and the anger, and the classical guitar brings these gorgeous flourishes that hang above it all, tragic in their beauty. I’m a little torn on the vocals; the growling vocals are fine, but the conventionally sung vocals are amazing and express the exact same emotion that the music instills in me. Similarly, the drumming is mostly fine, but a couple of songs use the standard black metal pounding double-pedal bass drum and it just feels kind of out place. I overall appreciated the metal origins and inspiration, but it sometimes felt like they threw in those identifiable metal elements as like a signal, or even just out of inertia. I also listened to both of their next two albums, both of which are much heavier metal, and I liked both a lot, so it’s not that I’m against the metal elements per se; I just think that the way they’re used isn’t particularly well-thought out. This is mostly a nitpick though, as the album is so good at creating a cold, dark, wintery, morbid feel throughout. The acoustic guitar in particular is responsible for this, as the production makes you hear the metal of the strings more than i’ve ever heard before. There’s also an aura of desperation around folk music that’s used well here. I very much enjoyed this record.

“A Celebration for the Death of Man…” is just a short instrumental opener, but it sets a dark and foreboding tone that the rest of the album matches. It also introduces the listener to the acoustic guitar, which weeps quietly and softly. The drum used on this track is a complete omen of darkness, and the electric guitar sounds evil when it begins. “In the Shadow of Our Pale Companion” is easily a top 10 track we’ve listened to so far, a 15 minute epic of despair and meaninglessness in the face of death. The classical guitar melody that occurs throughout the song is positively aching, and the strumming of the acoustic guitar continues from the opener to the same great effect. As I said earlier, the growling when it comes in is fine, but I was really pleasantly surprised in verse 2 when we got some normal singing. This track is just so dynamic, separated into so many different sections, it absolutely justifies its 15 minute run-time. Amazing, amazing track. It’s followed by “Odal”, an instrumental that begins with some great angular guitar work. This is one of the least metallic and most post-rock tracks, and as such it builds in an interesting way. While I would have preferred this to come later on the album I still enjoy it, especially the lonely, desolate piano at the end and how the wind blows everything away. “I Am the Wooden Doors” is the first song that I would classify as truly metal, given its the first to use the double-pedal bass drum and has some harsh riffs from the electric guitar. I really like the vocal melody and performance on verse 3 (the one that’s not growled), and the classical guitar is fantastic in both its flourishes and its solo (which blew me away, truly).

“The Lodge” has a fantastic transition from the previous track, and I’m still loving the acoustic guitar as the base. It’s around this song where I realized that what I really enjoy about it is the strumming, as opposed to the riffs or tremolo playing I expect from metal. There are also really nice strings (E-Bow, actually?) that feel totally and completely downtrodden. And I always love a good pregnant pause. “You Were But a Ghost in My Arms” opens with some of the most stellar vocals on the album. The double-pedal bass drum is back, and the guitars radiate distortion in all directions. The instrumental section from about 5:30 - 6:15 is beautiful. “The Hawthorne Passage” is a good instrumental piece, but I feel like it doesn’t fully justify its sizable run time. What it does justify is a great sample from Ingmar Bergman’s masterpiece film *The Seventh Seal*, which I thought fit the mood of this album perfectly. “…And the Great Cold Death of the Earth” says the album title!!!!!!! The classical guitar on this one almost sounds like a harpsichord, and it’s a very cool moment when they bring back the acoustic guitar line from the opener. “A Desolation Song” is a great closer that reminds me of “Mosquito Song” from *Songs for the Deaf*. The accordion in the background creates a unique effect that separates it from the rest of the album. The instrumental is so open and airy, but the vocals are claustrophobic.

Great record. I see myself revisiting this, as well as other Agolloch records, frequently. It makes me yearn for more folk, which we’ve had very little of so far. And it’s another album where the music sounds exactly like the cover art.

Favorite tracks: In the Shadow of Our Pale Companion, I Am the Wooden Doors, The Lodge, You Were But a Ghost in My Arms, …And the Great Cold Death of the Earth, A Desolation Song

Least favorite track: The Hawthorne Passage

# Aidan's review

Really cool record, through a lot of it sounds more like indie rock than metal. They list Godspeed as an influence, but the guitar (especially the rhythm guitar) reminded me a lot of Brand New. The vocals are fantastic whenever they aren’t growled (which is surprisingly often) and the acoustic guitar is used (but not overused) really well. There are parts where it lapses back into a more metal sound (kinda feels like them doing what they know), and while I thought those were the weakest parts of the album, it’s not unpleasant. In contrast, the two longer songs (not typing out those names)  are fantastic in setting a mood and really delivering on what the album wants to be. I’m also not a huge fan of the production on the drums, going by their influences, there should be more power coming from them, but it sounds muffled like some of the other metal tracks we’ve heard. I also found myself wishing that they embraced silence more, another defining trait of postrock and neofolk that didn’t transfer over. I think it’s an album that I should come back to more, as I found myself liking it more once I got over my little gripes. It’s a good record, but it feels like it could be great.
