# Write-up

Albert Ayler was a jazz saxophonist born in 1936 and raised in Cleveland, Ohio. Ayler’s father was a semi-professional musician who taught him how to play alto sax and introduced him to swing and early bebop jazz. His family was religious, which led to his lifelong obsession with spirituality in his art. Ayler became involved in Cleveland’s jazz scene as a high schooler in the early 50s, where he earned the nickname “Little Bird” in reference to legendary bebop saxophonist Charlie “Bird” Parker. Ayler enlisted in the US Army in 1958, where he picked up tenor sax. He was discharged in 1961, at which point he began searching for musical work in Cleveland and LA. His style was already prohibitively experimental at this point, however, prompting him to move to Sweden in 1962 as continental Europe has more of a market for avant-garde music. Ayler briefly played as a member of Cecil Taylor’s touring band not long after. He moved to NYC in 1963, where he began playing with Taylor and Ornette Coleman.

Ayler’s breakthrough year was 1964, during which he recorded his most famous records. One of these records, *Spiritual Unity*, released in 1965 and became his signature album. Credited to the Albert Ayler Trio, it features Ayler on tenor sax, Gary Peacock on double bass, and Sunny Murray on drums. The album was recorded at the behest of Bernard Stollman, a lawyer and Esperanto evangelist with ideas of starting a record label for experimental music who heard Ayler play at a New Year’s Eve concert in 1963. Ayler recorded two albums to finish out his contract with Atlantic Studios, then signed to Stollman’s upstart ESP-Disk record label in 1964. *Spiritual Unity* was recorded in one take in a studio near Times Square in May 1964. The album received mixed reviews upon release due to its far-out sound. However, it proved extremely influential on other jazz musicians in the process of developing the free jazz scene. Most famously, John Coltrane became enamored with Ayler’s playing on this album, convincing Impulse! Records to sign him. After recording 1965’s *Ascension*, his most notable foray into free jazz, Coltrane called Ayler to tell him that he had played just like Ayler. Retrospectively, *Spiritual Unity* is considered one of the finest jazz albums of the 1960s.

After *Spiritual Unity*, the Albert Ayler Trio became the Albert Ayler Quartet with the addition of trumpeter Don Cherry (who played on Ornette Coleman’s *The Shape of Jazz to Come*). Though Ayler left for Impulse! in 1966, ESP-Disk grew into an important record label for both free jazz and experimental rock. Starting in 1966 Ayler began to experiment with a new instrument: his voice. Ayler’s singing, which resembles the freedom and atonality of his sax playing, has polarized jazz aficionados. When Coltrane died in 1967, Ayler was one of two musicians he asked to play at his funeral. The other was Ornette Coleman. In the late 60s, Ayler began releasing slightly more accessible music after pressure from his label. These records pissed off both critics and audiences, causing them to perform poorly. Ayler began to lose his mind in 1969. He disappeared in 1970. Twenty days later, his body was found in the East River. He was 34 years old when he died. He most likely killed himself, though there is a popular conspiracy theory that he was murdered by the mafia.

# Hargrove's review

We have truly entered the home stretch of the album a day project. I am now writing a review of our penultimate jazz album. The only remaining jazz album, *Getz/Gilberto*, is a calm, melodic, and relaxing affair. By contrast, Albert Ayler’s *Spiritual Unity* is abrasive, aggressive, and avant-garde. Including today’s album, we’ve listened to 13 jazz albums classified as “avant-garde jazz”, and I’ve greatly enjoyed quite a few of them: Ornette Coleman’s *The Shape of Jazz to Come*, Miles Davis’ *Bitches Brew*, Charles Mingus’ *The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady*, and Matana Roberts’ *Coin Coin Chapter One: Gens de couleur libres* all sit at A Tier or higher for me. However, these 13 avant-garde jazz albums include four categorized as “free jazz”, a genre I’ve still found completely impenetrable. First we had Peter Brötzmann’s *Machine Gun*, which overwhelmed me with its triple saxophone attack. Then there was Sun-Ra’s *Atlantis*, a deeply irritating mishmash of electric piano and cacophonous hand drums. And then only two weeks ago we listened to Cecil Taylor’s *Unit Structures*, an analytical piece that went over my head and left me cold. *Spiritual Unity* was my final chance to crack this uncrackable genre. For better or for worse, it left me with mixed results. On the positive side of things, I liked it significantly more than any of the three other free jazz albums I just listed. Even at its most challenging, Ayler’s sax is just so Goddamn expressive. It satisfies my need for a fundamental emotionality that provides a warmth not found in any of the other albums. Part of that was due to the the trio setup, which allowed me to get to know Ayler much more intimately than Taylor’s septet, Brötzmann’s octet, or Sun-Ra’s 13-man ensemble. When you’re conveying chaotic freedom and raw emotion, I just think reducing the number of variables is the way to go. Even though I did enjoy this album more, however, I don’t think I succeeded in breaking into free jazz as a style. I still found the rhythm section prohibitively unstructured. I also found the sub-30 minute runtime a huge plus for this album, indicating that perhaps part of what I enjoyed about it was simply that there was less of it. I re-listened to all three of Brötzmann’s, Sun-Ra’s, and Taylor’s albums today, and I still don’t care for them at all. Oh well. I hopefully have a long life in front of me and can get there eventually. In the meantime, I’ll enjoy bits and pieces of Ayler’s sax.

The playful melody that opens and closes “Ghost: First Variation” is my favorite moment from any free jazz album I’ve heard. Of course, it melts into chaos within the first minute, but I like Ayler’s sax throughout. In fact, the worst part of this track is when the sax drops out for a minute. The bass and drums both feel more or less random to me. Thankfully, the melody comes back at the end. As “The Wizard” starts, all I can think about is how confusing I find Murray’s drumming. It’s like he’s only playing cymbals, like a wide array of cymbals, and is hitting them near-constantly. I still enjoy the parts of the track when Ayler is playing, but not quite as much as the opener. “Spirits” is the album’s token slow track. I never like it when these chaotic jazz albums get so slow. I think they work better at a frenetic pace. There’s a silver lining in that it’s the only track where Peacock’s bass stands out to me, but it’s easily my least favorite track. “Ghosts: Second Variation” is twice as long as the first variation, which is kind of a tough ask here at the end of the album as I start to lose my patience. Thankfully, Ayler goes fucking crazy on the sax to make it worth my while. I don’t even know for sure whether I like it, but I am certainly in awe of it. It even starts to meld in nicely with the drums at points.

I’m still not sold on this album as a whole, but I liked it enough to justify sneaking it in near the bottom of C Tier. After a day of re-listening, my final free jazz rankings stand at Albert Ayler > Peter Brötzmann > Cecil Taylor > Sun-Ra. Ayler is the only one to get his record up into C Tier, though, an impressive achievement.

Favorite tracks: Ghosts: First Variation, Ghosts: Second Variation

Least favorite track: Spirits
