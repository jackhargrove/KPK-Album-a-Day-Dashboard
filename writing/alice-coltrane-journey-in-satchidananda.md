# Write-up

Alice Coltrane, born Alice McLeod in 1937 was a jazz pianist and harpist who was married to the famed jazz saxophonist John Coltrane. She herself was no musical slouch, having been raised in a musical household and played in Detroit and Paris. Her first husband was also a jazz man, Kenny "Pancho" Hagood, who was a jazz vocalist whose contributions included a role on Davis's "Birth of the Cool" as well as work with Thelonious  Monk and Charlie "Yardbird" Parker. She left Pancho after having a daughter with him due to his heroin usage and met John soon after, himself having overcome a heroin addiction in years prior. They both became increasingly spiritual, leading to John's spiritual development as showcased on records like "A Love Supreme". After John's early death from liver cancer, Alice found herself lost, losing weight and seeing things, until she began following the teachings of Swami Satchidananda, a guru who had given the opening address at Woodstock '69. She adopted the name Turiyasangitananda and incorporated Hindu stylings into her already heavily spiritual music.

Journey in Satchidananda was her fourth solo album, all released after John's death and features legendary saxophonist Pharoah Sanders, as well as Cecil McBee and Charlie Haden(who played on “The Shape of Jazz to Come”) on bass and Rashied Ali on drums. She was one of the rare female bandleaders in jazz when the album was released, a distinction that was helped by the enormous respect accorded to her late husband. Sanders and Ali had previously played with John and "Something About John Coltrane" is based on theme he created. In addition to the piano, Coltrane plays the harp, a rare instrument for jazz. Before he died, John had ordered a concert harp for her and its arrival after his death was a major event in her journey to restart her musical career. Coltrane believed that the best way to experience the album was to lie flat on the floor, "floating on an ocean of Satchinandaji’s love". Soon after releasing this album, she packed up and moved to California, and established the Vedantic Center there. Satchidananda was not the only guru she found herself enthralled by, as she spent time extolling the Hare Krishna movement, founded by A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, and also later devoted herself to Sathya Lai Baba. Radiohead are notables enthusiasts of her work. Her grandson, Flying Lotus and metal band Sun O))) also related a tribute song after her death in 2007.

# Hargrove's review

Worst album with a harp we’ve heard so far!!! Still pretty good though! This was a really interesting listen. I was skeptical of the idea of spiritual jazz as a genre when we did *A Love Supreme*; I kind of assumed that it was like “conscious hip hop” or “viking metal”, a genre defined by its themes more than its sound. However, the ways in which this album is similar to *A Love Supreme* and not to other jazz records I’ve heard have done wonders in teaching me the musical qualities of spiritual jazz. Given n=2 for me, this could all be way off base and not applicable to the genre broadly, but still. The linearity stands out to me in both records. All jazz I’ve heard from bebop afterward generally takes a cyclical approach to songwriting, usually head-solos-head. In spiritual jazz, though, I’ve noticed the songs don’t really loop back around (with the exception of the bass line at the open and close of “Something About John Coltrane”). This presents differently in the two spiritual jazz records I’ve heard though. In *A Love Supreme*, the compositions are constantly moving forward, with new ideas and sections coming constantly. The compositions on this album, by contrast, feel almost frozen in place; the focus will occasionally jump from harp/piano to sax to tanpura/oud, certainly, but so much of this album has the same sonic character throughout. This isn’t (entirely) a bad thing, however. At its best, it helps to create an atmosphere that is positively hypnotic. There are parts of the opener “Journey In Satchidananda” where I completely lose myself, and again at the beginning of “Something About John Coltrane”. There’s such a psychedelic character as well. There are times where I feel like I’ve entered a dream world, especially during the pulsating rhythms and high-register harp solos of “Shiva-Loka”. This is just a great album to sit and zone out to. At the same time, four of the five tracks are too static to justify their lengths. As much as I was enjoying things, I found myself consistently checking how much time was left when I listened to every song but “Stopover Bombay”. The opener comes closest to justifying its length, as it’s the track where everything is just firing on all cylinders: the bass is a beautiful foundation, the tanpura is droning wonderfully, Sanders kills it both in the smooth and the frantic, and Coltrane’s harp is angelic. But either cut a minute or move from point A to point B. The closer, “Isis And Osiris - Live At Village Gate, NY, 1970”, is the longest and the least dynamic, making it the one I least looked forward to listening to. There were times where I wasn’t sure whether I had gotten lost in the music, or simply zoned out. There is a lot to love about this album and I think it could be a transcendent listen in the proper setting. As it is now though, I have too many gripes to put it in the A Tier. One of the coolest albums atmospherically though.

Favorite tracks: Journey in Satchidananda, Shiva-Loka

Least favorite track: Isis And Osiris - Live At Village Gate, NY, 1970

# Aidan's review

Probably my favorite jazz album so far, it's immensely successful as a psychedelic piece, especially on the first 3 tracks. The fuzziness of the tanpura is great, the harp add a lot, and the interplay between Sanders on sax and McBee on bass is amazing. I think this is the most strings melodies we've heard from a jazz album so far, and it creates a really laid back feel that fits well with the psychedelic affect. Sanders' sax is great, it's soulful without being intrusive, and allows for a full soundscape to be appreciated. I didn't find the harp particularly standout, but as a contributor to the atmosphere, it was very well done. The opener is by far the best track, and while I found Something About John Coltrane a bit too noodle-y, the second half of Isis and Osiris was a fitting end to the album, with a nice little oud melody. While it's obviously shares a genre and surname with A Love Supreme, I thought these albums were pretty different, and while I liked the religious repetition of A Love Supreme, I found the atmospheric work here to be even better. Probably a really nice album to get high to, but was great while sober.
