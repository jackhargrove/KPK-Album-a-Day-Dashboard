# Write-up

American Football was supposed to be an afterthought. The band's roots start in Buffalo Grove, a Chicago suburb where the Kinsella Brothers, Mike and Tim grew up. When Tim was 15, he founded his first band, Toe Jam with a few high school friends. When their drummer decided that being a star running back was better than playing drums, Mike was brought in on drums. The band brought on Victor Villarreal on guitar and Sam Zurick on bass before changing its name to Cap'n Jazz. All 4 went to Wheeling High School together but the band persisted after they graduated, eventually getting serious enough to record at Idful Music, the same studio that had recorded Liz Phair's Exile in Guyville. They brought in Davey van Bohlen as a second guitarist, and after a few EPs, decided to record a full album. The result, officially titled "Burritos, Inspiration Point, Fork Balloon Sports, Cards in the Spokes, Automatic Biographies, Kites, Kung Fu, Trophies, Banana Peels We've Slipped On, and Egg Shells We've Tippy Toed Over" and referred to everywhere as "Shmap'n Shmazz" was released on a small label to little note.

The album was inspired by post-hardcore bands that Tim was a fan of, including Fugazi and the offshoot genre, emo. The original emo band dated back to 1983, with Rites of Spring, which took the aggressive musical style of post-hardcore, added more melody and sung about more "emo"-tional topics than the typical hardcore band, like personal hardships and romantic struggles. They inspired several similar local bands, including Embrace, which was founded by Ian MacKaye( who would eventually front Fugazi), but the scene, which would retrospectively be called the first wave of emo, died out in a few years. The genre regained popularity a decade later in the Midwest, where Cap'n Jazz and peers took a softer approach, with repeating guitar parts and amateurish singing. These bands would get grouped together as "midwest emo" or "second wave emo". This was followed by a more pop-oriented third wave with bands like Fall Out Boy, My Chemical Romance and Jimmy Eat World. Brand New technically also falls into this category, although they musically are closer to the second wave. The fourth and as of yet final wave was a reaction to the excesses of the third wave and sought to go back to midwest emo, with bands like Modern Baseball, the Hotelier and The World Is A Beautiful Place And I Am No Longer Afraid To Die. As you may have guessed, emo bands are not known for being terse.

Cap'n Jazz did some light touring to support their first album, but the heavy drug use of Zurick and Villarreal caused issues. Both were heavy users of whatever they could get their hands on, and when the band had to take Villarreal to the hospital after OD'ing on Ritalin during a show, the band decided to disband. Each member founded another band. van Bohlen founded The Promise Ring, Tim Kinsella founded Joan of Arc, Villarreal and Zurick founded Ghosts and Vodka, and Mike Kinsella went to the University of Illinois Urbana-Champagne and founded American Football.

American Football was not a very serious band. It was Kinsella's second band at UIUC, after The One Up Downstairs. He, the drummer from The One Up Downstairs, Steve Lamos, and his roommate Steve Holmes ended up hitting it off and jamming together. The band started out doing instrumental songs and put one 3 song EP to tape. The band took its name from a poster that read "Come see American football, the most overpaid athletes in the world". In 1999, as college ended for the year and Kinsella graduated, the band decided to make a real record for the hell of it. Kinsella took lines from old journals and just yelled them out over already written songs until they had something. They snapped a picture of a nearby house to put on the cover, sent the record to a local label to put out and broke up. The album was liked, not loved, and as such, the record from 3 random college kids didn't really sell. Pitchfork managed to find it and give it a 7.5/10, calling it "maddeningly benign" and saying "they don't suck terribly". The reviewer spent most of his word count complaining about this album made him not hate emo.

Over the prior years, Cap'n Jazz had begun to acquire a cult following. Midwest emo as a musical movement had largely wrapped up by 2000, but it was beginning a second life online, as music fans began to take notice. There was enough demand for Cap'n Jazz to issue an anthology titled "Analphabetapolothology" and American Football starting getting the same degree of buzz. /mu/ was one of the earlier proponents and retrospective reviews have been far more positive. The band has gotten back together for a very bad sophomore album and a pretty good third album (Hargrove likes the Hayley Williams feature). The random house in the cover photo has become a destination for emo music fans, and the band ended up purchasing the house to preserve it. All of their releases are titled American Football.

# Hargrove's review

Rounding out the single greatest week of this project we’ve done so far is another one of my favorite albums. I have made it clear many times that emo is a genre near and dear to my heart and, though American Football didn’t exactly make the loud and aggressive style of emo I prefer, I’ve grown to love it just as much. Its mellowness was an impediment to me getting into it in the first place, though. I’ve also previously mentioned that The Dismemberment Plan’s *Emergency & I* was the first record I declared my all-time favorite after getting into music in 2017. My dad was the one who told me they called that record “emo” in the 90s. I thought I hated emo because my sister was very into My Chemical Romance and Fall Out Boy, and this threw me for a loop. But I decided to look up other critically acclaimed emo albums from the 90s because I liked *Emergency & I* so much and I wanted anything that sounded remotely like it. I was unanimously told *American Football* by the internet, but when I listened to it, I was sorely disappointed. There was no frantic energy, no semi-spoken vocals, no burning fire at all. It was just slow, sad, mostly instrumental guitars. I gave up after a listen. I spent that entire Summer listening to lots and lots of music. That autumn, I randomly decided to give *American Football* another try. Now, with a better trained ear, I had the patience to actually listen. I was floored. “Never Meant” simply had to be the greatest song ever written. “Honestly?” made me feel bittersweet nostalgia for my teenage years *even though I was 16*. It was aching, angsty, smoldering, swirling, unstable. I realized I had fucked up the first time. This feeling was kicked up to 11 just two years later after I graduated from high school. The summer between high school and college was very eventful for me in a stereotypical high school movie way. I was having trouble dealing with everything from leaving my friends to experimentation with drugs and alcohol to my first sexual entanglements. Listening to this album entirely about the feelings of the summer before college during your summer before college is an experience like crack cocaine. You all have a duty to introduce this album to your children before then. Their third LP, creatively titled *American Football* and often referred to as “LP3”, also came out around this time, and I cannot recommend it enough. I am not yet in my mid-30s, but I could feel that album gave the same thought and care to the emotions of that age as LP1 did for being a teenager. And the guitars are nearly as beautiful.

Since then, I’ve purchased this album on vinyl and listened to it consistently. Different parts stand out to me now that they did then. “Honestly, I can’t remember teen dreams/All my teenage feelings and their meanings/They seem to see-through to be true/All the who’s are there/But the why’s/The why’s are unclear” has to be the single greatest meditation on teenage nostalgia ever. It doesn’t hit for me as hard as it once did, but that’s okay. It’s not meant to. What makes this record so special is its honesty, its sincerity, its earnestness. It could only have been made by teenagers…except it wasn’t. Mike Kinsella was 22 when it was recorded. Everything is presented through the lens of memory; recent memory, certainly, but inexorably fading. As I revisit this album, it feels duller and dimmer with each passing year. With each listen, more of the “intensity” slowly moves into being “memory of intensity”. I don’t know that I’ll ever feel it so strongly again. I don’t know that I’ll ever feel anything so strongly again. When I would listen to this album back in 2019, I thought I felt what Mike Kinsella was feeling. Unfortunately, I’ve now learned that wasn’t true; I am only *now* feeling what Mike Kinsella was feeling. Man, is it weird. But what can you do? Not much. You can only surrender yourself to the swirling guitars, the unsteady drums, the amorphous song structures, the resigned vocals. And are they ever resigned.

Frankly, I don’t understand how a group of college kids from the Midwest put together a song as transcendent as “Never Meant”. It’s the same feeling I get with Slint. The instruments float together intertwined, combining and disintegrating again and again, always slipping through your fingers. It’s hypnotic. Just a perfect song. My hot take on this album is that “The Summer Ends” is the worst track, though it’s still good. It gets it together by the second verse, but the first verse is a too slow and unfocused for my liking. It’s also my least favorite use of the trumpet. It does deserve credit for its titular line, however, as it’s so important to the vibe of the album. I’ve already mentioned my feelings on the lyrics of the first verse of “Honestly?”, so here I’ll just harp on the guitar. What a stellar riff and, more importantly, what an outro. The song opens with the most intense vocal delivery you’ll find on this record, but it just gives way to four minutes of repetition to think on it. It haunts me every time. “For Sure” does a better job with the trumpet and provides a nice short calm after the storm that was “Honestly?”. “You Know I Should Be Leaving Soon” kicks off a trilogy of mid-length tracks with titles that work in sequence. All three are good. This one is nominally an instrumental track, but that lead guitar squeezes out as much emotion as any human voice. The last section of “But the Regrets Are Killing Me” is my favorite, but all of it is good. “I’ll See You When We’re Both Not So Emotional” has my favorite title track drop on its final line. This “trilogy of tracks is just solid, good stuff. “Stay Home” returns to the epic character of the album’s beginning with an eight minute journey. I love the missing beat feel given by the 7/4 time, it always feels like it’s tumbling forward haphazardly. And the guitar just shimmers. It’s unfortunately on the weaker side lyrically, but the instrumental more than makes up for it. “The One with the Wurlitzer” sticks the landing. It’s a short instrumental track that brings back the trumpet and just lets you reflect for the final few moments. There’s nothing too special in isolation, but it serves its role.

I think I’ve already said just about everything I have to say. It sucks that this record is so tied to a certain time of life that I’ve already left far behind, but then that’s the only way it could burn so bright in the first place. And I’m glad that I have something I can store some of my teenage feelings in forever and always have convenient access to it so long as Mike Kinsella never gets into a tiff with Spotify.

Favorite tracks: Never Meant, Honestly, For Sure, You Know I Should Be Leaving Soon, But the Regrets Are Killing Me, I’ll See You When We’re Both Not So Emotional, Stay Home

Least favorite track: The Summer Ends

# Aidan's review

It's insane that nobody has managed to properly ape this stuff. I guess the guitar parts are really that gnarly, but this album alone has managed to become my mental image of midwest emo, a genre that feels more imagined than real at this point. Besides this, there's a single Cap'n Jazz album, a few meh spinoffs, and yet there's TikToks of teens talking about midwest emo like it's as fully fledged a genre as Detroit techno or Southern Rap. I guess this album is just that good that it's managed to cause an entire generation of zoomers to mass hallucinate a genre. The lyrics are sparse, but less is often more here, there's not any sort of attempt to create emotion through verbosity, what is said is true, and the amazing guitars are allowed to just flow. Every time they go through a loop it's just more impressive how tight the guitar bits are, they're so obviously complex yet they just flow like it's nothing, they're not supposed to be flashy, they just happen. Perfect album for the end of summer, it just feels like the waning days of a sticky August, as responsible begin to loom and the glow of summer is fading with the sunset. One of those albums you have to listen to at least once because nothing else like it sounds half as good.
