# Write-up

"Moanin'" is officially titled "Art Blakey and the Jazz Messengers", but owing to it being the second eponymous record from the group, it is ubiquitously referred to as by its lead and most popular track. The Jazz Messengers started out as a collective featuring Blakey, Horace Silver, Kenny Donham, Hank Mobley and Doug Watkins. The group fell apart but Blakey kept the name and put his in front of it. The group put out several records in the emerging hard bop genre of jazz, including one aptly called "Hard Bop". The most famous lineup of the band and the one that produced "Moanin'" was Lee Morgan on trumpet, Benny Golson on tenor sax, Bobby Timmons on piano, Jamie Merritt on bass, and Blakey on drums. Blakey started learning music on piano, but switched to drums, possibly at gunpoint if one legend about being forced to move for Erroll Gainer (if you've heard of the movie Play Misty For Me, he's the guy who wrote Misty). He played with many legends of jazz before striking out on his own to form the Jazz Messengers, including Miles Davis, Charlie Parker, Dizzy Gillespie and Thelonious Monk. The Jazz Messengers had their own list of famous alumni, notably King of the Hill actor Chuck Mangione and Wayne Shorter, whose record "Speak No Evil" was our first jazz album. The Jazz Messengers continued with a rotating cast of young musicians throughout Blakey's lifetime, until he died in 1990 from lung cancer.

The original lineup of the Jazz Messengers are often viewed as among the pioneers of hard bop. To grossly truncate the history of jazz, jazz went from the "swing" era of dance music in the early 20th century to a more cerebral style known as "bebop" in the post war years. The former was the peak of jazz’s popularity, but the latter is where the most famous artists not named Armstrong or Ellington cut their teeth, including Parker, Davis, Monk, Max Roach, and Ella Fitzgerald. By the time the mid 50s hit, bop had become played out and artists began to search for a new style to play. Hard bop was one of several evolutions out of that era of bop. While cool jazz or West Coast jazz eschewed the grand flourishes that bop centered in favor of a more laid back style that incorporated some of the elements of swing. There was also modal jazz, which uses modes (one of the 3 things Carms autistically texts about). Hard Bop incorporated gospel elements and melodies that are often described as "funky". The genre hit its peak in the years surrounding 1960 and by the late 60s had ran its course. "Moanin'" is among the key albums of the genre.

# Hargrove's review

Now THIS is jazz music. As an avid fan of the drums, I was really disappointed by how much I hated that Max Roach album. And I still do. It’s really not good. The most insulting part is that the drums are never even particularly remarkable! Not here though. The drums are consistent in the background of the first three tracks, but you’ll occasionally get little unexpected bursts of the most booming drum sound you’ve ever heard. I have no clue how Rudy Van Gelder engineered the drums to sound the way that they do. That teases you until you get to “The Drum Thunder Suite”, which is one of the best jazz compositions I’ve ever heard. Insanely cool use of the drums, and Blakey was clearly a master. Hard bop is a cool genre; I was saying to Aidan earlier that I think it’s the first thing you think of when you picture jazz. Four or five jive cats trading improvised solos back and forth before going off to get jacked on skag. I’ve always been a Mingus guy so he was my primary window into hard bop. I had never heard of Art Blakey, but I’m damn sure glad I have now. The first four tracks on this thing are amazingly good, and the final two are still fine. This is approximately how I feel about *The Shape of Jazz to Come*, but this one is a much more pleasant listen so I see myself coming back to it more often. What was in the water in 1959 man. I will make a decision on which I prefer by the end of this review.

“Moanin’” sounds remarkably like Miles Davis’ “So What”, but it was apparently recorded first. Both are very good in my opinion. I do enjoy that call-and-response sound, but what really sets “Moanin’” apart is the bluesy piano. I was really impressed by the piano playing all throughout this record, enough that I bothered to memorize that the guy’s name was Bobby Timmons. One we get to the solos, the trumpet is honestly just slick as all hell. It’s not too fast, not too showoffy or virtuosic, just cool and confident and calm and collected. I find the sax solos to be consistently worse than the trumpet solos, but it’s fine here. The piano is of course wonderful. The bass solo goes a bit long but once we get back to the head the piano plays us out smooth. Cool track. I really enjoy “Are You Real” right off the bat, the dueling melodies of the trumpet and the sax are magical. The melody kind of sounds like “All My Loving” by The Beatles. The bass really impressed me on this one, just a well-executed walking bass line. However, it did overdo it on the solo again. The piano is my favorite of the solos, but then at the end there’s this unexpected but really, really smooth section of short solo trades punctuated by little drum interludes. It’s one of the single best moments on the album. I didn’t love “Along Came Betty” at first. It’s a bit slow and sappy for my taste. But it ended up growing on me over time. The trumpet solo is too fast and it clashes with the slow tempo of the rest of the song, but it’s followed by the best sax solo so far. This song is fine, enough to be one of my favorites, but my least favorite yet.

“The Drum Thunder Suite”, on the other hand, is an absolute masterpiece. I’m listening to it on repeat as I write this review. Right from the first cymbal crash this shit sounds amazing. It’s so cool to have a 7 and a half minute composition entirely focused around the drums. And they really do sound like thunder. The drums crackle and rumble with each pounding thud. This drum sound has been built up so perfectly in the preceding tracks. All of the solos are good, and I’m really impressed by just how fast the tempo is. I also find the complete abandonment of the standard bop structure to be refreshing. There’s so much more I could say, but I’ve already said enough on this track. Perhaps my favorite jazz composition so far, up there with Coleman’s “Lonely Woman” and Brubeck’s “Blue Rondo à la Turk”. “Blue March” has a good drum intro, but the rest of the track is a bit underwhelming. Dare I say the drums in the middle section are too choppy for my taste and drag down the whole track? I do dare. The piano is a highlight though. Least favorite track. “Come Rain or Come Shine” is apparently a standard, but I’d never heard it. I’d like this Bobby Timmons character to take a bow because man has the piano been perfect from the top all the way down to the bottom. It’s not my favorite performance from the trumpet, but it is my favorite of the bass solos. The head is admittedly quote pretty, but this track is only okay.

Great album. Some extremely high highs, just riveting and exhilarating when everything works. And the lows are not all that low. Pretty solid work. Maybe a bold choice that I’ll regret, but I’m putting this right above Coleman. My second favorite jazz album overall so far after Matana Roberts.

Favorite tracks: Moanin’, Are You Real, Along Came Betty, The Drum Thunder Suite

Least favorite track: Blues March

# Aidan's review

The run of great jazz albums (and great albums in general) continues. The title track is anchored by an incredibly catchy piano, trumpet and sax call and response that's been stuck in my head all week. Great, robust song, one of the first jazz standards that I've listened to that jumped out as "that's gotta be a standard". Blakey's drumming rarely stands out here, which mildly surprises me due to his status as the band leader, but the album's stars are the piano, trumped and sax working as a trio. The first two tracks are the peak, but what a peak, and the rest of the album manages to keep the momentum going with solid tracks. I could probably listen to this 50 times without getting tired of it, it's just the right combination of enjoyable and unassuming. For most of its run length, it meanders along happily and it's incredibly pleasant to listen alongside. It just puts me in a good mood to listen to this album, one of the best jazz albums we've done so far.
