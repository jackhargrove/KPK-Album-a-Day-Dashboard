# Write-up

Basic Channel is a techno duo formed in Berlin in 1993 consisting of Moritz von Oswald and Mark Ernestus. Before Basic Channel, Ernestus owned and operated the Hard Wax record store in Berlin, while von Oswald worked as a producer for a record label operated out of the Tresor night club. von Oswald is the great-great-grandson of Otto von Bismarck. The duo released a series of singles in 1993 and 1994 before putting out a debut album *BCD* in 1995. Basic Channel helped pioneer dub techno, which incorporated the atmospheric production techniques of dub reggae into techno. Today’s album, *BCD-2*, is a compilation of singles from the early 90s that was released in 2008. Basic Channel still exists and makes music.

Though techno is one of the most well-known major umbrella genres within EDM, *BCD-2* is one of only two techno albums on the list; the other, Aphex Twin’s *Selected Ambient Works 85-92*, belongs to the ambient techno subgenre. Techno began in Detroit in the 1980s as the city’s response to Chicago’s house and New York’s garage house music scenes. While it took some of house’s defining characteristics, particularly the pounding four-on-the-floor kick drum pattern that house distilled from disco, techno’s true sonic predecessor was the drum machine hip hop style of electro. Electro’s cold, robotic rhythms provided the perfect sound for residents of the post-industrial wasteland that was 1980s Detroit. The first wave of Detroit techno began with three DJs who had met as high schoolers: Juan Atkins, Derrick May, and Kevin Saunderson, the “Belleville Three”. They took the EDM format coming out of Chicago, stripped out the warm melodies, and replaced them with pure, repetitive rhythms. Like electro before it, Detroit techno owed much of its sound to the German krautrock scene, particularly Kraftwerk and Tangerine Dream. After house broke EDM into the national mainstream in 1986, Detroit techno took over as EDM’s most prominent subgenre in the late 80s. When acid house, a colder and more aggressive style of house music, kicked off rave culture in the UK in 1989, the stage was set for the even harsher techno style to take off in Europe. Most acid house records were imported into the UK from the US; by contrast, when UK DJs heard Detroit techno, they decided to try and replicate it. They had the spirit, but not the exact technical ability, and ended up creating their own genre known as bleep techno.

Techno quickly spread to continental Europe. It was of particular interest in Germany, where electronic musicians had already been influenced by the robotic sounds of Kraftwerk and the mechanical grooves of EBM (a style of industrial music made for the dancefloor). The aggressive, metallic sounds of Detroit and bleep techno were a perfect fit. While a major techno scene developed in Frankfurt as well, Berlin hosted the heart of Germany’s techno scene. Detroit and Berlin became musical sister cities in the early 90s, especially as many of the original Detroit techno DJs took up residence in Berlin as techno fell out of style in the US. Techno quickly inspired the development of hardcore techno, which bloomed into the new hardcore EDM style that includes gabber and became ubiquitous at European raves. However, it also inspired many new styles that remained within the techno umbrella. One was minimal techno, a style that reacted to the excesses of hardcore rave music by stripping back techno to its barest, rawest elements in a realignment with the original Detroit sound. Basic Channel fell into this category, further combining minimal techno with the cavernous atmospheres of dub reggae to develop dub techno as heard on today’s record. Techno also provided the starting point for IDM because it was Aphex Twin’s style of choice when he came onto the scene with his headier, ambient music. Techno remains one of the most popular and recognizable forms of EDM to this day.

# Hargrove's review

It’s no secret that I’ve struggled to connect with many of the purely electronic albums on this list. Sure, I love Daft Punk, and SOPHIE, and the Avalanches, and the Knife, but these are all electronic musicians that retain a strong connection to the pop and rock tradition. I’ve enjoyed DJ Sprinkles and Justice, but with neither did I feel an intense connection like I do my favorite music. Well, Basic Channel is the first purely EDM record I’ve heard to truly knock my socks off. In my obsessive reading about music genres, I’ve had the idea that I would like techno in theory; the focus on pure rhythm and generally harsher timbres are both right up my alley. But for whatever reason, I’ve never actually sat down and listened to techno. Well, that was a mistake. I should have trusted my instincts. When I’m bored, I often pass the time by tapping out rhythms with my fingers. I designate one finger to keep the beat and then let the other tap out whatever it’s feeling. The tracks on *BCD-2* all give me the same feeling that my improvised finger tapping does. They all set a firm, driving beat with a four-on-the-floor kick drum and sometimes a hi-hat on the off-beats, then they let a series of subtly different synth rhythms weave in and out. These tracks are long, and the rhythmic evolution is glacial; unless I’m paying close attention, it’s hard to pinpoint exactly when each track pivots from one section to the next. But with the rigid foundation of the kick drum, the tracks can unfold at their own pace. I can tune out at one point and then tune back in a minute or two later and have a new rhythm to internalize. In addition, I’m a sucker for aggressive timbres, especially ones of the metallic variety. I would be lying if I said that alone wasn’t a huge plus for me. I love how cold and robotic every second of this record is. I enjoyed listening to this as a headphones album, but I’d really like to see how it feels on a dance floor. For the first time, I’m optimistic that it might give me the same feelings that others describe while dancing. At 80 minutes, this album certainly goes on for a little longer than I’d like, but that’s forgivable for a singles compilation. The only track that I don’t particularly care for is “Phylyps Trak II/II”, which doesn’t instill in me the same feeling of determination as the others. It gets a little too lost in the dub sauce. Overall, though, this was the exact breakthrough I’ve been looking for with EDM.

I love that “Enforcement” starts this album abrasive right off the bat, without even a fade in to prepare for the squelching metallic sound. It feels like the rhythm is made up of little needles poking me in the ear, reminiscent of the microsounds of Ryoji Ikeda. This track exemplifies everything I enjoy about the album, like the one-by-one introduction of the elements, the slow fade in of the four-on-the-floor kick drum beat, and the glacial shifts. “Phylyps Trak” continues the hypnotic, pounding energy with a much more complex rhythm than the last track. I’m a big fan of the effect that makes the rhythms feel like they’re shifting from ear to ear. The inclusion of hi-hat on the offbeats makes this track even busier, and yet it never feels disjointed or even a little bit chaotic. There’s so much going on, but it all combines into this pool of rhythm. There’s also a delay in the synths that I presume comes from dub which fits like a glove. It just adds yet another layer of complexity to the intertwining rhythms. What a fucking banger. “Inversion” changes the pace significantly, starting with arpeggios and a tropical synth melody reminiscent of the trance music I’ve heard. It’s quieter and more low-key than the previous tracks, but I like it almost just as much. The shifting rhythms come to the foreground here, rather than as flourishes alongside the beat. The lead synth also has a swing to it that I enjoy. It goes on a bit too long, but I don’t mind all that much given how easy this is to let play in the background. “Octagon” returns to the sound of the first two tracks. I’m running out of different ways to describe each track individually, but I like how much this one sounds like tapping. The individual sounds are all so short. “Octaedre” has a different vibe than any of the previous tracks. It incorporates a more jarring syncopation, plus the offbeat hi-hats feel more prominent. It ends up making it sound trippier and dubbier than the preceding tracks. It’s just remarkable to me how it still sounds so unified and cohesive with all that’s going on. And I love how much it stands out despite starting nearly an hour into the album. After 68 minutes of fire, there had to be one miss, and that’s “Phylyps Trak II/II”. It has a similar approach to the previous track but it just doesn’t work here. The primary bouncy rhythm sounds silly and that derails everything else. It feels like something that would play at an exercise class for middle-aged women.

Listening to this had a similar effect on me as *Dopesmoker* did in that I felt a strong connection with a style of music I had never before connected with. I don’t like it quite as much as I did *Dopesmoker* (might have to do with the fact that I don’t like electronic as much as I do metal), but this is an easy A Tier for me.

Favorite tracks: Enforcement, Phylyps Trak, Inversion, Octagon, Octaedre

Least favorite track: Phylyps Trak II/II
