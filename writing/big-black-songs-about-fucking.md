# Write-up

Big Black was a punk band from Evanston Illinois. While a trio for most of its history, famed producer Steve Albini was the driving creative force behind the band, which he founded solo as an undergraduate as Northwestern. Accordingly, this write-up is mostly just about Albini.

Albini discovered punk rock via the Ramones while growing up in Missoula, and learned to play bass guitar at age 17 while recovering from being hit by a car. He played in a few local bands before attending Northwestern, where he majored in journalism and became involved in the local punk scene. From early on, two things were clear about Albini. He was a principled asshole, and he was talented. Throughout his career, he would say horrific things about anyone and anything that he did not like. His column in a local zine was titled "Tired of Ugly Fat?" and that was probably the part of the column that made people the least mad, as he trashed most of the Chicago punk scene. He would continue his work as a journalist/provocateur throughout his career.

Big Black started in Albini's dorm room, after he grew frustrated with a perceived lack of talent in the college bands he was playing with. The first EP, "Lungs", featured Albini on every instrument but saxophone, and featured the drum machine that would become one of the several distinctive elements of Big Black's sound. Albini managed to attract Jeff Piazzi and Santiago Durango of local band Naked Raygun to join Big Black as a side project for the next EP. While Piazzi was the lead singer of Naked Raygun, he played bass with Big Black, while Santiago played guitar alongside Albini, who also sang. True to form, Albini tried to name the new EP "Hey N\*\*\*\*\*" but his bandmates thought better of it. The sophomore EP, renamed "Bulldozer" featured a distinct doubled guitar sound achieved via metal guitar picks with clips attached to them. The record brought the band enough buzz to tour the Midwest, then the East Coast, then Europe. When the band found its way back to Chicago, they were a big name among the local punk scene, but Albini's confrontational nature pissed off the owners of the only punk club. Not content with being prevented from playing by outside forces, the band also refused to play for a while because they felt that too many of the people at their shows weren't real fans.

Despite their growing buzz, Big Black refused to sign traditional record deals, or any contract at all. Albini later wrote a very famous piece on how a band makes money. He believed that everything that the labels did was a way to suck energy and money out of young bands, and accordingly the band treated labels with caution, only letting them distribute their records and not taking any advances from them through their career. The band's relentless DIY ethics allowed them to make money from most of their tours, and their lack of a drummer eliminated both the need to pay a drummer and the need to hire a tour van.

I already did a very exhaustive write-up of Big Black for Songs About Fucking, so I'll just supplement it with some minor details. Atomizer is the first full length album that Big Black put out. The opener, Jordan, Minnesota is a reference to a child pornography ring that was uncovered in Jordan. On this album, and all Big Black albums, "Roland" is credited as the drummer. Of course, Big Black did not have a drummer, "Roland" being the brand of drum machine that Albini used for his first releases. The band was so happy with how this album turned out that they put a sticker on their next EP warning it wasn't as good as Atomizer, a move which Sonic Youth ripped off for their own EP, Master=Dik.

As Naked Raygun became to become a big deal in its own right, Piazzi and Durango were forced to chose between the two bands. Piazzi was the lead singer of Naked Raygun, so he chose to leave Big Black, while Durango ditched Naked Raygun. Dave Riley came on to play bass as the band began to work on its first full length album. The resulting record, "Atomizer", was an underground hit despite mixed critical opinion, and has since become regarded as a classic post hardcore album. It is also on this list. However, disputes over their label trying to make too much money off the album by releasing singles from it (and lying to Albini about it), the band left the label and joined Touch And Go Records for what was to be their second and final LP.

The band's lifespan was already not looking great. Albini was straightedge, while Durango drank and Riley drank heavily, causing tensions within the band, especially when Riley's drinking caused him to play poorly and destroy equipment. Albini was also the type to self-destruct when confronted with mainstream success, which the band was now bordering on. When Durango announced his intention to leave the band for law school at the end of 1987, it was a relief to all, and they set to recording "Songs About Fucking". Contrary to the name, not all the songs were about fucking, there were also songs about murder, drugs and trucking. The cover was taken from a hentai manga. The band ended on a high note and "Songs About Fucking" is now a classic album, and a worthy peer to Atomizer.

Post Big Black, Albini started a new trio, Rapeman (apparently named after a manga), and then another trio, Shellac, which lasted until he died in 2024. He is more well known for his production work, which he dabbled in while in Big Black, producing for Naked Raygun, but became prolific after the band ended. After producing the Pixie's "Surfer Rosa" in 1988, he became an in demand name, which lasted for all of 4 years before his disagreements with the label DGC (owned by major label Universal) while producing Nirvana's "In Utero" got him blackballed by most major labels. He was incredibly prolific, producing several thousand albums, and maintaining a firm policy of only taking a fee, never a split. In addition to the aforementioned albums, he also produced Ys by Joanna Newsom, Songs Ohia by Magnolia Electric Co. and Buyer's Market by Peter Soto on this list. He has also produced other records by Godspeed You! Black Emperor, Slint, The Stooges, Neurosis, Jimmy Page, Robert Plant, Jim O’Rouke and Sun O))) who all have records on this list. His style is noted for its "honestly", as he refused to overdub, raise vocals in the mix or compress the music, and pushed to replicate the live sound of the band. He died in 2024 from a heart attack. Durango became a practicing lawyer and represented multiple punk labels. Piazzi led Naked Raygun until it broke up in 92, then led his own band the Bomb. Riley suffered a stroke in 93 which left him a "Worthless Goddamn Cripple" (a title of a piece he wrote on the experience). He died from cancer in 2019.

# Hargrove's review

This is a record that my dad holds near and dear to his heart, and that I’ve been fascinated by since I was a little kid. The cover is just so vulgar when you’re 10. There’s a bad word and a lady in heat! But I never actually listened to it. First I was too young, and then when I started getting into music on my own I really steered clear of the overly harsh punk stuff. I did listen to Big Black’s version of “The Model” for the first time earlier this year and I was quite impressed. I genuinely think that Kraftwerk’s original “Das Model” is a top 20 if not top 10 song of all-time, and though Big Black’s version doesn’t do it better, it certainly does it justice. I also had a general familiarity with Albini as the guy who produced Joanna Newsom’s and Nirvana’s best albums, and also just as a generally important guy in the scene. I broke the news to my dad when he died last year, and I found myself surprised how distraught I was despite never having listened to any of his work as a musician. He just felt important to music as a scene, artistically and spiritually. Now I’ve finally listened to the vulgar album that so intrigued me as a kid. Many times. It’s so short! And I’ve found myself really liking it, more and more with each listen. There is nothing I *hate* more than agreeing with an artist’s take on his own work, but I have to agree with Albini that side 1 of this album is killer killer killer. Side 2 is much spottier, but overall this is a damn good record. The drum programming is phenomenal, it gives a cold, industrial edge that foreshadowed Nine Inch Nails (of whom I’m a huge fan). The bass lines are surprisingly funky. The vocals are wild, and the domination of the instrumental in the mix gives them a dark, creepy quality. And the guitars slice through your skin like a hot knife through butter. It’s unbelievable how close they sound to chainsaws at points. The biggest thing holding this album back is the weak songwriting on side 2. If side 2 were as good as side 1, this would be rank around XTC, Ween, and Weezer for me in high S Tier (my new highest tier is SS Tier). Unfortunately, if “ifs” and “buts” were candies and nuts, we’d all have a merry Christmas. Still, this album is good enough to rank quite highly. Certainly much higher than I thought it would going in.

“The Power of Independent Trucking” is a fiery and loud opener to this album that I thought was kind of insignificant at first but that I grew to really like. I enjoy how the vocals are so low in the mix as to be nearly inaudible, I love how fast the drum programming is, and the guitars feel like they’re flying right into my face. “The Model” is kind of cheating because it’s a cover of a perfect song, but I’m really impressed with how well they pulled it off. The jagged guitars work nearly as well as the peppy synths of the original, especially in the parts between the verses. The instrumental outro is pure sex. My only complaint is that they didn’t do verse 3. I really like the dissonance of the guitars in the opening of “Bad Penny” and the repeated riff is quite strong. I also find the lyrics quite funny; in the spoken word part in the middle, Albini kind of sounds like the “calc stands for calculator” guy and it reminds me that he’s just kind of a dork saying all this shit. This song scorches me from start to finish. “L. Dopa” slaps you in the face with its bass and drum programming straight from the get-go. The drums are so relentless that it’s almost a metal song. I also looked into the story behind the lyrics and it’s genuinely existentially terrifying. Good track. “Precious Thing” has a drum beat that makes it sound like a new wave song, albeit a really fucked up new wave song, and I think that’s funny. The bass line is crazy good too. I’m only so-so on the vocals and guitars, but the rhythm section makes it one of my favorite tracks. “Colombian Necktie” ends side 1 as the least good song, but it’s still good enough to be one of my favorite tracks. It has the energy and intensity of the songs that precede but without the memorability or stickiness. Thankfully, it has enough of the former to outweigh the latter, mostly because the guitar fucks so hard.

“Kitty Empire” starts side 2 as the first real miss, which is unfortunate as I really like it conceptually. Albini including an entire track imagining his pet cat as a king is both funny and endearing. It also has a rumbling, ominous atmosphere that feels oppressively heavy (in a good way). But the issue is that it’s the longest song on the album by a wide margin, and that combined with its slow tempo just grinds the momentum of the album to a halt. I think if they capped it at 3 minutes it would have been great, but it’s just left on a bit too long. “Ergot” though tops it as my least favorite track. The super fast hi-hats are cool, but the song is a chaotic mess. Still, I could overlook that if everything wasn’t so muddled. It dulls the razor-sharp edge that has defined the album up to this point. “Kasimir S. Pulaski Day” is fine but entirely unremarkable. “Fish Fry” starts to pick things back up again. It does the quiet/loud thing pretty well, the guitar is feedbacky as hell, the drum programming is back to breakneck pace, and the vocals are even kind of catchy. Not quite a favorite, but the best on this side so far. “Pavement Saw” is in a similar league, with one caveat: this is the only song where I think the drum programming is overdone. It jsut barely crosses the line into being overbearing and detracting from the rest of the track. “Tiny, King Of the Jews” is by far the best rack on side 2, and is as good as anything on side 1. It randomly sounds very 80s synthpop, and it’s, like, good 80s synthpop. It builds tension amazingly well, feeling like it’s constantly growing into something massive and unwieldy. It’s also got a great melody. One of the best tracks period. “Bombastic Intro” feels like the stellar drum programming is taking a quick bow, and I like that enough to include it on my favorite tracks list as well.

This is just a really cool album. It’s much better than it has any right to be. When things work, they work so well. I’m sure it no longer sounds as transgressive or abrasive as it did back in 1987, but man does it still sound sharp and unique. It’s inconsistent (how could it not be?), but I see myself coming back to it very frequently.

Favorite tracks: The Power of Independent Trucking, The Model, Bad Penny, L. Dopa, Precious Thing, Colombian Necktie, Tiny, King Of the Jews, Bombastic Intro

Least favorite track: Ergot

# Aidan's review

Albini is a fascinating contradiction by modern standards. He simultaneously embodies so much of what I think is cool and so much of what I find detestable. It's so rare to find someone who is both unquestionably talented and this level of an ass. In Albini's case, the two go hand in hand. The same complete disregard for public opinion that made him willing to refuse to change his production for the biggest band on the planet also makes willing to name his band Rapeman. I generally have liked his projects though, with the notable exception of Surfer Rosa, which made his career as a producer. I went into this album skeptical, I don't usually like punk albums, and only had a few songs I really liked off from Shellac, the only band where Albini plays that I’ve listened to.

This album, though, is very good. The drum machine is such a good fit for Albini's guitar style. The best songs on is album are the first three by far. The riff that opens the album is probably the best one on there, but the doubled guitar sound is unique and really good.  I'd heard his cover of Kraftwerk's the Model before, and in the hands of the sardonic Chicagoans, it reeks of misogyny and the youthful drive to get some at any cost. Bad Penny is the most prominent lyric ever get in the album, and deservedly so, the interlude pushes it over the top from something obnoxiously earnest to self aware mockery. This album is just bursting with that sort of personality, and while it still has a lot of the traditional punk anger, Albini is way more of an understandable figure than the overly straight Henry Rollins or the grotesque Darby Crash. He lacks the force of either, but over the course of the album, I can laugh at Albini far more. The lyrics are pretty buried in the mix, and they often deserve to be, but the guitars are tight and crisp enough to make up for it. That's half of the standard Albini production package, the other half being the lack of dumb flourishes. Thankfully there’s also not really anything over the top offensive, despite Albini’s deserved reputation for such. It's tightly packed aggression mixed with just enough humor to make it fun. What else can you ask for from a hardcore record?
