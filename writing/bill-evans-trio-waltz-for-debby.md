# Write-up

Bill Evans was an American jazz pianist born in 1929 in New Jersey. He and his older brother Harry took piano lessons as children, but Harry was always the star. Bill also learned to play violin, flute, and piccolo as he was being trained in classical music. He was introduced to jazz in the early 40s at age 12, which he soon took a liking to. In 1946, he entered Southeastern Louisiana College to study classical flute. He also joined a fraternity and was quarterback of the football team. He eventually switched from flute and received a Bachelors of Music in piano. In 1950, he moved to NYC to start a jazz trio. That quickly failed, and Evans moved to Chicago instead. He briefly toured with Billie Holiday, but was drafted into the army during the Korean War. Like Dave Brubeck, he played jazz in the Army band, where he first composed his most famous work “Waltz for Debby” in 1953. Unlike Dave Brubeck, he started using drugs while in the army, beginning a lifelong struggle with addiction. Evans also struggled with depression and insecurity, often considering himself a naturally poor musician. This drove him to buckle down and practice for a year after leaving the army. In 1955, he moved back to NYC, where he met Thelonious Monk and, most importantly, Miles Davis. Evans released his debut album *New Jazz Conceptions* in 1956 to commercial failure but critical acclaim. It featured his first recorded version of “Waltz for Debby”. Still thinking he wasn’t good enough, Evans intensively studied the works of Bach for the remainder of 1956.

Evans became a collaborator of George Russell, a classical composer who disliked the dominance of the major scale and campaigned on behalf of the Lydian mode. Russell’s work would inspire Miles Davis to experiment with various modes on his seminal 1959 album *Milestones*, establishing the genre of modal jazz. Davis took an interest in Evans’ piano playing and in 1958 asked him to replace Red Garland on piano in Davis’ group now known as the First Great Sextet. This was controversial with audiences, as Evans became the first white member of the group. Davis, however, was inspired by Evans to play a softer, groovier style of jazz. Evans left Davis’ sextet at the end of 1958 after only 8 months due to burnout. He briefly returned in 1959 to play piano for Davis’ album *Kind of Blue*, which to this day is the best-selling jazz album of all time. He even wrote one of the tracks on the album, “Blue in Green”. By the early 60s, Evans had become prolific and well-respected, but his personal life began to deteriorate. He left his long-term girlfriend, became a heavy user of heroin, and developed hepatitis. He recorded two live albums in 1961, including *Waltz for Debby* which would release the next year. It contains the Evans composition “Waltz for Debby”, the Davis composition “Milestones”, the legendary classical composer Leonard Bernstein’s “Some Other Time” from a musical, and three existing jazz standards. He was joined by bassist Scott LaFaro and drummer Paul Motian. It remains his most popular album.

Tragically, the young LaFaro died in a car crash only 10 days after the album was recorded. This crushed Evans, who retreated from music for a time. For the rest of the 60s, he still played on many records, but was less prolific. In the mid-70s, he made two collaborative albums with legendary crooner Tony Bennet, revitalizing his career. In 1979, Evans’ brother Harry committed suicide. This began Evans’ final downward spiral. He recorded one final album and then fell into drugs, this time cocaine. In 1980, he began refusing treatment for his hepatitis, and died not long after at age 51 in what his friends dubbed “the longest suicide in history”. He is remembered today as one of the greatest music theorists in jazz history, introducing many classical techniques to the genre. Many of his compositions have become standards, with “Waltz for Debby” being the most popular of them all.

# Hargrove's review

This week, we get two super pretty albums in a row. This is a jazz album that is very nice to listen to, and one that I would recommend to someone trying to get into jazz. It’s calm, relaxing, playful, melodic, and makes you feel like you’re at some hip New York soiree. Quite literally, in fact, on that last point thanks to the live recording of this album. I love that you can hear the audience after every song, finishing their conversations as the band goes quiet and hurrying into applause. Part of me wants to admonish them for not paying close attention to what will become an album that’s still played 60 years in the future but, to be fair, maybe that’s the way this was meant to be listened to. I played this in the background all day yesterday and with each subsequent listen I liked it more. But when I gave it my final note-taking listen today, I found that the three track run of “Detour Ahead”, “My Romance”, and “Some Other Time” didn’t hold up when I was paying close attention. I didn’t have this problem at all yesterday. And sure, “My Foolish Heart”, “Waltz for Debby”, and “Milestones” are just as good to me when I’m focusing. Maybe those are just the best-written songs; I wouldn’t be terribly surprised given that it includes a Davis composition as well as Evans’ most well-remembered piece. But even when the songs themselves aren’t stellar, I think they still serve a purpose. This album just has great vibes, great atmosphere, great interplay between the instruments. It enhances whatever else you’re doing. It’s far from the best jazz album we’ve listened to, but it knows its role and it executes it well. It also has really fantastic bass playing. I think LaFaro is the second-most talented bassist we’ve heard so far after Mingus.

“My Foolish Heart” is so gorgeous. I’m often a little nonplussed by jazz songs this slow and sparse (as you’ll see later in this review), but something about this one works. I love the way the bass meanders around. I love the melody the piano picks up in the second half. It’s just a joy to listen to. “Waltz for Debby” shows you immediately why it’s earned the reputation that it has. That melody in the head is both sublime and catchy as hell. I particularly love how the bass continues down the expected path even when the piano diverges into a weirder chord progression. The transition from the melody into the more upbeat solo section is also a highlight. “Detour Ahead” loses a lot of momentum, spending a long time in its extended midsection on a bass solo that goes off into the weeds. I was ready to list it as my least favorite when listening but then that melody in the last minute-and-a-half or so kicked in and man does it almost make up for it all. So twinkling and so wistful. Beautiful, beautiful, beautiful. “My Romance” picks up the pace from the previous track, but it doesn’t have anything quite so delicate. It’s largely forgettable overall, but I appreciate its role in the album’s pacing. “Some Other Time” unfortunately combines the worst qualities of the two tracks before it. It’s slow and sparse, but without any particular moments of divine beauty to point to. Thankfully though, “Milestones” closes things out on a very high note. It might be even better than “Waltz for Debby”. The drums just absolutely kill it here. They’ve been pretty nondescript to this point, but the way he rides the cymbal until the cows come home makes this song work. The bass noodles around in the best way, not taking anything away from the focus and progression provided by the drums. The chord changes in the piano are brilliant. And as it fades into that one guy’s laughter, the audience slowly realizes it’s over.

This is the most surprised I’ve been to enjoy a jazz album since Art Blakey (though that one was even better). I foresee myself using this as a go-to when I want background jazz.

Favorite tracks: My Foolish Heart, Waltz for Debby, Milestones

Least favorite track: Some Other Time
