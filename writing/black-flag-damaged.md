# Write-up

If Sonic Youth is the band that did the most to define what it meant culturally to be an American indie band, Black Flag is the band that defined what it mean to be on an American indie label. Black Flag was founded by Greg Ginn and Keith Morris at the ages of 22 and 23 respectively. Morris wanted to played drums but was pressed by Ginn into being the frontman (common theme with Black Flag), and the band was rounded out by Chuck Dukowski on bass and Brian Migndol on drums. Ginn's brother, Raymond Pettibon provided the artwork for the band, including the instantly recognizable logo. Black Flag got their start by marketing. Every underpass in LA was spray painted with the 4 black bars, Pettibon's artwork was posted on telephone poles across the area, and the band became a fixture in the SoCal punk scene. The band was also flat broke. Early stories of the band often mention the physical hunger of the members as a major motivator to play well. The SoCal punk scene was also known for its violent fans and Black Flag was no different, garnering LAPD attention (Ginn thinks that his phones were tapped in the early days). The band's early days were chaotic, Morris left to form the rival Circle Jerks, while Mingol was replaced by the Colombian Robo. The band cycled through vocalists, all of whom had been megafans of the band. Ron Reyes came and left, and Dez Cardana came and switched to guitar. Up until this point, while Ginn was the undisputed leader of the band, Dukowski was the band's face in the press, an unusual setup considering neither was the vocalist (although Ginn wrote the lyrics). This all changed while on tour in DC, when the band met Henry Rollins, who became their longest tenured vocalist. Rollins was a true believer in punk, being good friends with fellow diehard Ian McKaye (of Minor Threat and Fugazi fame). While Ginn remained the power behind the scenes, Rollins became the face of the band. Jokey songs like "TV Party" were out in favor of more melodramatic lyrics. Rollins embraced the violent nature of punk shows, lifting weights in order to be able to endure the abuse of fans who would pelt and punch the band members on stage. Damaged was the band's first full album, and was an underground hit, allowing the band to tour abroad. Among the first hardcore punk records, it came as punk reached its zenith of popularity.

While the business side is often seen as a dirty little secret in music, it's a crucial part of understanding the impact of Black Flag. Early on, the band was unable to find distributors, so they used Ginn's defunct electronics company, Solid State Transitors (SST) as a label. For Damaged, in an effort to achieve wider success, they partnered with major label MCA under the imprint Unicorn Records. While Damaged was critically acclaimed, the band's label at the time, Unicorn Records, refused to release it widely, calling it "anti-parent". In response, the band fully committed to SST, the first real "indie" label in the United States. The Minutemen were the first band to sign to the label, and over time SST became the label of the underground, signing bands that include Dinosaur Jr., Sonic Youth and Husker Du. The label's cultural cache was matched by its allegedly horrific accounting, which eventually drove nearly every other band off the label. Several of these bands ended up founding or finding their own indie labels, creating what would become the indie scene in the United States. Indie rock as a genre became to have a "sound" due to the style that SST deliberately chose, low production, low cost and artistically authentic. Due to the underground becoming big money after Nirvana's breakthrough, being an indie artist no longer meant being on the fringe, as indie bands like Sonic Youth went major label, and indie band were able to find widespread distribution.

After the release of Damaged, Black Flag was sued by Unicorn for breach of contract, preventing them from recording new music. After Unicorn went bust in 1984, they went on to release My War, a stylistic departure from the band that saw the influence of marijuana usage, and marked the beginning of the post-hardcore genre. While controversial at its release, its inventiveness and influence has been recognized with time.  Robo was deported to Colombia while on tour and Rollins is now a resist-lib vegan. The combination of Ginn's controlling presence and the poverty of the band (having never achieved financial success) drove every member away to the point that the band reformed in 2013 without Ginn, spurring a lawsuit from the spurned leader. Black Flag remains the iconic American punk band, and the 4 bars are a popular tattoo or bumper sticker in places where punk rock is still cool (the 40-50 age group in Portland and nowhere else).

# Hargrove's review

This album is aight. Hardcore’s still not really my thing, but I’m learning. “Rise Above” is an all-time classic of any genre that’s going on all of my playlists. “TV Party” is really fun, and is shockingly catchy for a hardcore record. “Gimmie Gimmie Gimmie” I think works really well, Rollins’ inflection on the chorus sounds genuinely deranged, and the stripped-back drum bridge is awesome. I liked the lyrics on “Depression” and the vocal delivery on the chorus. The slow, sludgy guitar sections of “Life of Pain” are fantastic and foreshadow *My War*. The instrumentals generally on this album play a much stronger role than they did on that Germs album, and there are a few songs where the bass is really overpowering in a good way. The guitar is completely atonal in some of the riffs and just never finds a home, which keeps you on edge. The drums are rattling and rolling at frantic pace. Rollins’ vocals are equal parts annoying and invigorating. The album does get pretty tedious by the end, even despite its brevity. I really don’t think hardcore, or at least not this style of hardcore, is well-suited to the album format. To be honest, I liked this just about as much as that Germs album, but I’m gonna rank it slightly above because “Rise Above” is better than any individual track on *(GI)*. It’ll be in my extracurricular roundup for this week, but I’ve listened to *My War*, Black Flag’s next record, a few times this week and I definitely liked it better. This is a quintessential hardcore album though, and a good place to start.

Favorite tracks: Rise Above, TV Party, Gimmie Gimmie Gimmie, Depression, Padded Cell, Life of Pain

Least favorite track: Thirsty and Miserable

# Aidan's review

There’s a good reason that punk fans like live shows, the energy is super important, and while individual songs can convey that energy, full albums rarely can. Black Flag is legendary for originating hardcore punk, but listening to this album, especially the back half, it doesn’t sound legendary at all, it sounds exactly like what it is, a bunch of kids trying to make a rebellious record. It’s authentic, but amateurish, and it’s just not something I particularly want to listen to. Rise Above is great, TV Party is hilarious, but it’s enough to carry the whole album. One of my least favorites as a whole so far, despite liking individual songs, just because of how much of a slog the back half was.
