# Write-up

Black Sabbath was a British rock band formed in Birmingham in 1968 consisting of guitarist Tony Iommi, vocalist Ozzy Osbourne, bassist and lyricist Geezer Butler, and drummer Bill Ward. They are one of the most popular and influential bands of all time, with the entire genre of metal originating from their first few albums. Though Osbourne is much more famous nowadays, guitarist Tony Iommi was the leader of the band, a tradition (guitarist being the leader instead of singer) that would continue in metal. Iommi worked in a factory when he was 17 and, on his last day, an accident resulted in the tips of his right middle and ring fingers getting cut off. He was terrified that he would never be able to play guitar again, but he persevered, and used thimbles to create prosthetic fingertips to play with. This was incredibly painful for him, so he got into the habit of tuning his guitar lower, which loosened the strings and made them easier to hold down. This technique, referred to as “drop tuning”, is responsible for the evil sound of his guitar, and would become a defining aspect of metal. After their band broke up in 1968, Iommi and Ward decided to start a new band. They brought in their friend Butler to play bass, and found Osbourne in an advertisement at their local record store that read “OZZY ZIG Needs Gig”. They cycled through various names, most famously Earth, and Iommi left the band briefly to play for Jethro Tull. In 1969, they settled on the name Black Sabbath, taken from a 1963 horror movie. Horror movies would play an important role in their sound; Butler found it interesting how so many people enjoyed being scared by horror movies, and they decided they wanted to make the musical equivalent. The result was the song “Black Sabbath”, which to this day has the nickname “the most evil song ever written”. Their sound was also based on their ridiculous drug consumption; their early marijuana use and later cocaine allowed them to explore the dark side of drug use in their music.

The group wrote some other songs in the vein of “Black Sabbath” in 1969, and released their debut self-titled album in early 1970 (on Friday the 13th). Like their hard rock contemporaries Led Zeppelin, their music was dismissed and derided by critics; the term “heavy metal” was coined by critics as an insult to make fun of their loud and slow sound. However, they got popular very quickly, with their debut breaking into the top 10 on the British charts. Just four months later, Sabbath returned to the studio to churn out *Paranoid*, also released in 1970. Featuring three of their most famous songs in “War Pigs”, “Iron Man”, and “Paranoid”, *Paranoid* was similarly critically reviled but commercially successful. In 1971, they released *Master of Reality*. Though the critics still didn’t get it, this capped off one of the most beloved three album runs in music history. After a short break, they released *Vol. 4* in 1972. Their critical breakthrough finally came in 1973 with their fifth album *Sabbath Bloody Sabbath*. By the time the critics finally came around, Sabbath were already one of the most popular bands in the world.

Although Sabbath wouldn’t reach their peak popularity until the late 70s, their output post-1973 is more inconsistent. Their drug use, particularly Osbourne’s started to take a toll, and Ozzy was kicked out of the band in 1979. Ward left the following year, and the band continued to put out albums of Iommi and Butler surrounded by various other musicians. They released their final album in 2013, and this summer are doing one final show in England as a send-off. Osbourne had a successful solo career, and became even more famous in the 2000s when a popular reality show focused on his family life. Their influence is still felt in everything from punk to alternative rock, and especially in metal, where their riffs and lyrics provide the touchstone for everything that came after. All four members are still alive.

# Hargrove's review

I actually got really into Sabbath a few months ago, after we started this group. My dad is a huge fan, and I used to sing “N.I.B.” on Rock Band as a kid, and I had of course heard “War Pigs”, “Paranoid”, and “Iron Man” through cultural osmosis, but I had refused to ever actually listen to them out of rebellion. Then, when we were deciding on our first metal album, I sampled this record and thought it was pretty cool. The next week, when I was listening to a lot of extracurricular records, I chose to listen to *Paranoid* and I really fell in love with it. Fantastic, fantastic record. I listened to *Master of Reality* a little later, and it’s great too. I now consider myself an unabashed Sabbath fan when at the beginning of this year I would have just brushed them off. Still, I do think this is the weakest record of their first three. I *really* like “N.I.B.”, and people generally seem to love “Black Sabbath”, but it’s just a little too scattershot and weak in the songwriting department. The highs are very high. The second half of “Black Sabbath” is amazing, the chunky guitar riff during the verses of “The Wizard” is awesome, I love the smoother melodic guitar work on “Behind the Wall of Sleep”, “N.I.B.” demonstrates a fantastic pop instinct underneath it all, and the instrumental breakdown of “Warning” is very well done. At the same time, I think the first section of “Black Sabbath” is a little bare and could use more action (this is a very hot take), the lyrics of “The Wizard” annoy me for whatever reason, as do the lyrics of “Wicked World” (on the American version), “Evil Woman” just doesn’t fit Sabbath’s style at all, and “Warning” just goes on for way too long. A lot of these issues are standard debut record problems, and nearly all of them are absent from their next two records. I am actually very sympathetic to including this record on lists like this just because there are very few records that launched a genre as massive and diverse as heavy metal, but I say that with the caveat that it should only be included after *Paranoid*. Sabbath is certainly worthy of multiple inclusions! One thing that jumps out to me here compared to their other albums is just how good the drumming is. Not that Ward’s drumming is worse on other albums, but it really pops throughout this record. I found myself noticing it in just about every track, even when other things were not to my liking. Young Ozzy sounds awesome here, but I found the slightly off split-pan stereo effect on some of the tracks to be unpleasant. Iommi has great riffs on just about every track, but the ones that stand out to me as having the best guitar work are “Behind the Wall of Sleep” and “N.I.B.”. The bass is good throughout, but one cool thing on this album is the “Bassically” bass solo at the beginning of “N.I.B.”. I kind of wish it were tracked separately, but man does he kill it. Despite this record’s flaws, I’m still putting it in (low) B Tier because as I’m typing this “N.I.B.” just started and man that song fucks.

Favorite tracks: The Wizard, Behind the Wall of Sleep, N.I.B.

Least favorite track: Evil Woman

# Aidan's review

(2009 Remastered Version)

I don't know what I was expecting but it wasn't that. The opener is sick, but I'm left with the impression of it being just a solid classic rock album as opposed to the extreme metal that we've been doing weekly. Didn't love the bluesier elements at times, in particular the harmonica on the Wizard feels really out of place. I was surprised at how strong the prog influences were, and I really enjoyed those, especially on the fifth track (on Spotify). I can understand why people can love this band, they are straightforward and effective, there's no irony and the provocation doesn't feel childish, they just love drugs, rock and roll and the Mephistopheles, Prince of Darkness. While it drags at times, as a whole it's a pretty effective record, and far better than my (low) expectations. I do wish that this was our initial metal track, because it makes the influences a lot clearer than its successors do and when the pure metal aspects come, I really enjoyed them.
