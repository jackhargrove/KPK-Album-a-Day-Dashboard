# Write-up

Boards of Canada is an electronic duo formed in Edinburgh, Scottland in 1986 by brothers Mike Sandison and Marcus Eoin. They are one of the most famous electronic artists associated with IDM (Intelligent Dance Music), a broad label applied by critics in the 90s to cutting-edge and non-dance oriented electronic artists like Aphex Twin, Autechre, and Squarepusher. Sandison and Eoin were born in Scotland in the early 70s but largely raised in Canada after their family moved to Calgary. Both moved back to Scotland as young adults to attend the University of Edinburgh. They formed Boards of Canada as a band in 1986, but the identities of the other early members have been lost to time; by 1989, only the two brothers remained. Given their consistent artistic focus on themes of nostalgia, they fittingly took their name from the National Film Board of Canada, aname they often saw on movies growing up. They self-released a tape titled *Catalog 3* in 1987 and several EPs between 1989-1994 which allegedly still exist but cannot actually be found anywhere. Their first music that any of us can hear is the 1995 EP *Twoism*. They released their debut album *Music Has the Right to Children* in 1998, a record which is still considered by many to be their finest (NOTE: this is the Boards of Canada record I had previously listened to). The sound was heavily inspired by the commercial aesthetics of the 1970s and evoked nostalgia sonically with aging analogue equipment. It received critical acclaim at the time and is retrospectively considered one of the best electronic albums of the 90s.

While recording their next album, four airplanes were hijacked by members of terrorist group al-Qaeda and intentionally crashed into office buildings in New York and DC on September 11th, 2001, killing over 3,000 people. This greatly affected both members of Boards of Canada, altering the direction they chose to take their music. Instead of the calm and dream-like atmosphere of *Music Has the Right to Children*, they put together an album they themselves described as “a record for some sort of trial-by-fire, a claustrophobic, twisting journey that takes you into some pretty dark experiences before you reach the open air again”. It was released in 2002 as *Geogaddi*, a title they claim is a portmanteau of several words but have never revealed which. Its darker take on nostalgic themes made it an early and influential work of hauntology, a style of ambient electronic music that would eventually become very important in the 2010s through its descendants, hypnagogic pop and vaporwave. It received similar acclaim to their debut and there is no consensus among fans or critics today on which is their masterpiece. Boards of Canada have released two albums since, 2005’s *The Campfire Headphase* and 2013’s *Tomorrow’s Harvest*. The duo is still active, but much less prolific; in a very weird coincidence, one of their few commercial releases from the past decade was a 2016 remix of a song by Odd Nosdam, the former producer of the obscure and short-lived hip hop group cLOUDDEAD whose debut album we will listen to tomorrow.

Boards of Canada’s music falls under “chillout music”, an umbrella of EDM styles with lower tempos that originally developed to be played in separate rooms at clubs for patrons who needed a break from dancing. Many IDM-associated styles came out of chillout music, particularly the ambient techno of Aphex Twin. Boards of Canada’s particular genre of chillout music is downtempo, a slow and groove-based genre of ambient electronic music with some influence from hip hop. Downtempo originally developed in Bristol in the late 80s among groups like Massive Attack and Portishead who would go on to establish trip hop. However, neither member of Boards of Canada cites many electronic artists as influences; instead, they point the Beatles, my bloody valentine, and Joni Mitchell, among others.

# Hargrove's review

As I have mentioned many times before, I am an outsider to the world of EDM and its many descendants. In 2017, when I decided I wanted to broaden my horizons, I looked into the EDM-adjacent music that my fellow indieheads at Pitchfork and online enjoyed. They, of course, directed me toward IDM. For whatever reason, I was intimidated by Aphex Twin, so I tried out *Music Has the Right to Children* by Boards of Canada. I stopped after a few tracks because it just wasn’t for me. Come 2020, shortly after COVID sends me home, I was in a better position to branch out my music tastes. I gave that very same album some more spins, and I came to really like it. “An Eagle in Your Mind”, “Telephasic Workshop”, “Sixtyten”, “Turqoise Hexagon Sun”, “Rue the Whirl”, and “Pete Standing Alone” have all entered regular rotation for me over the last 5 years. I’ve certainly played a few of them on our Homecoming/Green Key road trips. But that was it for me. I checked off the IDM box. I could return to safer genres. As we’ve been doing this list, I’ve struggled a bit to get into the albums in the electronic section, but I think I’m getting there. I was excited for *Geogaddi* because I already liked Boards of Canada, so it should be an easy one to get into! Having re-listened to *Music Has the Right to Children* twice now in the last week, I feel more confident than ever to say that album rules. It just has so many memorable bangers. *Geogaddi*, on the other hand, is…only pretty good. Definitely better than many of the other electronic albums we’ve listened to. Early in my first few listens, I realized that it didn’t have the same individual track quality as its predecessor. There are a few that stand out to me, like “Julie and Candy” and “Alpha and Omega”, but even those aren’t as fun as my favorites from the debut. This isn’t necessarily a problem though, especially given the conceptual promises of *Geogaddi*; even beyond the direct statements made by the artists and critics about its dark tone, the album makes it clear from its structure, sample choices, and even length (66 minutes and 6 seconds) that it’s supposed to be a harrowing journey. As a devoted album listener, this certainly piqued my interest. But in every listen, whether I gave it all or none of my attention, it just never sucked me in. I’m just not that creeped out by the music, and it never establishes enough of a flow for me to feel like I’m spelunking ever deeper into an evil cave. Maybe part of the issue is that we listened to this right after *Hounds of Love*, where *The Ninth Wave* on side 2 actually did suck me in and terrify me in exactly the way *Geogaddi* was supposed to. Maybe it was too long, often losing me by the much weaker second half. Overall, though, this was just a pretty good ambient hip hop album with some standout tracks. Which, to be clear, is not a bad thing to be! But I’m taking *Music Has the Right to Children* ten times out of ten.

My track-by-track review will focus on the longer songs instead of the interludes. I appreciated the existence of the interludes, they do genuinely give good flavor and atmosphere to the album, but there’s no point in me appraising them all separately. My favorites were “Beware the Friendly Stranger” for being one of the only moments on the album to actually scare me, and “Corsair” because I enjoyed the pulsating drone textures. The first of the longer tracks is “Music Is Math”, which I thought was quite good. The skitteriness of the drum beat reminds me a bit of Burial. The best part is the eerie, organ-esque wall of sound in the background. I also appreciated the expressiveness of the vocal sample, and the way the track devolves and disintegrates in the final third. “Gyroscope” has a cool rolling drum beat that sounds especially good at high volumes, but the rest of it leaves a lot to be desired. It’s too static for my tastes. “Sunshine Recorder” reminds me a lot of the style of track on their debut album. I don’t think it would be particularly standout there, but on this album it brings something unique and I like that. “Julie and Candy” is my favorite track from all of *Geogaddi*. It lumbers forward with such a trip hop swagger that if you told me it was a Massive Attack cut I might have believed you. It packs a real punch, with serious power to its slow growth across its run time. I’m a bit mixed on “1969” but I’d say I liked it overall. Because of the vocal sample’s resemblance to verses, it’s the track that feels most like a pop song (in a good way). However, it’s also quite repetitive. I like it more than I dislike it. “The Beach at Redpoint”, on the other hand, is quite good. It’s one of the most frantic and energetic songs on the album and, coming right in the middle, it’s a good point for that. “Alpha and Omega” starts hazy and washed out but around the 50 second mark it fades into an unprecedented directness. That synth melody carries the track, but the errant flutes are also a highlight. It has a background synth that mimics the kinds of string arrangements I adore and an uptempo beat. Easily one of my favorite tracks. “The Devil in the Details” is kind of a mess. It’s at this point that *Geogaddi* begins to lose whatever groove it did have. The interludes become more frequent and break the established pattern, while the longer tracks become less focused. “Dawn Chorus” tries to bring things back with one of the most recognizably hip hop drum beats, but I’m just not crazy about the music on top of it. “You Could Feel the Sky” ends things on a sour note as my least favorite track. There’s something interesting about the industrial feel to the looping of that rope sample, but man is there not much more to it for 5 minutes than that. Thankfully for the album’s structure, the “Corsair” interlude (outro?) still remains to right the ship somewhat.

I don’t want to sound too negative here, this was still one of the better records from the electronic section for me. There are definitely a few tracks that will get added to my playlists and rotation with all of the ones from *Music Has the Right to Children*. But it’s definitely a flawed album, exacerbated by the fact that these guys have an album with a similar sound but that is just better in every way. Also, purely atmospherically, Tim Hecker’s *Ravedeath, 1972* and *Virgins* create that scary apocalyptic atmosphere with ambient music much more effectively. For what it’s worth, I would comfortably put *Music Has the Right to Children* in A Tier as of right now if it were on the list.

Favorite tracks: Music Is Math, Beware the Friendly Stranger, Sunshine Recorder, Julie and Candy, 1969, The Beach at Redpoint, Alpha and Omega, Corsair

Least favorite track: You Could Feel the Sky
