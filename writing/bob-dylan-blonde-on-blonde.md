# Write-up

In 1961, a 20 year old Robert Zimmerman dropped out of college and hopped on a bus from his native Minnesota to New York City. He wanted to meet his idol, Woody Guthrie, who was being treated for Huntingtons at Greystone Hospital. Soon Robert Zimmerman became Bob Dylan (after Dylan Thomas) and he had released his first eponymous album, which featured largely covers. It was not a success, selling only 5,000 copies. For his second album, he managed to sign with Albert Grossman, who had already made folk superstars out of Peter, Paul and Mary. Grossman persuaded Dylan to change producers for the second album to Tom Wilson, who had previously mostly produced Jazz. Dylan's second album, "The Freewheelin' Bob Dylan", featured mostly original songs, and several protest songs that found their audience in the increasingly political youth. Among his best known songs from the album are "Blownin' in the Wind", "A Hard Rain's a-Gonna Fall" and "Don't Think Twice, It's Alright". Dylan's appeal as a political artist grew even more when he walked out of The Ed Sullivan Show, the biggest gig in music at the time, after he was forbidden from playing "Talkin' John Birch Paranoid Blue", which was incredibly mocking of the anti communist organization. His third album, "The Times, They Are a-Changin'" continued with his political development, featuring some of his most explicit protest songs such as "Only A Pawn in Their Game", "The Loansome Death of Hattie Carroll" and the title track. It sold well but did not reach the same heights as his sophomore album.

Starting in 1963, Dylan began to tire of his status as a figurehead for protest. The song "It Ain't Me Babe" on his fourth album, "Another Side of Bob Dylan" is often interpreted as a farewell to the protest songs that contributed to his initial fame. He soon began to ditch his folksy looks for an all black look and sunglasses outdoors and indoors. His next album, "Bringing it All Back Home", saw the folk singer embrace electric instruments on its first side. While he was still not writing protest songs, the opening song, "Subterranean Homesick Blues" notably gave a name to the leftist terror group the Weather Underground. ("You don't need a weatherman to know which way the wind blows") He also foreran an internet trend from the 2010s by filming a music video in which he slowly dropped cue cards with the lyrics on them. The album's back half featured a more folk sound and contained the two hits "Mr. Tamborine Man" (more famous for the Byrds' cover) and "It's All Over Now, Baby Blue". After the alubm was released, he "went electric" by playing electric instruments at the Newport Folk Festival in 1965, an event which formed the center of the recent (very good) film, "A Complete Unknown".

Dylan followed the controversy with two of his best albums, "Highway 61 Revisited", which features "Like a Rollin' Stone" and is on our list, as well as today's album, "Blonde on Blonde". The backing band which Dylan formed for touring "Highway 61 Revisited" was called the Hawks at the time, but later changed its name to The Band and had their own storied career.

"Blonde on Blonde" began in New York with the Hawks, but only one song from those sessions, "One of Us Must Know (Sooner or Later)" made it on to the resulting album. Dissatisfied, Dylan took up sticks for Nashville. He ditched the Hawks for Nashville's experienced local session musicians, and was enormously productive, completing the entire album in two (possibly one) recording session. Dylan even produced enough material to create one of the first totally original double albums. The album was a hit upon release, with "Visions of Johanna" and "Rainy Day Woman #12 & 35" standing out as among his best songs ever. Its acclaim has grown even more over years to the point that it's a strong contender for "Best Dylan album".

Having reached such dizzying heights, there was nowhere else for Dylan to go but down in the early 70s. He married a model, began heavily using amphetamines, among other things and crashed his motorcycle badly enough to break his back, preventing him from touring the next 8 years. Album #8, “John Wesley Harding” while good, is mostly notable for having “All Along the Watchtower”, which Hendrix would cover. His next album, "Nashville Skyline", was highly influential on what would become country rock and features a duet with Johnny Cash on "Girl from the North Country". Dylan's output began to grow shaky, following the poorly recieved "Self Portrait" with the well liked "New Morning". He wrote songs with George Harrison, published his own book of poetry and tried his hand at acting with "Pat Garrett and Billy the Kid". During this time period, he returned to his political roots a bit, wiring a song for murdered Black Panther George Jackson and writing a letter protesting the deportation of John Lennon and Yoko Ono for weed possession.

Next for Dylan was the release of several live albums as he switched labels from Columbia to Geffen and back again while releasing the completely fine "Planet Waves". He still had one last great album left in him, but it took a nasty divorce from a marriage that had now reached 12 years in length to draw it out of him. The resulting album, "Blood on the Tracks", was not well received at the time, but has been reevaluated as among his best and is my personal favorite.

Dylan's career went a bit off the rails after "Blood on the Tracks", as he converted to Christianity and released gospel albums before turning back to Judaism. He managed to put out one more good album, "Time Out of Mind" in 93, and has managed to bring his total of studio albums to 40, releasing his most recent in 2023. He formed the Traveling Wilburys with fellow legends George Harrison, Jeff Lynne, Roy Orbison and Tom Petty, releasing two albums together. He also began the "Never Ending Tour", playing 100+ shows a year since 1988, and is still doing it at the age of 84. In 2016, the Nobel Committee gave him the only prize for Literature to a songwriter, adding to his collection of basically every single prize that a musician can win.

# Hargrove's review

The first Dylan album I ever listened to, and it will probably always be my favorite. I had a huge Bob Dylan phase my freshman fall, just before COVID. “Visions of Johanna” is a masterpiece in every aspect of its composition; the melody, the lyrics, the vocal performance, the wailing harmonica, everything about it is perfection. One of my favorite karaoke songs too, I’m very proud of my Dylan impression and he sounds very Dylan-y here. Speaking of, “Trapped Inside of Mobile with the Memphis Blues Again” might just be the quintessential Dylan song. The lyrics are positively inscrutable but the words sound so good, his vocal, uh, “quirks” are on full display, the hook is catchier than anything this artsy has any right to be, and that folk/blues/rock combo is clanging. Another of my favorites. “Sad Eyed Lady of the Lowlands” is my favorite of Dylan’s long songs and just one of the most beautiful love songs I’ve ever heard. I hope one day I’ll meet someone who inspires me to create so many colorful metaphors. “Rainy Day Women #12 and 35”, is such a badass choice for an opener. The band sounds INSANE, I had no idea what I was listening to when I put it on for the first time. Out of time, out of tune, and so loud. Dylan can’t stop himself from cracking up as he delivers the most thinly-veiled drug references ever crafted. Such a banger. “Just Like a Woman” is both aching and dripping with spite. One thing I’ve always loved (and related to) about Dylan is how spiteful, petty, and vindictive he can be. Here, he thoroughly picks apart the psyche ofan ex with one of the sweetest melodies I’ve ever heard as the hook. Speaking of spite, “Fourth Time Around” takes down two birds with one stone: Edie Sedgwick and John Lennon. He’s such a dick, but the way he insults you with beautiful hand-picked guitars and a more sophisticated version of your song (“Norwegian Wood”, in this case) is just brutal. My personal choice for best spiteful Dylan song is the non-album single “Positively 4th Street”, but there’s lots of venom on *Blonde on Blonde* as well. “I Want You” is great if only for its gorgeous chorus melody. Beyond the big songs, there are some great deep cuts here as well. I was surprised to see how few streams “Pledging My Time” has, its one of my favorite blues rock tracks. “Leopard-Skin Pill-Box Hat” abandons the innovations of the 50s for a pure blues sound, and has in my opinion one of the funniest lines of all time: “You might think he loves you for your money/But I know what he really loves you for/Its your brand new leopard-skin pill-box hat”. I love the chord changes on the lead in to the bridges in “Absolutely Sweet Marie”, and the soaring vocals over the piano on “One of Us Must Know (Sooner or Later)”. The big flaw in this album is the bloat; this has the classic double album problem of “we made 3 good sides of material, but for economic reasons we either have to cut one side or make another”. Side 3 really suffers, with “Most Likely You Go Your Way (And I’ll Go Mine”, “Temporary Like Achilles”, and “Obviously Five Believers” being kind of duds. Of these, the first kind of annoys me, the second is paced far too slowly, and the third is utterly forgettable. Still, I’m willing to forgive these flaws. The highs are not only incredibly high, but also far more numerous than the lows, which are themselves not even that low. This album is Dylan at his most Dylan, angry, horny, brash, tender, unmistakable, and impenetrable. He’s just so *cool*. The coolest guy who ever did it. I mean look at that cover photo! Insane drip! And why is his head shaped like that? No one’s head is shaped like that!

Favorite tracks: Rainy Day Women #12 and 35, Pledging My Time, Visions of Johanna, I Want You, Stuck Inside of Mobile with the Memphis Blues Again, Leopard-Skin Pill-Box Hat, Just Like a Woman, Fourth Time Around, Sad-Eyed Lady of the Lowlands

Least favorite track: Temporary Like Achilles

# Aidan's review

Wasn't my favorite Dylan going in and still isn't. But there's a lot of room to be good below the masterpiece that is "Blood on the Tracks" and this is nipping at its heels. I know "Rainy Day Women" is considered an all timer, but I feel that it's more of a joke than a serious tune, especially after the first 4 times we all must get stoned. Not a big fan of "Pledging My Time" either, but after that it's hit after hit. Visions of Johanna is great, "I Want You" is even better and "Stuck Inside of Mobile with the Memphis Blues Again" is my favorite song on the album. "Just Like a Woman" is quintessential Dylan, a love song that is tinged with just enough disrespect to make it like no other. The later half of the album is good, but forgettable, although "Sad Eyed Lady" is a fitting closer. Generally I think the instrumentation is occasionally a bit too bluesy to work with the harshness of Dylan's voice, but at the same time, it's probably his best abstract songwriting on display here. Dylan has a good sense for what his really good songs are, and while he absolutely could have made "Stuck Inside..." 2 minutes shorter, I'm as happy listening to the 5th chorus as the first. This is still the only Dylan album that I like where I'd still complain a bit about his infamously grating voice, but the highs are so damn high that I'd still call it a great, not good album. Looking forward to Highway 61 Revisited.
