# Write-up

We've already covered the broad strokes of Dylan's career when we did Blonde on Blonde, so this write-up will focus on the specifics of Blood on the Tracks and Dylan's career to this day. Blood on the Tracks came after a period of middling releases from Dylan that began with Self Portrait. He also published a book of poetry and starred in a movie about Billy the Kid, neither of which was very good. He briefly left Columbia records for Geffen before going back to them. And his marriage with his wife Sara was beginning to struggle. Dylan blamed his painting class which he believed made him see time differently. Others have blamed him leaving on tour for 6 months for the first time since he began seeing Sara. Either way, the two began living apart and Dylan channelled his frustration into songwriting, filling notebooks with songs and ideas. The result was 17 songs which eventually were cut down to the 10 that appear on the album. Dylan showed them to a superstar group of friends including Crosby, Stills and Nash. They didn't like them. The album itself was recorded first in New York, and then when Dylan felt that the sound was too similar to his earlier non-electric work, rerecorded 5 of the songs in Minnesota with his brother David Zimmerman.

To this day, Dylan denies that the album is autobiographical, instead claiming it is inspired by the writings of Anton Chekhov, something that has been disputed by his son Jakob and basically every chronicler of Dylan himself. The album reviewed mixed reviews on release but since has risen in estimation to be ranked among his best. After Blood on the Tracks, Dylan's career began to swerve back and forth. He converted to Christianity and recorded three gospel albums, refusing to play his earlier work at concerts to the chagrin of fans, including John Lennon, who wrote a song critiquing Dylan shortly before he was assassinated by Mark David Chapman. Most of his well liked songs from this period were left off of his albums, further hurting his contemporary approval. He returned to more normal music with Infidels, and inspired Willy Nelson to organize Farm Aid. He featured on a Kurtis Blow album, and fell into such a career downturn that he was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1988 while releasing an album that did't break the top 50 and a Grateful Dead collab that ranks among the worst output from either him or the Dead.

The 90s saw him return to more folk music, and his continued lack of great releases was chalked up by some to alcoholism. It took a bout of pericarditis before he would release another good album, Time Out of Mind, which was seen as a huge comeback for him. He then followed it up by rerecording his gospel period, writing a memoir full of lies and hosting a radio program in the early 2000s. He continued putting out good music though, with "Love and Theft" and  Modern Times forming a trilogy with Time Out of Mind and the next album, Together From Life being well received as well. He did a Christmas album for some reason, recorded ads for brands from Cadillac to Victoria's Secret, released his own whiskey brand and did an album of Sinatra covers. In 2016 he won the Nobel Prize and in 2020 he sold his catalog for an estimated 300-400 million dollars. He has been touring on the "Never Ending Tour" since 1988, with roughly 100 shows a year.

# Hargrove's review

I had already listened to the other two Dylans on the list, but *Blood on the Tracks* is the one I’ve been meaning to get around to forever but just didn’t. Something about taking Dylan out of the 60s scares me a bit on a visceral level, like when I realize that Pablo Picasso died after both of my parents were born. Well, it turns out I’m a very dumb bitch! This album kicks as much ass as anything Dylan did in the 60s! “Tangled Up in Blue”, “Idiot Wind”, and “Lily, Rosemary and the Jack of Hearts” stack up to “Like a Rolling Stone” and “Visions of Johanna” and “Subterranean Homesick Blues”. “Simple Twist of Fate”, “You’re Gonna Make Me Lonesome When You Go”, and “If You See Her, Say Hello” are as tender as “I Want You”, as heartbreaking as “Just Like a Woman”. I feel like most comeback albums feature the artist adapting to new times or shaking up their sound, but here Dylan hits all the same notes he used to, and damn do they sound good. My favorite Dylan has always been angry, spiteful Dylan (number 1 fan of “Positively 4th Street” over here), so thank God divorce inspires a lot of those same feelings. At the same time, I was super impressed by how mature and holistic some of these songs were. Obviously “Idiot Wind” leaves its mark through its combination of bile and wisdom, but the charity Dylan gives his ex-wife in “Shelter from the Storm” and “If You See Her, Say Hello” is just plain admirable. It reminds me a lot of Fiona Apple’s “Werewolf”, where every line describes how she and her ex are both good people who sadly didn’t mix well together. Dylan’s wordplay is as good as it ever was, and his storytelling is, dare I say, even better. Instrumentally, I generally prefer the five re-recorded tracks for their depth and their fullness, but there’s a lot of stark beauty in the simplicity of the acoustic tracks as well. This might be the perfect album to recommend to someone who is Dylan-curious but just can’t get into him. That being said, I still prefer *Blonde on Blonde*; yes, it’s a sprawling mess of a double album, but it’s *my* sprawling mess of a double album, and it’ll always have some of my favorite tracks.

How had I never heard the BANGER that is “Tangled Up in Blue”??? Unconscionable. The endless catchiness of the “tangled up in bluuuuuuuuuue” refrain alone makes it one of the most fun songs in his catalogue. I find myself enraptured by Dylan’s storytelling every time I listen, with the fourth verse concerning the topless bar being my favorite. It’s like an entire verse of facial imagery as evocative as “The ghosts of electricity howled in the bones of her face” from “Visions of Johanna”. I’m also partial to And every one of them words rang true/And glowed like burning coal/Pouring off of every page/Like it was written in my soul” from verse 5. “Simple Twist of Fate” is not a banger in the same way, but it’s one of the best acoustic tracks here. Dylan delivers the penultimate bar of each verse with such heartbreaking passion. I can’t stop thinking about “Felt an emptiness inside/To which he just could not relate”. How can you not relate to something you’re currently feeling? And as someone still caught up on a girl from high school, “People tell me it's a sin/To know and feel too much within/I still believe she was my twin/But I lost the ring” hits hard. “You’re a Big Girl Now” isn’t bad, but it’s also not one of my favorites. I like Dylan’s vocal performance and the delicate guitar, but it just doesn’t resonate with me. “Idiot Wind”, on the other hand, is my favorite on the album. What a banger of a banger of a banger of a banger of a banger. It introduces the organ perfectly, blowing like a cold wind over the whole song. Dylan howls out every utterance of “idiot” with terrifying venom. My favorite spiteful lines include Even you, yesterday/You had to ask me where it was at/I couldn’t believe after all these years/You didn’t know me any better than that/Sweet lady” and “I noticed at the ceremony/Your corrupt ways had finally made you blind/I can’t remember your face anymore/Your mouth has changed, your eyes don’t look into mine”. And all of this comes before the absolute masterstroke, the sudden empathy at the end of verse 8 with “And I’ll never know the same about you/Your holiness or your kind of love/And it makes me feel so sorry” and the switching from “you” to “we” in the final chorus. This twist floored me my first listen, and it just keeps getting better each time. “You’re Gonna Make Me Lonesome When You Go” brings some nice breeziness after the intensity of “Idiot Wind”. It’s a bit simple, but I adore the nature imagery. It’s an idyllic spot on such a bleak album.

The straightforward blues of “Meet Me in the Morning” gets me going, but it’s disappointing lyrically. It’s overall unremarkable outside of Dylan’s delivery of “Well, you know I even outran the hound dogs”. On “Lily, Rosemary and the Jack of Hearts”, Dylan tells a story in 15 verses, all with the same melody and over the same instrumental. And yet, I’m so entranced by the storytelling that it works. It’s hypnotic. I’m a little biased because I’m so into poker right now, but he pulls off the poker imagery throughout and particularly in verse 3. Dylan crafts one of his most tragic characters in Rosemary, with “Rosemary started drinking hard and seeing her reflection in the knife/She was tired of the attention, tired of playing the role of Big Jim's wife/She had done a lot of bad things, even once tried suicide/Was looking to do just one good deed before she died/She was gazing to the future, riding on the Jack of Hearts” tugging at my heart strings every time I hear it. The organ reminds me of the three chord organ line in the Velvet Underground’s “What Goes On”. I like the polka bassline quite a bit as well. Just a great track. “If You See Her, Say Hello” continues the mature breakup song theme from earlier in the record. “Always have respected her for doing what she did and gettin' free” is a beautiful sentiment. I also love the quasi-Spanish tremolo guitar near the end. “Shelter from the Storm” kicks the maturity up a notch and becomes gratitude, a thoroughly refreshing emotion. Few verses in music are so beautifully formed as “'Twas in another lifetime, one of toil and blood/When blackness was a virtue, the road was full of mud/I came in from the wilderness, a creature void of form/‘Come in,’ she said, ‘I’ll give ya shelter from the storm’”. I also think “And the one-eyed undertaker, he blows a futile horn” is such a hilariously Dylan line. It’s yet another good track, with the structure in particular standing out. “Buckets of Rain” is really pretty but I’m a bit nonplussed by the lyrics, with the exception of “Everything about you is bringing me misery” at the end of verse 3. That’s a banger, but otherwise it’s a letdown closer.

I love you, Bob. It’s gonna hurt when you die.

Favorite tracks: Tangled Up in Blue, Simple Twist of Fate, Idiot Wind, You’re Gonna Make Me Lonesome When You Go, Lily, Rosemary and the Jack of Hearts, If You See Her, Say Hello, Shelter from the Storm

Least favorite track: Meet Me in the Morning

# Aidan's review

An all time favorite and my pick for the best Dylan album. He had already got at the same sort of mood towards a breakup where he manages to somehow be both mocking yet forgiving in the amazing"Don't Think Twice It's Alright" , but here he managed to put together an entire album just as good. My personal favorite is "Lily, Rosemary and the Jack of Hearts" which perfectly balances abstruse symbolism with a delightful story. If you want to read it, there's pages of speculation of mixed quality regarding the real story behind the song. I don't think it's that deep, but I always love a Dylan song with the organ and harmonica, "I Want You" accomplished the same thing on Blonde on Blonde. Dylan's lyricism is at full display on tracks like "Tangled Up in Blue", "Idiot Wind" and "Shelter from the Storm", and the only flaw I find in this album is "Meet Me in the Morning" where Dylan persists in trying to pull of the blues. I still think that Dylan's Nobel is a populist farce, but when I'm listening to this album, I'm not mad about it. Dylan pulls off a mix of lovesickness, bitterness and irony in a way I'm not sure any other singer could.
