# Write-up

Our last Dylan album is our first chronologically. At the beginning of his career, Dylan was seen as a "voice of a generation". His second album, The Freewheelin' Bob Dylan, was mostly composed of protest songs like "Blowin' in the Wind" and "Masters of War" with a few apolitical songs like the kiss-off "Don't Think Twice, It's All Right". He continued that reputation by walking out of the Ed Sullivan show when they wouldn't let him perform the "Talkin' John Birch Paranoid Blues" and writing even more explicitly political songs on his third album "The Times They Are A-Changin'", including the title track and “The Lonesome Death of Hattie Carroll”. Another song on the album, “Only a Pawn in Their Game”, addressed the assassination of Medgar Evers, a civil rights activist and claimed that the murderer was less responsible than the broader institution of racism. But Dylan was growing tired of his reputation as a "voice of a generation" His fourth album, "Another Side of Bob Dylan" showed a lighter side, including the song "It Ain't Me Babe" which has often been interpreted as a farewell to his role as a political spokesperson for the social reformers of the time.

As he moved away from folk and protest music, Dylan grew interested in the growing rock genre. Across the pond, the Beatles were on their third album, and Dylan began to dress the style of rock stars. He wore sunglasses during the day, dressed in mostly black and borrowed the habit of wearing black heeled ankle boots from the Beatles. His next album, Bringing It All Back Home, saw him foray into electric instrumentation, and the tour was recorded in the famous documentary, "Don't Look Back". At the Newport Folk Festival, Dylan played electric instrumentation live drawing boos from the crowd. The scene was depicted in "A Complete Unknown" and was filmed a mile from my house. The folk establishment was in uproar, and Dylan was seen as a traitor and a sell-out.

Two months before performing at Newport, Dylan was exhausted from touring and unhappy with his output. He scrawled some twenty pages of lyrics and finally cut it down to a mere four verses and chorus that would become "Like A Rollin' Stone". He brought in a group of backing musicians and began recording. While they were recording, a 21 year old named Al Kooper was invited to watch by the producer Tom Wilson. Kooper, having never played an electric organ before, managed to convince Dylan to let him play and improvised the riff that formed the core of "Like a Rollin' Stone", replacing a previous version that was piano-based. Kooper became a member of the sessions on the spot, and collaborated heavily with Dylan on the chords for the rest of the album. The final song to be recorded was "Desolation Row", the only acoustic song on the album.

The record was an immediate success. For the tour, Dylan found a group of 5 musicians, Rick Danko, Garth Hudson, Richard Manuel, Robbie Robertson and Levon Helm who were playing for the rockabilly singer Ronnie Hawkins as the Hawks. The five liked each other so much that they would eventually have a career as their own band, The Band. "Like A Rollin' Stone" reached the second position in the charts and is among Dylan's best known songs. It is cited along with Pet Sounds as beginning the album era where the album was the focus of musicians rather than the single. It's considered among the greatest records of all time, Dylan's first "rock" album and possibly his best.

# Hargrove's review

It’s funny how unreliable memory is because I was pretty certain that *Blonde on Blonde* was my first Dylan album, but my Spotify data clearly shows that it was actually *Highway 61 Revisited*. Which does make sense, as “Like a Rolling Stone” was one of my entry points into Dylan. In the years since, *Blonde on Blonde* has become my favorite Dylan album because of its self-indulgence, because of the way it sums up his most productive period, because of how unabashedly Dylan it is (seriously, all the best Dylan impression lines are from *Blonde on Blonde* songs). But man, listening to *Highway 61 Revisited*, you do recognize the value of slightly tighter presentation. In just nine tracks, Dylan packs in three of his greatest, sequenced perfectly as the first track, the middle track, and the closer. On top of that, bangers like “Tombstone Blues”, “Queen Jane Approximately”, and “Just Like Tom Thumb’s Blues” stand up with any of Dylan’s album cuts from other albums. Like *Blonde on Blonde*, Dylan can’t hide his spite one iota on any of these songs, but he keeps it a little less overtly personal here. You won’t find something so obviously about a real person as “4th Time Around” or “Sad-Eyed Lady of the Lowlands” on *Highway 61 Revisited*. Instead, he sticks to opaque extended metaphor, peppering his songs with characters and references. As such, this album feels like kind of the opposite pole to *Blood on the Tracks*, leaving *Blonde on Blonde* as the perfect middle ground between the two. But, man, what a great trio of albums. *Bringing It All Back Home* is on a similar level as well in my opinion. Gotta love Bob Dylan.

Let me to be the first to say “Like a Rolling Stone” is a very good song. Perhaps even one of the greatest songs ever. That organ line is just unbelievable. What was Al Kooper smoking? And of course, the chorus is one of Dylan’s catchiest. There’s nothing more cathartic than screaming “How does it feel?” along with him. As for the writing, I’m always impressed by the sheer number of rhymes in “Once upon a time you dressed so fine/Threw the bums a dime in your prime, didn't you?/People call, say ‘Beware, doll, you're bound to fall’/You thought they were all a-kiddin' you”. I’m always quite disturbed by the line “As you stare into the vacuum of his eyes/And say, ‘Do you want to make a deal?’”, how sleazy and depraved it sounds. And then there’s the freedom in “When ya ain't got nothin', you got nothin' to lose/You're invisible now, ya got no secrets to conceal”. After spending 3.9 verses hating, he spends 0.1 giving advice. Wholesome. “Tombstone Blues” is a real fun rocker. It drives with unbelievable momentum and swinging fun, from the raucous guitar work to the cool rhythm in Dylan’s verses. I always found the line “The sun’s not yellow, it’s chicken” funny when I was younger. The album slows down a bit with “It Takes a Lot to Laugh, It Takes a Train to Cry”. It’s nice to have a bot of a slower and more tender track, but it’s really not as good as the first two. The organ remains a highlight, as does the harmonica. And “Well, I want to be your lover, baby/I don’t want to be your boss” is one of the album’s best quotables. But this song is only alright. The album hits bottom with “From a Buick 6”, another one that’s kind of a snoozer. It’s really not bad, but I think *Highway 61 Revisited* does have more *Blonde on Blonde* side 3 style meh tracks than people want to admit. Thankfully, “Ballad of a Thin Man” ends side 1 masterfully. It is absolutely one of Dylan’s finest. The jangly blues-iness of the piano reminds me of like 20s Vaudeville blues stuff. There’s something very theatrical about this track. The level of spite in Dylan’s voice matches my other favorite songs of his, and of course there’s a catchy chorus. My favorite lines include “And says, "How does it feel to/Be such a freak?”/And you say, ‘Impossible’/As he hands you a bone“, “You've been through all of/F. Scott Fitzgerald's books/You are very well read/It's well known”, and “There oughta be a law/Against you comin' around/You should be made/To wear earphones”.

“Queen Jane Approximately” feels like it runs the risk of being a filler track, but honestly it’s a really nice reprieve to start side 2 after the intensity of “Ballad of a Thin Man”. It’s got a lighter, more laidback vibe that makes its melancholy much prettier. I like it. I’m a little torn on “Highway 61 Revisited” because it really is super fun. It’s just driving, bouncy blues with funny lyrics that infect you with the way Dylan says “highway 61” as a new vocal stim. But FUCK is that police whistle stupid and annoying. It’s kind of hilarious to me now, but it still provokes a physical reaction. “Just Like Tom Thumb’s Blues” has a similar pace to “It Takes a Lot to Laugh, It Takes a Train to Cry” but I like it better than that one. It’s just a really pleasant listen. “I started out on Burgundy/But soon hit the harder stuff” is a great line too. The album closes with one of Dylan’s most undeniably impressive tracks, “Desolation Row”. I don’t have a ton I can add here. I like the relentless Spanish-inflected guitar work. It feels almost industrial in the way it drives the song, fitting the Cannery Row vibe. My favorite verse is verse 2 about Cinderella. My favorite line include Cinderella, she seems so easy/‘It takes one to know one,’ she smiles/And puts her hands in her back pockets/Bette Davis style”, “On her twenty-second birthday/She already is an old maid/To her, death is quite romantic/She wears an iron vest/Her profession's her religion/Her sin is her lifelessness”, At midnight all the agents/And the superhuman crew/Come out and round up everyone/That knows more than they do”, and The Titanic sails at dawn/Everybody's shouting/‘Which side are you on?’”. I will say that I think I like “Sad-Eyed Lady of the Lowlands” as a long closing track just that much more, though.

Overall, I think *Blonde on Blonde* > *Highway 61 Revisited* > *Blood on the Tracks*. All are fantastic though. I’m sad we’re shutting the door on Dylan, but all things must pass.

Favorite tracks: Like a Rolling Stone, Tombstone Blues, Ballad of a Thin Man, Queen Jane Approximately, Just Like Tom Thumb’s Blues, Desolation Row

Least favorite track: From a Buick 6

# Aidan's review

The first Dylan album on here that I haven't heard and I've been missing out. Like anyone with ears and a white male parent over 40, I'd heard Like a Rolling Stone many times, and the (pretty good) Complete Unknown movie made sure to play it again when I saw it in 2024. When I listened to this album, I was retroactively annoyed that hey didn't include any other songs from it besides the title track, especially considering it was from the time period the film focused the most on, and the songs are so incredible. I've moaned about Dylan's voice with the blues, but the uptempo Tombstone Blues relies on tempo rather than Dylan's vocals to give it energy. Ballad of a Thin Man is Dylan at his most biting and the title track somehow manages to pull of that stupid fucking whistle. The album closes on what's certainly its best song, probably my favorite Dylan song and maybe even my favorite song from this project, Desolation Row. There's so many incredible lines in those 11 minutes, from the opening "They're selling postcards of the hanging" to "And she also keeps the cards that read 'have mercy on his soul'" and "Everyone is making love or else expecting rain". It's an eleven minute miracle of songwriting coming on top of an already great album. One of my favorite albums so far.
