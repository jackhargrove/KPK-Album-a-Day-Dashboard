# Write-up

For more information about David Byrne, see the two write-ups I did on Talking Heads’ *Remain in Light* and *Speaking in Tongues*. For more information on Brian Eno, see the write-up Aidan did on *Ambient 1: Music for Airports* last year. *My Life in the Bush of Ghosts* is a collaboration between the two artists. The duo recorded this album after finishing work on Talking Heads’ third album *Fear of Music* (which Eno produced) in 1979 but before recording Talking Heads’ fourth album *Remain in Light* (also produced by Eno) in 1980. However, because of its innovative use of sampling, a technique that at the time had little legal precedent, the album’s release was delayed by legal issues until 1981. The album takes its name from the 1954 Nigerian novel of the same name, which follows a young boy in West Africa who escapes the slave trade and must find his way through the wilderness. Instrumentally, the album reflects both Eno’s then-recent ambient style of music and Byrne’s obsession with African drum rhythms and African-American funk. Vocally, the album entirely uses samples taken from sources including Arabic chanting, radio broadcasts, and religious sermons. It was not the first album to ever use samples, but it was perhaps the first to use them in full as an unadulterated component of the music. Eno cited CAN’s Holger Czukay as his inspiration for using samples.

The process of clearing all the samples took years, with some being rejected. In the case of the track “Qu’ran”, a sample of Qu’ranic recital was used and cleared for the album’s initial release. However, it angered the Islamic Council of Great Britain, who declared it blasphemy. In response, Eno and Byrne decided to replace the track with the b-side “Very, Very Hungry” on all future editions. The tracks “Regiment” and “The Carrier” sampled a Lebanese singer who was unaware of the samples until 2017, at which point Eno and Byrne worked out a deal with her to keep them on streaming. “Regiment” also features Talking Heads member Chris Frantz on drums, as well as Robert Fripp of King Crimson on “Frippertronics” (Fripp’s proprietary style of tape looping). As if that weren’t enough, “Regiment” also features bassist Busta Jones, a touring member of Talking Heads who would join Gang of Four in the early 80s. Mingo Lewis, who plays a bàtá African drum on “The Jezebel Spirit” and “The Carrier”, would go on to play percussion on XTC’s *Skylarking*. Another percussionist from this album, David Van Tieghem, returned to play on Talking Heads’ *Speaking in Tongues*.

The album received mixed reviews upon release, garnering praise from Rolling Stone but facing criticism for being self-indulgent and bland by critics not yet sold on ambient or electronic music. In retrospect, the album’s reputation has increased sharply, particularly given the enduring popularity of both worldbeat and sample-based music since. One of the album’s earliest proponents was Kate Bush, who sang its praises as early as 1985. Richard Wright of Pink Floyd and Hank Shocklee of the Bomb Squad (Public Enemy’s team of DJs) have also cited it as an influence. The track “Mea Culpa” was later used as a sample by both Fishmans and Makaveli (alter ego of Tupac), while “Mountain of Needles” was sampled by Nine Inch Nails for a remix of “Closer”.

# Hargrove's review

I had been pretty excited for this one because I like so much of both Eno’s and Byrne’s work, both together and separately, and I had no idea this album existed. It didn’t really live up to my expectations, but it didn’t quite miss them entirely either. The fundamental problem is that the first half of this record has unique banger after unique banger, while the second half falls into indistinct mush. I have a feeling that Byrne had less involvement with the album’s second half and, as a result, a lot of the oomph was lost. It’s honestly one of the steepest fall-offs I’ve ever heard a single album have, which is especially crazy because it’s not like it’s an overlong record stuffed with filler. It’s only 40 minutes long! And yet, starting with “Qu’ran”, every track just suddenly sheds all definition and structure. This is only so frustrating because there are some genuinely very good songs on the first half. I don’t want to get into it too much in this paragraph, but every track from “America Is Waiting” through “Jezebel Spirit” has something cool and new to get into every time I listen. They are five strong tracks. Focusing only on this part of the record, it feels a lot like *Remain in Light* but with the dense central elements removed. Instead it’s the overall aura of the album expanded to the length and consistency of a cloud (interestingly, Oingo Boingo’s *Only a Lad* is a near perfect counterpart, taking only the attention-grabbing new wave elements of *Remain in Light* and distilling them into a cube of tungsten). In this way, *My Life in the Bush of Ghosts* represents the perfect combination of Eno’s and Byrne’s strengths; that is, until halfway through. It’s a frustrating album to review, but we’ll always have those first five tracks.

The record kicks off with “America Is Waiting”, which provides the perfect intro in both samples and instrumental. The funky guitar builds and coalesces slowly, creating an on-ramp to the album’s sound. The sample invites the music directly, telling Byrne and Eno not to take so long. I enjoy this track a lot, but “Mea Culpa” is even better. While it also nails the sample, the highlight is easily the drums. That bodhrán sounds INSANE, like a stampede of wildebeests coming at you. “Regiment” tops its predecessor once again, led by a funk guitar part with immense swagger. It just sounds great. I also like the sung female vocals in the sample. “Help Me Somebody” is the worst of the first five, but still quite good. It’s got a lot going on, to the point of being busy, but it nails a jungle vibe perfectly. I love the sample of the exorcism on “The Jezebel Spirit”. It’s a stellar find. The instrumental works too, kind of twinkling and with a good groove. After this point, I have very little to say about the album, positive or negative. “Qu’ran”, “The Carrier”, “A Secret Life”, “Come With Us”, and “Mountain of Needles” are all bleh, indistinct nothingness. They don’t even have much ambient value. They just exist, neither enticing nor offending. The only break from this is “Moonlight in Glory”, which is actively grating and bad. I do not like its musical ostinato, nor do I like the jerking of the sample. With that one negative exception, the second half of this record is just a complete nothingburger.

But then, that first half had a lot of what I wanted. And it’s not like most of the second half is offensive. It’s tough to choose between low B and high C for this. Ultimately, I think I’m going to use it to break up my classic rock cluster in high C because those are also albums with high highs and low lows.

Favorite tracks: America Is Waiting, Mea Culpa, Regiment, Help Me Somebody, The Jezebel Spirit

Least favorite track: Moonlight in Glory
