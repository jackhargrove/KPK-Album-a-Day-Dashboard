# Write-up

Burial is the recording alias of British electronic musician William Bevan. Bevan was anonymous and known only as Burial until the media discovered his identity in 2008, the year after *Untrue* released. Before Bevan’s identity was revealed, many speculated that the man behind Burial was Aphex Twin or Fatboy Slim. Bevan came of age during the flourishing UK electronic music scene of the 90s and attended school with Kieran Hebden, who would become influential producer Four Tet. Bevan released his self-titled debut as Burial in 2006 in the dubstep style that was becoming popular in the UK at the time (more on genres later). It was an instant critical success, garnering praise for its gloomy atmosphere and musical subtlety. Bevan released his second album as Burial, *Untrue*, in 2007. It received further acclaim and commercial success and is still today his most well-known album. In the nearly 20 years since the release of *Untrue*, Burial has not released a full album. Instead, Bevan has used the Burial project to release increasingly experimental EPs, as well as multiple collaborations with Four Tet and Radiohead’s Thom Yorke. He is still releasing music as of July 31, 2025.

*Untrue* is Burial’s signature album and cemented his particular style of music, known as future garage. His production is notable for his refusal to use trackers or sequencers, which gives his music feel more like live music. *Untrue* is also notable for its use of vocals, something that is relatively uncommon in the main genres of EDM. *Untrue* is often grouped with the Hauntology movement, a musical movement in the 2000s that used some ideas from French philosophers to try to create a dark nostalgic feeling. Multiple other artists we’ve listened to are grouped with or inspired by Hauntology, including Tim Hecker, Oneohtrix Point Never, Ariel Pink, and Blank Banshee. *Untrue* was highly acclaimed at the time, and is still considered one of the best and most important electronic records of the 21st century so far.

I believe this is the first mainline EDM record we’ve listened to since Aphex Twin, so I get to have some fun explaining genres. *Untrue* is dubstep, which ultimately descends from house music in the early 80s (as does a great deal of EDM). House is kind of the original EDM genre, evolving in clubs in the early 80s from disco music made with electronic instruments. One of the most notable early house DJs was Larry Levan, who would DJ at the Paradise Garage club in NYC. Levan’s particular style came to be known as “garage house” or just “garage”. Garage music would eventually make its way to the UK in the 90s, where it became known as UK garage. In the late 90s, a form of UK garage developed known as 2-step, which abandoned the steady and repetitive four-on-the-floor drum beats of house and most other EDM styles for twitchy and jittery rhythms. 2-step became all the rage in the UK by the early 2000s. By the mid-2000s, people had combined the hazy production techniques from dub, a style of reggae, with 2-step to create dubstep. Dubstep became very popular in the UK by the mid-2000s, and then Burial came in with his own style of dubstep called future garage which evoked nostalgia for the original garage house of the late 80s. Dubstep took diverging pathways from this point. Artists like Skrillex brought the dubstep style to America, but they added things like a strict buildup and bass drop structure. This style became known, derogatorily, as “brostep”. Brostep dominated American clubs in the late 00s and early 2010s and is considered by some to be the worst music style of all time, although it’s having a bit of a Renaissance. In the UK, dubstep went multiple directions, but the most interesting is the minimalist, vocal-centric style known as post-dubstep. FKA Twigs is the most notable artist from the post-dubstep scene.

# Hargrove's review

I unironically listened to this for the first time after Grimes and Elon named their first kid after one of the songs. Sue me. I was and am a huge Grimes fan. I thought it was pretty middling at the time. Electronic music is like speaking a foreign language for me, and while I am happy I’ve found a good amount of electronic music that I like, it’s always going to be an uphill battle. This had some interesting sounds, but nothing really stood out to me. Coming back to it after half a decade, I feel largely the same, but a little more positive. My listens yesterday really made me appreciate the skittery drum beats. I liked how complex, human-feeling, and sometimes even anxiety-inducing the beats are. The vocals are really hit-or-miss for me. Sometimes they have just the right amount of melodrama and sadness, but other times they can get really grating if they miss the mark. Absent the opener and the 4 ambient interludes, this record can get really same-y, which is punishing over repeated listens. Every one of the main tracks truly follows the same formula. It’s EDM, so repetition is to be expected, but that was something I found a bit tiresome. Still, I think this is a pretty neat record. In terms of atmosphere, it actually evokes some of the feelings that hard bop and post-bop jazz do for me. Sleek urban nightclubs at 3 am in the rain. This album is a little meh, but I would move my feelings on it from slightly negative before to slightly positive after.

I’m going to get the opener and the 4 ambient interludes (“Endorphin”, “In McDonalds”, “Dog Shelter”, “UK”) out of the way first: I don’t think these add a ton to the record, and really they just serve to drag out the run time. I think this would be a tighter collection of songs without them (although you can keep the opener for vibes). “UK” is my favorite of these for whatever reason, it just flows well. “Endorphin” is my least favorite of these and my least favorite track on the album. The opener and the other two are somewhere in between. “Archangel” is the best track and the prime example of everything this album does well. The drums are jittery, the vocals are melodramatic, and I really like the background strings. It’s a bop. “Near Dark” follows with a spindly, spidery drum line I really enjoy. “Ghost Hardware” rounds out the banger first part of the album with a really interesting bass synth part. “Etched Headplate” follows “Endorphin” to start the middle part of the album as pretty mid. It’s too quiet, and the vocal line is very meandering. “Untrue” brings the album back on track though. I really like the vocals here, and the rhythm section is firing on all cylinders. “Shell of Light” reminds me of Daft Punk and other more traditional house musicians. It’s pretty decent but the end is drawn out and a little lame so it just misses favorite track status. “Homeless” doesn’t have a ton of distinguishing features, but I enjoyed it enough on my final listen to add it to my favorite tracks. “Raver” is a fantastic closer with perhaps the coolest drum beat on the album. The drums have a tropical timbre, almost like a steel drum. The synth is soaring, and I like the voice that sounds like a saxophone at the end. Strong finish.

Overall, this is pretty middle of the pack for albums we’ve listened to, but I enjoyed spending a day listening. I definitely see myself revisiting.

Favorite tracks: Archangel, Near Dark, Ghost Hardware, Untrue, Homeless, Raver

Least favorite track: Endorphin

# Aidan's review

In the late aughts and early 2010s, dubstep burned a trail through popular music, conquering the charts and attracting bandwagons from stars as big as Britney and Taylor. It was bold, brash, proudly artificial and loud. Everyone promptly grew to hate it and it was gone as soon as it came, leaving just annoying stereotypes and some not great pop. Untrue is not that kind of dubstep. It's intimate, it's touching, it's hesitant and just a remarkable album. The sampling is the first thing that stands out in this album, they're simultaneously incredibly personal while also corrupted by the artificiality of pitch shifting and a smattering of effects. The result is something that elevates the mundane to something beautiful, a hymn to the small moments. The drums have the same superposition, they put out incredibly these human-feeling herky jerky beats that are repeated over and over again until it's impossible to mistake the source as anything other than a computer. And the coup de grace is the effects strewn across the tracks, be it rain, the “crackle of vinyl” (never knew that was even a sound), or even less identifiable sounds that somehow still feel entirely natural within the course of the album. There's no draw dropping moments, no incredibly catchy single melody and certainly no Skrillex-esque beat drop. Yet despite the lack of "wow" moments, Untrue is stylistic and focused in a way that very few other albums are. There's such an intense feeling of place with this record, it's urban, it's claustrophobic, it's humble, it's downtrodden yet hopeful. I'm probably never going to be the biggest fan of dubstep and that's the single thing that stops me from having this album as an all time favorite, but I'm still blown away by how focused the vision that Burial presents is and how well he accomplishes that.
