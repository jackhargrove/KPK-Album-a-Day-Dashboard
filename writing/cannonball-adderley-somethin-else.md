# Write-up

Julian “Cannonball” Adderley was a jazz saxophonist from Florida. He was born in Tampa in 1928, raised in Tallahassee, and moved near Miami for college in 1948. He was given the nickname “Cannonball” as a child by the other kids; they were trying to call him “Cannibal”, in reference to how much he ate, but mispronounced it. He was band director at a local high school for a few years before getting drafted for the Korean War in 1950, where he led an army band. After finishing his service, he moved to Queens, New York to study music. One night, he was at a club in Manhattan with his saxophone when he was asked to fill in for a missing musician. He did well, and generated enough buzz locally to get compared to Charlie Parker, saxophonist and father of bebop. He was eventually noticed by Miles Davis, and joined the Davis’ First Great Quintet as a second saxophonist alongside John Coltrane, turning it into the First Great Sextet. He played on various Davis records, including 1959’s *Kind of Blue*, the best selling jazz album of all-time. Adderley recorded the album *Somethin’ Else* as bandleader in 1958, with Davis playing trumpet. Adderley left the Davis Sextet in 1959 and formed his own quintet alongside his brother Nat. He and his band were quite successful across the 1960s and featured a rotation of well-known jazz artists. By the late 60s, Adderley started experimenting with electronic instruments. Adderley died of a brain hemorrhage in 1975 at age 46.

*Somethin’ Else* is Adderley’s best-known album as a bandleader. It features Adderley on saxophone, Miles Davis on trumpet, Hank Jones on piano, Sam Jones on bass, and Art Blakey (our bandleader in last week’s jazz album) on drums. It was engineered by Rudy Van Gelder, who has worked on many of the jazz albums on our list. Miles Davis was and is a much bigger star than Adderley, and his appearance on the record does unfortunately outshine Adderley’s; I can’t find hard proof, but I read some stuff about how this was intended to be a Davis record but they made it under Adderley’s name for contractual reasons. Davis chose which songs they would play and takes a few of the first solos. Davis also wrote the third track, “Somethin’ Else”, while Adderley’s brother Nat wrote the fourth track “One for Daddy-O”. Davis’ voice can be heard at the end of the latter track. The other three tracks are covers of standards written in the 30s and 40s.

Genre-wise, *Somethin’ Else* exists at the intersection of cool jazz and hard bop. Cool jazz was a relaxed style of jazz that developed in California as a reaction to the speed of bebop and was made popular by Dave Brubeck. Hard bop developed in New York as a reaction against cool jazz that brought back some of the speed and technicality of bebop, as well as many influences from contemporary black music styles like soul, blues, and R&B. *Somethin’ Else* has a little column A, a little column B.

# Hargrove's review

I am just not crazy about this one. It’s definitely way better than that terrible Max Roach album, but I’m really not moved here at all. And I’m having trouble determining why that is. There’s nothing that jumps out at me for why this album can’t hold my attention at all. Davis’ trumpet is a consistent highlight, good for solos and melodies. But otherwise, I have difficulty thinking of elements to praise. I think it kind of reflects some of the worst qualities of both cool jazz and hard bop. It’s consistently slow and plodding, aping the tempo of cool jazz without taking any of its swagger. And it’s rigorously structured like hard bop, but without the emotional intensity or strength of composition. It’s muddled, stylistically-confused background music. Adderley’s saxophone playing is fine, but it leaves me unimpressed. I found the piano playing to. be consistently lackluster, particularly the unimaginative solos. Art Blakey’s drums are a complete nonentity, a heartbreaking contrast with last week. The bass is good though, one of the bright spots along with Davis’ trumpet. The first two tracks have really good head melodies, but none of the others left anything in my head afterward. And not that it really matters, but I feel like it’s demonstrative of the album’s laziness that 3 of the 5 songs are covers of standards. Just kind of a bleh album. No, that isn’t what I wanted, Albert.

“Autumn Leaves” and “Love for Sale” are by far the two strongest tracks. The former is already too smooth and drawn out for my tastes, but at least it has that interesting section at the beginning and then again at the end. Great bass playing in those sections. Davis’ first trumpet solo is among the best on the album. The piano solo is the first of many bland piano solos. I heard Cannonball was on this one? And it’s soooooooo slooooooow. But overall, it’s fine. One of the better tracks. “Love for Sale” is certainly the best. The head is really quite good. The trumpet line is so lyrical and even a bit wistful. It’s actually stuck in my head quite well. Unremarkable otherwise, though. “Somethin’ Else” is entirely forgettable except for the interplay between the sax and the trumpet. It’s fun to hear them play back and forth, and it escalates throughout. Certainly notable however, how dominant the trumpet is in that relationship. “One for Daddy-O” is a slog, a complete slog. Soooooooooo sloooooooow. “Dancing In the Dark” ends things as blandly as the album deserves.

I’m being overly negative because I’m sick of listening to it. I very much do not hate this album. It’s quite nice at points, and if I didn’t feel the need to listen to it over and over again, I probably wouldn’t be so bothered. But man is it just uninteresting compared to pretty much every other jazz album we’ve listened to. Listening to it feels like pure opportunity cost.

Favorite tracks: Autumn Leaves, Love For Sale

Least favorite track: One for Daddy-O

# Aidan's review

3 of the biggest names in jazz, all ones we've seen before, plus one of the great sax players of the era, this album has incredible potential. It instead just hums along, hitting all the right notes (or per one definition of jazz, not hitting any of the wrong notes), but I was just underwhelmed throughout. It's fine, but something just never clicks, it lacks any sort of noticeable individualism, partially due to its nature as an amalgamation of songwriters. The only real constant is Adderly's sax, which takes lead throughout but is often outshone by the excellent supporting cast (by no fault of his own, he's playing with legends). Love for Sale is my favorite song, it feels tender and groovy, but the rest of the album never manages to strike the same balance. Solid, sturdy, but it only soars once.
