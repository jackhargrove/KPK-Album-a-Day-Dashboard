# Write-up

Captain Beefheart (real name Don Van Vliet) was an experimental blues singer born in 1941 and raised in the Mojave Desert, approximately 70 miles north of LA. At the time of this album, the Magic Band consisted of lead guitarist Ry Cooder, guitarist Alex St. Clair, bassist Jerry Handley, and drummer John French (nicknamed “Drumbo”). Beefheart was a prodigious sculptor from the age of 4 and even became an apprentice to a professional sculptor before he was 10, he gave it up as a teenager because his parents didn’t want him to be an artist. He instead turned to music, particularly blues and avant-garde jazz. He and his classmate Frank Zappa became close in high school, beginning a fruitful artistic collaboration resulting in two of the most important careers in art rock history. Despite his initial shyness, Beefheart developed his voice and frontman skills in the early 60s, using blues musician Howlin’ Wolf as an inspiration. His friend Alexis Snouffer (later Alex St. Clair) formed the Magic Band in 1965 and invited a 24-year-old Beefheart to become its lead singer. By the next year, they had somehow already signed to a major-independent label.

A few early blues and R&B singles in 1966 made the Magic Band local celebrities in LA. However, the demos they recorded for their debut studio album were deemed commercially unsuitable, and they were dropped from their label. They moved to the new Buddah Records and recruited two important new members: hotshot local guitarist Ry Cooder and drummer John French. The former helped grant the Magic Band some legitimacy and virtuosity, while the latter performed the necessary job of turning the musically illiterate Beefheart’s ideas into actual songs. This new lineup recorded their debut album *Safe as Milk*, the title a sarcastic reference to some delusion Beefheart had about pesticides contaminating breast milk (he spent most of the 60s on heroic doses of LSD and might have been schizophrenic). The music is a psychedelic take on blues with the spirit of garage rock and a madman on vocals. “Grown So Ugly” is a cover of a song by blues singer Robert Pete Williams, while Beefheart wrote the lyrics to “Call on Me”, “I’m Glad”, and “Abba Zaba” by himself; Beefheart and Herb Bermann collaborated to write the lyrics for every other track. Bermann was later credited as one of the two writers for the screenplay *After the Gold Rush* that inspired Neil Young’s 1970 song and album of the same name. *Safe as Milk*was a mild critical success, but an incident at the famous Monterey Pop Festival in which a drugged-out Beefheart walked off the stage neutered its commercial viability. This incident caused Cooder to quit, beginning his long career as a guitar aficionado and expert of “Americana” music. The album sold better in the UK, a theme that would continue for Beefheart’s next few albums. John Lennon and George Harrison were early fans and considered *Safe as Milk* one of their all-time famous albums. The Beatles tried to sign Beefheart to their record label, but they were talked out of it.

Captain Beefheart would go on to have a one-of-a-kind career that proved highly influential. Though he would always remain rooted in blues, his sound quickly went off the rails into an indescribable and inimitable anarchic fever dream. His most celebrated work is his 1969 album *Trout Mask Replica*, which we listened to in February as our second album. Despite its ultra-challenging sound, it inspired countless weirdos to take creative risks and now sits in the Library of Congress. Artists of the related punk (Sex Pistols, the Clash), post-punk (Minutemen, Gang of Four, Pere Ubu), new wave (Devo, Talking Heads, XTC), and industrial (Throbbing Gristle) movements cited him as an influence. Beefheart retired from music in 1982, at which point he became a celebrated visual artist. He suffered from multiple sclerosis, rendering him disabled by the 90s and killing him in 2010.

# Hargrove's review

I was NOT looking forward to this one after how brutal of a listen *Trout Mask Replica* was. However, *Safe as Milk* is actually a super fun, enjoyable, breezy listen. It’s a tight 32 minutes of punchy blues rock tracks sung by a crazed hippie. I really wish we had listened to this album before we listened to TMR because I re-listened to TMR last night for the first time since February and, while it’s still an incoherent mess, I got a lot more out of it now that I have a better understanding of where the deconstruction was coming from. Anyway, I was most taken aback by just how faithful and authentic all of *Safe as Milk* sounds. Captain Beefheart sounds genuinely soulful on so many of these tracks. There are understandably no original blues records on this list because those artists pre-date the album era, and what we do have on the list isn’t bad but also sounds distant from the origins of the sound (e.g. Led Zeppelin). Captain Beefheart, however, sounds legit here. That also goes for the soul songs, like “I’m Glad”. If you hadn’t told me who it was, I would definitely have guessed some 60s black southern soul singer. This album’s biggest flaw is that it gets really same-y by the end. It’s gimmicky, and that gimmick doesn’t last forever, especially near the end when the songwriting is nowhere near as good as the first side. Thank God it’s only half an hour. Still, this is a solid, fun record that gives me plenty of reasons to come back to it.

“Sure ‘Nuff ’n Yes, I Do” bangs as an opener. It’s bluesy as hell. He says “too” in such a crazy way. It’s a nice blues track that stays true to the genre while also sounding distinctively like Captain Beefheart. He makes things a little more rockin’, and also a little more soulful, on “Zig Zag Wanderer”. His vocals sound exquisite in the part halfway through where the instrumental quiets down and things speed up. I love the twinkling, feedback-drenched guitar on “Call on Me”, but its weak melody and lackluster songwriting make it my least favorite on the first side. I have a lot of fun with “Dropout Boogie” though. There’s a catchy guitar riff, a funny voice and dialogue, and pretty waltz interludes between the early punk rock main sections. What’s not to love? “I’m Glad” sounds straight up like an authentic soul song, from Beefheart’s impassioned vocals to the swinging backing vocals to the gorgeously lush instrumentation. “Electricity” has a cool theremin and picks up into a nice song about a minute in, which is enough for me to call it a favorite, but I don’t understand why it’s by far the album’s standout. I just don’t love his vocals, I guess. “Yellow Brick Road” kinda blows. It’s just a bit too upbeat, and sounds a bit too stereotypically 60s psychedelia. “Abba Zaba”, on the other hand, is so damn catchy. I’ve had it stuck in my head for days. I love the nonsense singing, as well as the high notes in the vocals. The last four songs on the album, “Plastic Factory”, “Where There’s Woman”, “Grown So Ugly”, and “Autumn’s Child”, are all fine but unremarkable. They’re nice to listen to in isolation, but they don’t really add anything to an album that’s already quite one-note. The songwriting also noticeably takes a dive. It’s not catastrophic, but if this album were any longer it would start to get exponentially worse.

This was a surprisingly fun album! I enjoyed listening to it. But the mono version is way, way, way better than the stereo version!

Favorite tracks: Sure ‘Nuff ’n Yes, I Do, Zig Zag Wanderer, Dropout Boogie, I’m Glad, Electricity, Abba Zaba

Least favorite track: Yellow Brick Road
