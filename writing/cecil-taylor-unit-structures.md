# Write-up

Cecil Taylor was a jazz pianist born in 1929 and raised in Queens. He began playing piano at age six and was classically trained in the instrument at the New England Conservatory. As a result of this background, he frequently drew influence from the cutting-edge composers of the European classical world like Karlheinz Stockhausen and Béla Bartók. Taylor developed a unique style of piano playing that is often likened to percussion; his piano was once famously described as “eighty-eight tuned drums”. Upon graduating from conservatory, Taylor returned to NYC in 1955 and formed his own quartet. Though the Cecil Taylor Quartet’s 1957 debut *Jazz Advance* was grounded in bop, its avant-garde impulses made it one of the first ever examples of post-bop and even “The New Thing”, a movement that would become free jazz by the 60s. Taylor’s music only got weirder from there, to the point that he struggled to book gigs in NYC clubs by the late 50s.

Taylor’s most important recording of the 60s was *Unit Structures*, released in 1966. In addition to Taylor’s piano, it features trumpeter Eddie Gale, alto saxophonist Jimmy Lyons, alto saxophonist/oboist/bass clarinetist Ken McIntyre, bassist Henry Grimes, bassist Alan Silva, and drummer Andrew Cyrille. Only Silva and Cyrille are still alive as of 2026.

Taylor continued to release records for the rest of the 20th century. Lyons became his most frequent collaborator, his bluesy sax playing the only thing keeping Taylor’s avant-garde compositions grounded in jazz tradition. Taylor’s group eventually took the name the Cecil Taylor Unit. Taylor’s position as one of the pre-eminent pioneers of free jazz resulted in him becoming a decently well-known name despite the impenetrability of his genre. He was even invited to play on the White House lawn by President Jimmy Carter in the late 70s. When Lyons died in 1986, Taylor disbanded the Cecil Taylor Unit and formed the Feel Trio, a group that fully left jazz behind for unbounded free improvisation. He remained at the forefront of his sound, earning a MacArthur Genius Grant in 1993 at the age of 64. Taylor began ramping down his career in the 2000s, though he began to receive numerous lifetime achievement awards. Taylor died in Brooklyn in 2018. He was 89 years old.

# Hargrove's review

There are seldom few albums on the list that I have found this impenetrable and difficult to crack. I really put in effort with this one, even reading the dense, Joycean four-page treatise that Taylor included in the liner notes for this album. And yet, I’ve made it to writing this review without having succeeded at finding a way into this album. Not only do I not fully understand the way it’s supposed to make me feel, it doesn’t make me feel any way at all. Whether I’m listening to it in the background to gain familiarity or listening to it with intense focus to try and figure it out, it doesn’t change my mood at all. Much of it might as well be random playing; perhaps that’s what it is, though I had hoped there would be more to free jazz. There were a few bits I liked, particularly in the second track “Enter, Evening (Soft Line Structure)” which was the only one that created any kind of tangible atmosphere for me to describe. There were also some nice skittery sections in the middle of “Unit Structure/As of a Now/Section” that I found myself enjoying. The saxophone was a consistent highlight as well. As for Taylor’s piano, well, I just didn’t get it at all. I don’t understand the description of his playing as percussive. It feels really important to the music that he plays so many different pitches so frequently. I don’t find that to have a percussive effect. Instead, it’s just a melodic and harmonic drag. Overall, I come away from this album understanding Cecil Taylor about as well as I did before I had even heard of him.

“Steps” starts chaotic but slow, with the drums going crazy in my right ear. My favorite part of the track is when the sax goes bonkers, but for the most part I found the instruments all blended together with no particular direction. I appreciated the softer playing of “Enter, Evening (Soft Line Structure)” more. It actually does feel like the entrance of the evening in the atmosphere it creates. I particularly like the oboe, as well as the way the track keeps a nicer pacing and more space between each instrument. “Unit Structure/As of a Now/Section” starts as a mishmash of instruments, becomes enjoyable for its fast pace and hard playing in the middle, then returns to a mishmash of instruments. If this track were just the middle section, it would easily be my favorite, with the squealing sax over the nutso drum line. Unfortunately, it’s not just the middle section, and at 17 minutes it drags on for quite some time. “Tales (8 Whisps)” keeps the focus largely on Taylor’s piano. Given that Taylor’s piano is one of my least favorite parts of the album, this is my least favorite track.

Maybe I’ll get into the Albert Ayler album in a way I didn’t with this one. Maybe it’ll even help me find a foothold into *Unit Structures*. Or maybe free jazz is not the genre for me.

Favorite tracks: Enter, Evening (Soft Line Structure)

Least favorite track: Tales (8 Whisps)
