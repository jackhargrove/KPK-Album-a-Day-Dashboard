# Write-up

Charles Mingus is a jazz bassist and bandleader from Los Angeles who was active in the bebop and post-bop eras (40s through 70s). He is the jazz musician most loved by artsy rock music nerds too contrarian to say Miles Davis or John Coltrane. He was raised on church music in the 20s and 30s, but as an adolescent he trained as a cello player in classical music. He would always maintain that the cello was his greatest love, but at the time there were no jobs for black musicians in professional classical music and no place for cello in jazz. Instead, he picked up the closest jazz equivalent: the double bass (also called an upright or standup bass). However, he developed composition skills from classical music that he would put to use in developing a style known as “third stream”. At the time, classical was seen as the first stream of music and jazz the second, so third stream combined the two.

Mingus began working as a professional jazz bassist in 1943, first for Duke Ellington and then for Louis Armstrong. During this time, he met Charlie Parker (later known as “Yardbird” or just “Bird”), a young saxophonist who would become the most influential jazz musician of all time through his development of bebop. Mingus, like many of his contemporaries, would classify all music as either “pre-Bird” or “post-Bird”. In the early 50s, Mingus moved to NYC, where he cofounded Debut Records with drummer Max Roach. Mingus, Roach, Parker, Dizzy Gillespie (trumpet), Bud Powell, and Thelonious Monk (both piano) were some of the defining musicians of bebop, a fast, technical, and improvisational style of jazz that departed from the earlier dance music of swing. In 1955, Mingus played a “reunion” show with Parker, Roach, and Powell. Powell and Parker ruined it with their public drunkenness and drug use, prompting Mingus to denounce them to the audience. Parker died a week later from drug overdose at age 34.

In the 1950s, Mingus released the bulk of his best known solo records as bandleader. His most loved from this period was 1959’s *Mingus Ah Um*, a defining work of post-bop (abstract and experimental descendant of bebop) .”Goodbye Pork Pie Hat” from that album would become one of his defining tracks. Also in 1959, Mingus attended one of Ornette Coleman’s wild concerts in NYC, which shocked him into pursuing more avant-garde sounds. In 1963, Mingus released *The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady*, widely regarded as his masterpiece. Despite some improvisational elements, it’s through-composed from beginning to end as a coherent work with six movements. It is structured as a ballet (see the dancer instructions in the track titles) and includes flamenco and other Spanish-influenced sounds. The band for the album includes 12 musicians, making it the first big band jazz piece we’ve listened to.

Mingus’ musical output slowed significantly starting in the second half of the 60s. In the 70s, he began feeling the effects of ALS (yes, the one Stephen Hawking had). While nearing his deathbed, he made music with 70s folk rock legend Joni Mitchell. He died in 1979 at age 56. He is remembered today as a jazz legend, one of the most notable jazz bassists, and perhaps the greatest jazz composer.

# Hargrove's review

What a fucking album man (you’re going to hear that a lot; this is the most consistently excellent week we’ve had so far). This shit knocked my socks off. It’s everything that I hoped *A Love Supreme* would be. And that’s not shade to Coltrane, I enjoyed that record quite a bit. But it didn’t hit me hard in the chest and knock me over like so many claimed it did for them. Instead, *The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady* is the through-composed avant-garde jazz record that I can feel reverberating in my bones. It’s funny because I just wrote in my review for *Doolittle* that the Pixies’ biggest strength is their controlled chaos, letting all of their prominent elements shine without everything spiraling into nonsense. Well, I don’t have to change a word of that to describe *The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady*. There are so many different instruments, some of which I couldn’t even recognize (apparently the “wa wa” sounds were a muted trumpet and trombone), and none of them wait their turn. There are solos, yes, but any instrument is liable to kick in at any point, whether it’s for a sudden climactic accelerando, or just for accents. Despite this, it never feels messy or muddled. Every little piece knows its place, knows how to unselfishly blend into the broader tapestry. Remarkably, and I’m not just saying this because I know he’s the bandleader, what keeps everything together is Mingus’ bass. I was a tuba player, I played bass lines, I’m partial to rhythm sections and good bass. But still, I did not expect Mingus’ playing to be so far and above any other jazz bass I’ve heard before. He has the exact right notes for every moment and he always shines through from the back to the front. He’s a super glue that keeps everything together and moving forward. And though it does always go forward, I was struck by the constant ups and downs, ins and outs, expansions and contractions of this record. From the volume to the melodies to the arpeggios to the tempo, *everything* in this record oscillates. It makes the music feel alive, as though it has a beating heart, or is a restful giant breathing in and out, slowly but deeply. It really, truly feels like a living being. I don’t know how much music I can say that about.

“Track A - Solo Dancer” catches my attention immediately with that weird scrunchie sounding thing (muted trumpet/trombone, I now know). The intro builds tension masterfully, and my first instinct was to expect a long, continuous build. But no! This album builds and releases tension at a moment’s notice. A cataclysmic crescendo/accelerando can just come in the middle of a section, and once it’s over you return straight back to that melody line from before. The solo that starts about 4 minutes in (I think it’s soprano sax) is one of my favorites on the album. “Track B - Duet Solo Dancers” opens with a section that makes it sound like I’m in a dark, grimy cafe in a big city late at night. It’s a quintessential jazz sound, an image that can only form in my head if the rhythm is swung, but it’s almost jarring coming after the grandiosity of the opening track. I love this. It reminds me what I’m listening to whenever things get too far afield. I feel like I’ve fallen asleep in my cups at a jazz bar and dreamed I could fly before being awoken at the beginning of this movement. The entire middle section of this movement is batshit climax after batshit climax before we return to the dark cafe at the end. Perhaps I’ve had too much to drink. Perhaps I’m losing control of my life by day. Perhaps I need some sentimental piano to soothe my suffering. "Track C - Group Dancers” delivers. This movement is my least favorite just because it doesn’t feel like it stands on its own, but it introduces two ideas that will get fully fleshed out in the epic on side 2: a twirling, tumbling melody that makes me feel like I’m in a quirky horror film, and the first notes from that Spanish guitar that drives me loco.

As we enter “Medley: Mode D - Trio and Group Dancers/Mode E - Single Solos and Group Dance/Mode F”, I’ve jolted back awake at the dark cafe. As the wa wa sounds return to let me know not everything is quite right, a cinematic motif is introduced that makes me feel like I’m stuck in a classic Hollywood film. I absolutely love this. It’s my favorite motif on the album. It introduces such a magical feeling, though I don’t know if it’s in my dream or here in reality. There are two fantastic Spanish guitar solos that bookend what has to be one of the fiercest accelerandos I’ve ever heard. In this track, things just build up and then crash back down, and the rate of accelerandos constantly increases (is this the first time I’ve used a third derivative to describe a record?). Now, even the accelerandos are part of this album’s beating heart. As the movement goes on, we travel through every musical idea again and again, jolting back and forth from the tumbling melody to the cinematic melody to the wa wa sounds to the Spanish guitar to an accelerando to the dark cafe. It’s a truly transcendent collage that blurs the line between dream and reality completely. It feels like my life is flashing before my eyes. However, it ends with us at the cafe as a lonely saxophone plays us out.

Really, really cool record. Up there with my favorite jazz records we’ve heard. And each time I listen, it’s better than the last. I cannot recommend this record enough.

Favorite tracks: Track A - Solo Dancer, Track B - Duet Solo Dancers, Medley: Mode D - Trio and Group Dancers/Mode E - Single Solos and Group Dance/Mode F - Group and Solo Dance

Least favorite track: Track C - Group Dancers (but it feels wrong to put anything here)
