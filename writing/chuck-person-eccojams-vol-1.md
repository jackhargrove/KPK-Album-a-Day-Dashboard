# Write-up

Chuck Person is one of many aliases used by electronic musician Daniel Lopatin. See above for my write-up on *Replica*, an album released under Lopatin’s most well-known project Oneohtrix Point Never, for more details on his life. In a career that has included acting as musical director for the Weeknd’s halftime show and collaborating with David Byrne, Lopatin’s most influential album was a side project release in 2010 that was originally limited to just 100 cassette tapes. Lopatin began releasing music in the style of *Chuck Person’s Eccojams Vol. 1* in 2009, posting his “eccojams” to the YouTube channel @sunsetcorp. These tracks were made up of entirely sampled material, usually short vocal samples from highly commercial 80s pop songs made to sound contemplative through looping, slowing them down, and adding copious amounts of reverb. Lopatin coined the term “eccojam” in reference to *Ecco the Dolphin*, a 1992 game for the Sega Genesis with a sleek, corporate art style that matched the vibe of the pop songs Lopatin chopped up. The album’s cover fittingly chops up and rearranges the cover of the original *Ecco the Dolphin* game. Though Lopatin didn’t release any eccojams until 2009, he had been making them on his own since 2004. He took inspiration from numerous artists that came before him, namely the sinister and nostalgic style of Boards of Canada, the reflective reverb of William Basinski’s *The Disintegration Loops*, the chopped and screwed production techniques of DJ Screw, and the hazy, dreamlike atmospheres of hypnagogic pop artists like Ariel Pink (and, by extension, Animal Collective). As the @sunsetcorp YouTube channel began to pick up steam online, Lopatin released a full album of eccojams under the alias Chuck Person in 2010. The style of eccojams would become specifically associated with samples of 80s music, and the majority of the tracks on this album follow that pattern; however, it includes a greater variety of source material than later projects, with “A5” sampling a Byrds song from the 60s, “A7” sampling a prog song from the 70s, “B5” sampling a 2Pac song from the 90s, and “A3” sampling two pop tracks from the early 00s. While the release of *Chuck Person’s Eccojams Vol. 1* was limited to 100 physical cassette tapes, Lopatin also uploaded each track on YouTube.

Though *Chuck Person’s Eccojams Vol. 1* was niche upon its 2010 release, it proved to be unbelievably influential in online music circles. Other artists began uploading their own eccojams, as the production process was particularly easy. By 2011, artists like Vektroid (also known as Macintosh Plus) established the vaporwave genre using eccojams as a template. While *Chuck Person’s Eccojams Vol. 1* thus predates the concept of vaporwave, it is usually viewed in retrospect as the founding document of the genre, and eccojams became the first of many subgenres of vaporwave. Vaporwave became one of the defining musical styles of the 2010s, establishing the hazy, nostalgic, ironic, 80s electronic aesthetic that would reach artists as mainstream as the Weeknd (good) and the Chainsmokers (bad). The entire “slowed and reverbed” phenomenon on YouTube comes from vaporwave. The line between eccojams and vaporwave is poorly defined, but I usually think of vaporwave as a broader and more complex take on the style, while eccojams are usually just individual songs chopped and screwed.

# Hargrove's review

I listened to *Chuck Person’s Eccojams Vol. 1* a few times when I first discovered vaporwave right before COVID, so I knew what the sound was like and the concept, but I hadn’t revisited it in over half a decade and did not remember any of the specifics. What struck me most was how enjoyable some of the tracks were even when setting aside the album’s process-based concept. I’ve come to expect this from Oneohtrix Point Never by now, given his M.O. is making genuinely good music out of unusual sources. I very much enjoyed what OPN was able to make out of television commercials in *Replica*, for example, or from a 90s internet public sample set on last year’s *Tranquilizer*. While neither of those albums nor *Chuck Person’s Eccojams Vol. 1* sound completely natural, they all at least serve as more than just sound art, more than just a technical curiosity. With this album, Lopatin had an easier base to pull from, as these are pre-existing pop songs that all have melodies and instrumentation. Still, Lopatin absolutely turns each sample into his own, removing any doubt that this is a wholly original and creative work of art. By slowing and pitching down all of these vocalists, Lopatin creates a uniform deep singing voice that helps the album feel unified, coherent, and dominated by one single author. The samples are all chopped up in such a way that I feel dragged into a dream world, spitting out malfunctioning bits and pieces of whatever music I had heard at the grocery store earlier today. It’s a disconcerting feeling, both comforting and unnerving, full of happy feelings that may prove to be entirely fabricated the second I wake up. Lopatin gives us a glimpse of the outside world at the end of side 1 on “A8”, where piercing static overwhelms the sputtering pop vocalizations, before returning to the dreamworld with side 2. Overall, I think this record is highly successful at conveying its intended concept, and decently successful at just being a good record of music. Some of the eccojams aren’t great to listen to, particularly on the second side. My primary complaint is that Lopatin sometimes overdoes it with the really short segments getting looped over and over again for a stuttering feel. Still, more of them sound good on their own than I expected, and I was satisfied with this listen more than I thought I would be for a legendary genre creator (which sometimes don’t live up to their reputations)

“Africa” by Toto has had such a major resurgence since 2010 that it puts “A1 (She’s Waiting)” into an entirely different context now, but it really was the perfect soulless earworm to start off this project. I particularly like the looped ascending sections that sound like a staircase up to nothingness. I have some issues with choppy editing of very short sections, but that’s a persistent issue across the album and I can get over it. “A2 (Angel)” is my favorite eccojam from the standpoint of pure listenability and enjoyability. That “angel please don’t go” bit gets stuck in my head, but then, it is Fleetwood Mac. I like how much it shifts between tempi and pitches as well; usually, I don’t appreciate that kind of disjointed editing, but it all blends into something nice and melancholy here. The best eccojams on this album are sequenced toward the front, continuing on track 3 with “A3 (Be Real/Dreams)”, my favorite of the tracks with two samples. The two samples directly contrast with each other in a way that is thematically pleasing. Both melodies were also curated well. “A4 (Demerol)” may be shorter than the other tracks, but, fuck, is it creepy. I like it because of how much it unsettles me. “A5 (The Door)” features a good vocal pull from the Byrds, and I like the role this lethargic, sleepy track plays at this point in the album, but it’s kind of meh in and of itself. The meat of the sample on “A6 (Alone)” works really well, driving home that dream world feel that side 1 has thus far cultivated. However, Lopatin goes overboard on the choppy repetitions on this track and it pulls me out of it. I especially don’t like that he focuses on that style for the outro. “A7 (Horsemen)” features a stellar sample pull. It’s an awesome vocal melody and an even better drum loop. I don’t know, I just enjoyed listening to it greatly. I don’t love the first sample of “A8 (Make You Mine/Information)” because it’s so chaotic, but I’m willing to overlook it because of how well this track works thematically. So far, it feels like I’ve entirely been inside of a dream state, and this first sample is like I’m tossing and turning. The second sample gives way to piercing static that pulls me out of the dream state entirely; along with the “where’d you get that information from?” refrain, it feels like I’ve been shown something I’m not supposed to see.

“B1 (Letters/Never Been Easy)” rejects the reality I’ve just been shown and instead keeps the good music coming. I love Lopatin’s DJ technique on the first sample, giving it a danceable rhythm. Then, the Kate Bush sample freaks me out. Unfortunately, the eccojams start to drop in quality beyond this point. I really don’t like the way the first sample is used in “B2 (Let It Go/Lightning Strikes)”. It’s too bouncy and almost has a weird Latin feel. It’s just not my thing, and a missed opportunity because I do like the Fleetwood Mac sample. The second sample of “B3 (One More Year/No Compromise)” is only okay, but the first is wistful enough to make me like the whole track. “B4 (Nobody Here)” is completely forgettable, in one ear and out the other. I don’t like “B5 (World)” at all. I don’t think hip hop fits the profile of an eccojam sample. In fairness, G-funk is the best form of hip hop to use, but this track doesn’t give me the same dream-like ruins of the others. “B6 (Silence)” is just as generic and forgettable as “B4 (Nobody Here)”. I like all three samples individually in “B7 (SOS/Womack Circuit/Woman in Chains)”, but I don’t really see how they connect with each other. It leaves me feeling a little unsatisfied. It’s not a bad track by any means, but it’s not what I wanted from a closer.

This was a solid, solid record. My tiering (somewhat unintentionally) tends to underrate heavily conceptual records because I find them more difficult to return to. However, this one satisfied me more than most, so I’m sticking it right into the middle of B Tier.

Favorite tracks: A1 (She’s Waiting), A2 (Angel), A3 (Be Real/Dreams), A4 (Demerol), A7 (Horsemen), A8 (Make You Mine/Information), B1 (Letters/Never Been Easy), B3 (One More Year/No Compromise)

Least favorite track: B5 (World)
