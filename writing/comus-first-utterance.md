# Write-up

Comus is British progressive/psychedelic folk band formed in Beckenham, Kent (outer part of London) in 1969 by acoustic guitarist and vocalist Roger Wootton and all-purpose guitarist Glenn Goring. Both were students in art school and regularly attended the Arts Lab run by a pre-fame David Bowie in the late 60s, who asked them to open for him at a club once. They quickly expanded into a 6-piece outfit, adding bassist Andy Hellaby, violinist and violist Colin Pearson, percussionist Bobbie Watson, and flautist and oboist Rob Young. You know, all the typical rock instruments. The name Comus refers to the late-antiquity Greek god of festivity and revelry (cupbearer to Dionysus).

They released their debut studio album *First Utterance* in 1971. The lyrics are quite dark, frequently referring to taboo topics like torture and rape. The cover was drawn with ballpoint pen by Wootton. Unsurprisingly, it sold poorly. The band’s lineup shifted around afterward, with the most notable addition being that of bassoonist Lindsay Cooper in 1972. She would later join Henry Cow and become about as famous as a bassoonist can be. They released a second album, *To Keep from Crying*, in 1974. It got bad reviews at the time and is still regarded as pretty dogshit. They broke up afterward. Wootton joined Slapp Happy, which eventually merged with Henry Cow, and Pearson went on to produce the global synthpop hit “Forever Young” by Alphaville in 1984. Though largely unappreciated at the time, Comus would become influential on multiple music scenes and genres. Opeth, a progressive metal band with an album on our list, are noted fans. Pioneering neofolk (folk + industrial, from the 90s) group Current 93 also took influence from Comus. Most important, however, was their influence on the freak folk scene of the early 2000s. Freak folk involved independent American artists who played psychedelic, weird forms of folk music and included Animal Collective, Devendra Banhart, and early Joanna Newsom. Thanks to this resurgence, Comus reformed in 2008 with 5/6 original members and has been together since, releasing a third album in 2012.

Comus’ musical style was made possible by the second British folk revival moment. The first revival began in 1890 and consisted of nationalist Brits compiling and conserving folk songs from the Medieval era and before. It saved a lot of music but overly focused on agricultural songs and fizzled out after WWI. The second revival began in the late 1940s, inspired by the American folk revival movement. A healthy scene formed, led by Ewan MacColl and Peggy Seeger (half-sister of Pete Seeger). This scene was politically leftist, focused on industrial folk songs over agricultural ones, and accepted musical influence from American folk. One outgrowth was the skiffle revival, started when Lonnie Donegan’s “Rock Island Line” unexpectedly exploded in 1956. Skiffle, an early 20th century American style where bands played blues music with jugs and washboards, defined the late 50s music scene in Britain. Among those who got their start in skiffle were Liverpool band the Quarrymen (later the Silver Beetles (later the Beatles)). Also in the 50s was the development of folk baroque, a style of British fingerpicked guitar based on modal chords that would continue to influence later folk artists like Nick Drake. As rock became dominant in the 60s, the folk scene began to follow it. When rock introduced psychedelic influences, so too did the British folk scene, producing Donovan, the Incredible String Band, and Tyrannosaurus Rex. When psychedelic rock gave way to the expansive, classical-influenced progressive rock, folk artists began writing 10+ minute songs with dynamic atmospheres and technical playing. Some prog rock groups like Jethro Tull shifted from rock to folk as well. Comus existed at the intersection of psychedelic and progressive folk. In recent years, Joanna Newsom has revived and perfected the progressive folk form.

# Hargrove's review

This was the first album in a while to really catch me off guard. I thought I knew what I was getting into, and I kind of had the right idea, but I was not prepared for the sheer intensity. This album sounds like genuine, honest-to-God (honest-to-Comus?) madness. When I took a class on Greek tragedies senior spring, my favorite play (yes, Aidan, I actually read through more than half of the assigned plays. It was interesting!) was Euripides’ *The Bacchae*. It described followers of Dionysus as they essentially laid siege to a city whose King rejected him. As you can imagine, a lot of these tragedies were, well, tragic; there was no shortage of violence, treachery, damnation, and despair. And yet, when Dionysus whipped the woman of the city into such a hedonistic, unrestrained fervor that the King’s own mother helps tear him limb from limb, I was terrified in way that couldn’t be matched. There’s something about the dark side of the pleasure, violence not as grim reality but the ultimate delight, that I find completely unnerving. And what is *First Utterance* if not a crazed exploration of hedonism to the detriment of all others? It only works when all of the elements are on the same page of insanity, and it’s a pretty inconsistent album. But when Wootton’s vocals are sufficiently unhinged, Watson’s vocals equal parts angelic and uncanny, Pearson’s violin adequately screeching, Young’s flute convincingly Medieval, Goring’s guitar percussively strummed, and Hellaby’s bass surprisingly active, Comus produces sheer, bone-chilling rapture. This is primarily true of the album’s two masterpieces, “Drip Drip” and “Song to Comus”. The other songs have their moments, but none that touch those two. This is one that I think I’m still cracking, and will be for some time. As of now, it feels like after I heard Henry Cow: weird as shit with high highs, if inconsistent.

“Diana” kicks the album off with a decently conventional song structure so as not to throw you into the deep end of the madness. While usually this kind of pop-fusion on a weirdo album would be my favorite track, what I enjoy most about this album is its freeform lunacy, so I find this one a little constrained compared to later tracks. Still, the tension of the violin and the beauty of the backing vocals make this a good track. “The Herald” is unfortunately my least favorite. The vocal bookends are quite nice, and the guitar part of the breakdown heavenly, but unfortunately those elements are not enough to carry a 12 minute song on their own. That being said, I have just learned that the original LP version of this track was 5 minutes, and the 12 minute version comes from a 1989 CD release. I’ve been looking for the original with no luck, so for now it’s my least favorite. But I could see it shooting up for me in the shorter version. The album begins its peak with “Drip Drip”. It starts out a bit messy, but once everything comes together I can’t do anything else for 10 minutes. That chorus is TERRIFYING. Everything is staccato, from the guitars to the strings to the vocals, making every note a stab straight into your ears. The lead vocals are a highlight; I still jump every time Wootton spits out “drip drip” with the grunt afterward. Like “The Herald”, “Drip Drip” has an extended instrumental section, but this time the focus on rhythm keeps me hypnotized. And then there are the lyrics. This song is straight up evil. It has a disgusting, disgusting aura. “Even the earth will fill the crack where entered my blade/Where entered my blade!” is not something anyone should ever say. It genuinely might be one of the most viscerally disturbing songs I’ve ever heard.

*First Utterance*’s second masterpiece is “Song to Comus”. “Drip Drip” left things very tense, and “Song to Comus” keeps things at that level. Wootton sounds deranged; I’ve never been so creeped out by the word “play”. The abrasion of the violin adds to this atmosphere, while the lovely flute work gives it a firm sense of time and place. It’s also just so damn catchy! I’ve had it stuck in my head. And, again, the lyrics. “Hymen hunter, hands of steel/Crack you open and your red flesh peel/He's gonna cut ya!/Pain procurer, eyes of fire/Pierce your womb and push still higher” is just foul. Between these two tracks, *First Utterance* has a towering, sustained 20 minute peak. “The Bite” is not as good as either, but brings things back to the quality of “Diana”. This is another one where Young uses the flute to great effect, though the highlight for me is the fast-paced vocals. Though far from the most disturbing on the album, I found the Christian’s plight and sudden doom moving. “Bitten” is a short instrumental that almost has a black metal feel to it with those tremolo guitars (NOTE: if you remove the distortion, black metal kinda sounds like early 60s surf). It sounds like a cross between King Crimson and a horror movie score. It’s alright. “The Prisoner” is sadly kind of a weak closer. I like the contrast of the idyllic intro with sudden insanity as the shock treatments start, but it’s just not a very satisfying song. If the original shorter version of “The Herald” is good like I hope, this would become my least favorite track.

Overall, I’m still kind of at a loss. Super cool album, but not one that’s easily compared to others. I’ll be thinking about “Drip Drip” and “Song to Comus” for a long time, but I don’t know how often I’ll be able to listen to them. I’m gonna put it in low A because I think it needs to be higher than a B.

Favorite tracks: Diana, Drip Drip, Song to Comus, The Bite

Least favorite track: The Herald

# Aidan's review

The combination of Satanism (or just general evil-ness) and prog is quite a weird one. The presence of the flute and the up-tempo nature of most of the songs makes this album end up sounding like the evil version of Jethro Tull. I liked Jethro Tull, although I found their lyrics a tad childish at the time, and these lyrics are probably a net improvement, although they do certainly cross the line of "mature" and keep going. I don't find them particularly interesting, but they do fit the general tone of the often nauseatingly sweet music. I'm partially fond of the guitar here, on The Herald (my favorite song and the least aggressively evil one) the arpeggios (I think that's the right term) are gorgeous, as is the individual picking that starts out the song. Bitten is a bit of failed experimentation, but it's just one song. My impression is this album is on the list for its largely lyric influence on freak folk, which is my least favorite part, but there's definitely something to enjoy here.
