# Write-up

dälek (pronounced like “dialect”) is an experimental hip hop group formed in Newark, NJ, in 1998 originally consisting of rapper dälek (real name Will Brooks), producer Oktopus (real name Alap Momin), and multi-instrumentalist Joshua Booth. MC dälek was a college student in New Jersey who used his scholarship money to buy an audio workstation and dropped out. The group has included various DJs as members, including DJ rEk (real name Rudy Chicata) and Still (real name Hsi-Chang Lin). Between 1998 and 2010, dälek released five studio albums, as well as a few EPs. After Booth left the group in 2009 to pursue a Ph.D. and Oktopus moved to Belin in 2010, dälek went on hiatus in 2011. The group returned in 2015 with only MC dälek and new producer Mike Manteca as official members. They have released four albums since, with another scheduled for 2026. They have also released two collaboration albums with German krautrock group Faust, one in 2004 and the other in 2019.

The members of dälek are known for their wide array of eclectic influences, ranging from hip hop artists like Public Enemy and De La Soul to alternative rock artists like my bloody valentine to metal artists like Jesu and Melvins. However, the influence they are most associated with is that of industrial music, specifically citing Einstürzende Neubauten, as well as Godflesh and the Young Gods. “Industrial hip hop” as a concept has existed since the late 80s, but dälek were important to formalizing the sound as we currently conceive of it, setting the stage for Death Grips, JPEGMAFIA, clipping., and Kanye West’s *Yeezus* in the 2010s and beyond.

*From Filthy Tongues of Gods and Griots* is the second album by dälek, released in 2002. A “griot” (pronounced “gree-oh”) is a type of oral historian and storyteller in some West African cultures. The album credits dälek (vocals), Oktopus (production), and Still (turntables) as official members of the group for the album. Booth (production, instruments), DJ rEk (cuts), and seven other contributors are credited as additional personnel. The album received a muted but positive response, with the strongest praise at the time coming from Pitchfork. In the 25 years since, it has remained dälek’s most popular album and been re-evaluated by many as a major inspiration on the industrial hip hop explosion of the 2010s.

# Hargrove's review

DAMN this album is a banger. This has got to be the strongest week we’ve had in some time because this will be the fourth album I place in A Tier or above. Obviously, I’m a huge fan of Death Grips, JPEGMAFIA, Danny Brown, and other practitioners of the industrial hip hop genre, so I’ve been looking forward to this album that allegedly presaged the style. At the same time, though, I was apprehensive because I’ve had a bit of a tough time getting into things labeled “experimental hip hop” of late. That was not so here, thankfully. This album starts strong with wailing instrumentals, hard-hitting drums, and vocals that flow perfectly. It even has hooks, something that a lot of the hip hop on this record leaves behind. It fuses all of these elements together in song after song after song after song. There are so many great tracks on this thing. There are technically 11 tracks, but two are short interludes, and one is a 12 minute experimental piece I’ll get into later. That leaves eight true “songs”, of which six are absolute bangers and one more is pretty good. All things considered, that is a pretty good hit rate! My biggest complaint about this album is that MC Dälek’s lyrics just aren’t all that interesting. He’s a gifted rapper, don’t get me wrong; his flow and hooks work wonderfully with the album’s sound. The construction and content of the lyrics, however, leaves a lot to be desired. He phrases things clumsily and tries a little too hard to brand himself as unique (“How many MC's know who Faust is?”). There’s also the elephant in the room, “Black Smoke Rises”. Look, this is a crazy cool track that I feel comfortable calling *truly* experimental, not just some noise thrown on top of a rap song. It definitely makes the album less listenable, though. Especially being smack-dab in the middle. But given the hit rate of this album’s main tracks, I gotta hand it to dälek. This is some good shit.

The first drop of searing feedback on “Spiritual Healing” after the soft piano opening straight up makes me want to cum. I just LOVE that noise, and I love hearing it in hip hop more than any other genre. The lyrics of the hook are kind of dumb and clunky, but, from the standpoint of catchiness, it’s a banger of a hook. And the screeching noise goes on and on. I love it. “Speak Volumes” contains some really tight drums, a consistent highlight across the album. Otherwise, though, I’m a little less impressed compared to the previous track, though it’s still good. “…From Mole Hills” amps things back up with these super cool tinny drums and my favorite lyrics on the album. I find “Could never quite quench my own thirst for ancient drums/There lies a language in the noise and the hum/Prepare for martyrdom, prepare for martyrdom/I speak that ancient tongue/There lies a language in the noise and the hum” a really quite beautiful ode to hip hop. I also go crazy for the turntablism over grinding white noise in the extended outro. “Antichristo” is a short interlude that’s just meh. “Hold Tight” has this digital industrial beat that just climbs and climbs, inducing much anxiety. It rules. I also love the melancholic synth under the verses and the crashing cymbals in the hook. It’s my favorite track up to this point. “Heads” is another short interlude, but this one feels more justified because it’s a precursor to the insanity of the next track.

I respect the hell out of “Black Smoke Rises” and don’t know that the album would be *better* per se without it, though it would certainly be much more listenable. The relentless feedback truly evokes *Metal Machine Music*, complemented perfectly by the distressed spoken word. But, man, is it tough to have this 12-minute sound bomb in the middle of a record. “Trampled Brethren” has a kind of George Harrison Indian feel to it, but I don’t think it works all that well, especially paired with the annoying voiceover. The drums are great, but it’s my least favorite track. “Voice of the Ether” has a unique beat for the album, more shuffling and dynamic than abrasive. It’s a good track with a catchy flow in the vocals. “Forever Close My Eyes” is fucking AMAZING and the best hip hop song I’ve discovered in quite a while. It’s as much a rock track as it is a hip hop track, sounding like if “Livery” by Divine Styler were a lot better. I love the 6/8 rhythm, which, combined with the fluttering metallic synths that come in and out, make everything sound swirling and ethereal. The chorus sounds wonderful, as do the guitar licks. And the ending must be the high point of the album. It’s just a phenomenal song. “Classical Homicide” closes things on a bit of a horrorcore vibe. And it certainly works. It’s another great track on a solid album.

I do have to ding this as an album for the various reasons I mentioned, but I liked it a lot. I’m placing it pretty high now, and I find it likely that I’ll revisit this one over time. I see great potential for it to rise in the way that *Relationship of Command* did.

Favorite tracks: Spiritual Healing, …From Mole Hills, Hold Tight, Voice of the Ether, Forever Close My Eyes, Classical Homicide

Least favorite track: Trampled Brethren
