# Write-up

See the above write-ups on the other three Bowie albums we’ve listened to for the story of his earlier career. Bowie released the single “Where Are We Now?” on his 66th birthday, January 8th, 2013. It was accompanied by the announcement of *The Next Day*, his 25th studio album and first in 10 years. In the time since his heart attack on stage in 2004, Bowie had retreated from the public eye, even turning down an offer to perform at the 2012 London Olympics. Bowie wanted complete secrecy while recording, forcing everyone who worked on the album to sign NDAs. Despite his extended absence and advancing age, the single was a success; it was his first to break into the top 10 of the UK Singles chart since the early 90s. *The Next Day* saw similar success upon its March 2013 release, becoming the fastest-selling album of the year to that point. Unlike Bowie’s previous output, *The Next Day* had a distinctly nostalgic tone. Bowie asked Tony Visconti, who had first worked on Bowie’s second album in 1969 and later produced albums including *Low* and *“Heroes”*, to produce it. Multiple tracks musically and lyrically reference older songs from Bowie’s glam, funk, Berlin, and new wave periods. Most strikingly, the album’s cover was a modified version of the cover of *“Heroes”* with Bowie’s face blocked by a white square. *The Next Day* received the best reviews for a Bowie project in decades, though critics noted its bleak atmosphere.

Even though Bowie’s music started looking backward, he remained as plugged into the musical zeitgeist as he ever had been. In 2013, he appeared on Arcade Fire’s “Reflektor” worked with LCD Soundsystem’s James Murphy on a remix single. Bowie became a noted fan of hip hop artists Death Grips and Kendrick Lamar around this time, particularly the latter’s 2015 album *To Pimp a Butterfly*. He would cite both as major influences on his final album. Bowie released the single “Sue (Or in a Season of Crime)”, an out-of-left-field venture into avant-garde jazz, in 2014. It featured “’Tis a Pity She Was a Whore” as its B side. In November 2015, Bowie released a cryptic, 10-minute track entitled simply “★” (pronounced “Blackstar”) that had developed out of a theme song he wrote for a TV show. It became the longest song to enter the Billboard Hot 100, a record broken by Tool in 2019 and again by André 3000 in 2023. Along with its accompanying music video, featuring a visibly aged Bowie as a preacher on a foreign planet and aliens using the likely corpse of Major Tom in rituals, “★” enthralled audiences and critics alike. The next month, Bowie released “Lazarus”, a grim jazz-rock number with opaque lyrics and a terrifying video. Bowie wrote the song for a musical of the same name which debuted in NYC in December 2015; this would be the final public appearance of his lifetime.

On Bowie’s 69th birthday, January 8, 2016, he released his 26th and final album *★*. In addition to its title track and “Lazarus”, the album included re-recorded versions of “Sue (Or in a Season of Crime)” and “’Tis a Pity She Was a Whore” with new arrangements, as well as three completely unreleased tracks. Shortly before recording the album, Bowie saw a jazz quartet play in Greenwich Village, where he was floored by the performance of tenor saxophonist Donny McCaslin, pianist Jason Lindner, bassist Tim Lefebvre, and drummer Mark Giuliana. He hired the quartet to perform as his backing band on *★*. Bowie and his band began recording the album in early 2015 under similar secrecy conditions as *The Next Day*. Guest performers included James Murphy (drums on “Sue” and “Girl Loves Me”) and jazz guitarist Ben Monder (guitar on “★” and “I Can’t Give Everything Away”). “Girl Loves Me” features lyrics written in a mixture of Nadsat (fictional language from *A Clockwork Orange*), Polari (British gay slang from the 70s), and English slang. The album’s closer, “I Can’t Give Everything Away”, includes a harmonica line strongly reminiscent of “A New Career in a New Town”, the last song on side A of *Low*. The album’s minimalist cover features a black star on a white background, with the name “Bowie” vaguely spelled out using star segments at the bottom. It’s the only Bowie album not to feature a photo or drawing of himself on the cover.

*★* received significant acclaim upon release, with many viewing it as a daring record for an artist so far into his career. Two days later, David Bowie died of liver cancer on January 10, 2016, at age 69. Unbeknownst to all but his closest friends and family, Bowie had been diagnosed with the disease in the summer of 2014. During *★*’s recording in early 2015, Bowie had been undergoing chemotherapy. He had entered remission after recording finished in the summer of 2015, but the cancer returned and was deemed terminal by the Fall. Bowie’s doctors informed him that they would end treatment the same week that the video for “Lazarus” was filmed. The combination of Bowie’s fame and the shocking timing of his death resulted in an unprecedented outpouring of mourning and tributes. People began reinterpreting the songs on *★* repeatedly, with the title track, “Lazarus”, “Dollar Days”, and “I Can’t Give Everything Away” containing lyrics that obviously reflected Bowie’s impending death in retrospect. Even before Bowie’s death, the album was poised to sell well; Billboard had already anticipated it would debut at the top spot on the Billboard 200 for the first time in his career. However, his death supercharged sales, resulting in 51,000 more copies sold than expected in its first week of release. “Lazarus” entered the top 40 of the Hot 100 more than a month after its release as a single. As Bowie says on “Dollar Days”, “I’m dying to push their backs against the grain/And fool them all again and again”; with *★*, he ended his life and career with one final twist, solidifying his legacy as one of the most unpredictable and inspired pop stars of all time.

# Hargrove's review

It’s hard for me to believe that I’m actually writing this review. On February 13, 2025, we started this chat and listened to *Pet Sounds* by the Beach Boys. In the 591 days since, we’ve toured through 318 albums, 239 of which I hadn’t heard before. Some have been amazing, some have been mid, some have been atrociously terrible. I’ve been introduced to sounds I never even knew existed, and I’ve been given the opportunity to solidify my opinions in writing on albums I had already heard a million times. This project has been a second full-time job for over a year-and-a-half. I genuinely can’t believe that it’s over. In some ways, I’m quite excited that it’s over; I’ll have so much more free time to work on other things, and it makes my schedule infinitely more flexible. In other ways, I’m deeply sad. This project has given me structure and discipline, inspiring me to improve my life in all kinds of unrelated ways once I proved to myself that I could consistently chip away at a massive goal little-by-little over time. And there’s still so much music I need to listen to! A few months ago, I mapped out an entire genre history of metal through just about every metal album I want to listen to. It’s hundreds upon hundreds! There’s so much left to experience, and so much left to say. Some of it I’ll get to, some of it I never will. No matter how hard I try, I can’t give everything away. But I’ll sure as hell give it everything I’ve got.

I strive to live my life the way David Bowie did. Has anyone ever lived a fuller life? From the art school underground, to novelty one-hit wonder, to failed pop star, to glam rock superstar, to spiraling drug addict, to Berlin seclusion and experimentation, to the brave new world of new wave, to history’s most tasteful sell-out decade, to a renewed interest in the underground and cutting edge musical styles, to health issues and privacy, to a career resurgence that reached new commercial and artistic heights, to a premature demise. Bowie might have preferred being second to being first, but he was second to such a breathtaking variety of things. He was a man who clearly loved life and desperately wanted to experience anything and everything that he could. And he died with dozens of unfinished projects, pleading with the universe to let him push our backs against the grain, fool us all again and again. If he never saw the English evergreens he was running to, what hope do any of us have?

I have an uncommon relationship with *★* on two levels. It was the first Bowie album I had ever listened to, which I have to imagine is unusual. Sure, I knew “Space Oddity” and “Starman” and “Changes” and “‘Heroes’” and “Let’s Dance”, but I still had never explored his music at all by age 15 when this album came out. I remember once when I was 12 or 13 telling my dad that Nirvana’s cover of “The Man Who Sold the World” was way, way better than Bowie’s version. He told me I was an idiot. In addition to *★* being my first Bowie album, I also belong to the relatively select group of people who listened to it before he died. It came out in my freshman year of high school; I didn’t yet have my driver’s license, so my dad would get up at 6 every day to drive me to 0 period band at my high school 25 minutes away. We would of course listen to music, and my dad put on the new Bowie record on Friday, January 8. Both of us were immediately transfixed by the 10-minute opener. What a strange track! Neither of us had ever heard anything like it. Bowie plays charismatic cult leader over a chaotic jazz band with Arabian undertones. We both immediately fell in love. The drive wasn’t long enough to finish the whole album, but the title track, “’Tis a Pity She Was a Whore”, and “Girl Loves Me” all stuck with me, as did “I Can’t Give Everything Away” once we had a chance to finish the album together the next day. It finally converted me to the cult of Bowie after years of my dad nagging me. And then came January 10. Something happened on the day he died. Spirit rose a meter and stepped to the side. But no one else took his place. No one else bravely cried, “I’m a blackstar”. I got to be a Bowie fan for two days before he disappeared forever. Ain’t that just like me?

I can’t believe it has been 10 years since the events of the previous paragraph. Where the fuck did Monday go? Since then, I’ve become a Bowie superfan. I’ve listened to all of his albums through the 80s, and will make it into the 90s and 00s someday. But there’s still something about *★* that draws me back again and again. Every listen to the opener “*★*” transports me to a different world, right into the center of the villa of Ormen. I love the frenetic drum beat, the Arabian influences, and the sax solos. But more than anything, I love Bowie’s performance. He inhabits the two sides of a sinister religious leader in both sections, performing rituals privately in the first before preaching charismatically to the public in the second. There’s such a thick, ominous, vivid foreignness to this track, intensified by the cryptic lyrics. The second section in particular features some of my favorites, like “Something happened on the day he died/Spirit rose a meter and stepped aside/Somebody else took his place, and bravely cried/I’m a blackstar, I'm a blackstar” and “I can't answer why (I'm a blackstar)/Just go with me (I'm not a filmstar)/I’ma take you home (I'm a blackstar)/Take your passport and shoes (I'm not a popstar)/And your sedatives, boo (I'm a blackstar)/You're a flash in the pan (I'm not a marvel star)/I’m the Great I Am (I'm a blackstar)”. This track serves as the perfect farewell to Bowie’s ability to construct different worlds in his music. “’Tis a Pity She Was a Whore” showcases his humor a bit more. The opening line “Man, she punched me like a dude” cracks me up every time, as does the title phrase. By the end, Bowie sounds absolutely deranged when he says it. I love the relentlessly propulsive backbeat in the drums and the chaotic, dueling horns. “Lazarus” opens with a guitar and drum sound that I would expect on some indie post-rock album with a 9.1 on Pitchfork. It’s crazy how well Bowie kept up with music, man. The saxophones wail with the utmost melancholy, while the distortion of the guitar cuts through everything else. Bowie delivers his most emotionally affecting vocal performance on the album. There are so many lines that cut me deep, like “Look up here, I'm in heaven/I’ve got scars that can't be seen/I’ve got drama, can't be stolen/Everybody knows me now” and “Look up here, man, I'm in danger/I’ve got nothing left to lose”. But its his echoing of perhaps my favorite Velvet Underground song “Candy Says” with “This way or no way, you know I'll be free/Just like that bluebird now, ain't that just like me?/Oh, I'll be free, just like that bluebird/Oh, I'll be free, ain't that just like me?” that gets me crying like a baby. Even once he’s done, the song continues to build in the instrumental. What a sax solo!

“Sue (Or in a Season of Crime)” is not a bad song by any measure, but it’s surely my least favorite on the album. While I like this arrangement better than the original single, the song itself just lacks a certain punch. I appreciate the breakneck pace and DnB stylings of the drums, but I just want more out of Bowie’s part, as well as more connection to the album’s themes. Still, it’s awesome to hear the track grow into such a calamity by the end. “Girl Loves Me” is a terrifying little song that perplexes me just as much today as it did the first time I heard it. Bowie sounds unhinged speaking in gibberish, and yet still delivers what may be the most memorable and powerful line on the album with “Where the fuck did Monday go?”. I love the strings in the bridge and the chorus. The album ends with the heartbreaking one-two punch of “Dollar Days” and “I Can’t Give Everything Away”. The former finds Bowie pleading and angry, desperate for a stay in execution. “I’m trying to/I’m dying to” right up to the very end. You get a taste of just how much Bowie enjoyed life and how badly he wanted to keep living. It kills me. On the other hand, I love hearing him say “I’m dying to push their backs against the grain/And fool them all again and again/I’m trying to”. I’m so glad he succeeded in doing so. Additionally, Bowie plays the acoustic guitar in such a beautiful way. What a gorgeous, devastating song. With “I Can’t Give Everything Away”, Bowie finds an uneasy acceptance of his fate. It’s not an affected acceptance; he hasn’t pulled the wool over his own eyes, soothing himself with sweet nothings as the end approaches. No, it’s a real acceptance, a bittersweet acknowledgement of the inevitable. No matter how hard he tried, he couldn’t do everything that he wanted to do, and that’s okay. He still said and did a whole awful lot. He’s found a new career in a new town, the great Berlin in the sky. With “I know something's very wrong/The pulse returns the prodigal sons”, the man who fell to Earth must leave it again.

There are few records I can think of that pack the emotional punch of *★*. Further, there are few records I can think of with the creative ambition or tight execution of *★*. It’s truly a miracle of a record. At the end of this project, it’s given me some solace as I ponder what my life will look like without it.

Favorite tracks: *★* [Blackstar], ’Tis a Pity She Was a Whore, Lazarus, Girl Loves Me, Dollar Days, I Can’t Give Everything Away

Least favorite track: Sue (Or in a Season of Crime)
