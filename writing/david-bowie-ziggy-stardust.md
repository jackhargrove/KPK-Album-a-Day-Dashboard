# Write-up

“It's not who does it first, it's who does it second.” - David Bowie

David Robert Jones was born and raised in London, England in 1947. He started his first band in 1962 at age 15, and from then on out, his only goal in life was to be an international music superstar. He spent most of the 60s trying to desperately to gain any recognition, releasing unsuccessful single after unsuccessful single under various names before finally settling on David Bowie. In 1967, at 20 years old, Bowie finally had the chance to release his debut album; it was a quite bad amalgam of popular British music at the time, and even with the context of his later career it’s not good. The album didn’t bring him any fame, and he took 2 years to better himself. He studied avant-garde theater and learned how to act as a mime (which led to him working with Marc Bolan of T. Rex), giving Bowie a lifelong interest in characters and personae. He developed his first persona, Major Tom, as part of the song “Space Oddity” in his next attempt at music stardom in 1969. The song was of course a major hit, coming out just before the Moon landing, but Bowie’s second album that followed shortly was mostly swill and contained no other hits. In 1970, Bowie completely switched up his sound to hard rock, releasing his third album *The Man Who Sold The World*. The album was a commercial failure at the time, but went on to be largely influential to goth rock as well as to grunge (it was Kurt Cobain’s favorite Bowie album) and is today considered his first good album. In 1971, Bowie completely switched his style once again, releasing the art pop *Hunky Dory*. Despite having two singles that would eventually be career-defining in “Changes” and “Life on Mars?”, Bowie’s label refused to promote the album, and it didn’t sell. After 10 years and 4 studio albums of chasing stardom and reinvention, Bowie had one novelty hit single to show for it. Would he forever remain a one-hit wonder?

While on tour in 1971, a stroke of inspiration hit Bowie: he wanted to create a character that combined the music of Lou Reed with the image of Iggy Pop, and he would name the character Ziggy (very original). Inspired by Marc Bolan of T. Rex, Bowie decided that this character would wear lots of makeup, have crazy hair, and wear loud clothing. Further, Ziggy Stardust would be a bisexual alien, and his backing band (Mick Ronson on guitar, Trevor Bolder on bass, and Mick Woodmansey on drums) would be called the Spiders from Mars. Bowie debuted the character in live performance in 1972 to immediate success, generating a cult of fans who would follow him around. That same year, he recorded *The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars*, a loose concept album in which humanity discovers Earth will be destroyed in 5 years, and the alien Ziggy Stardust comes from outer space to inspire hope by becoming a rock star. The album, especially the single “Starman”, was a huge success, and finally gave Bowie the stardom he had always craved. Bowie retired the Ziggy Stardust character in 1973 at the end of a live performance in London.

*The Rise and Fall of Ziggy Stardust and the Spiders from Mars* is one of the defining albums of glam rock, a type of pop rock that is both bluesy and extremely theatrical. Glam rock was a reaction to the serious and anti-commercial nature of prog rock, as well as the austere socialist aesthetics popular in the late 60s. While early glam rock artists like Bowie, T. Rex, Lou Reed, and Brian Eno are well-regarded, glam would go on for a bit too long, and by the late 70s was decidedly uncool. Bowie would release two more albums in the glam style, 1973’s *Aladdin Sane* (“Ziggy Stardust goes to America”) and 1974’s *Diamond Dogs*, before bidding the genre farewell. By 1975, Bowie was a funk and soul artist, and by 1977 Bowie was making weird avant-pop and ambient music with Eno in Berlin.

# Hargrove's review

I really like this record (shocker). As I said to Carms earlier, this is just a perfect pop record. The hits like “Starman”, “Moonage Daydream”, and “Ziggy Stardust” are tight 3-4 minute pop rock masterpieces with iconic guitar riffs, catchy hooks, a charismatic vocalist, focused and thematic lyrics, grand but subtle string arrangements, you name it. But what really sets this album apart is how consistently good it is, even outside of the hits. The album cuts are NOT an afterthought. I love the balladry of “Lady Stardust”, the stop and start of “Star”, the not-quite-punk of “Suffragette City”, the driving pace of “Hang on to Yourself”, the relaxed beauty of “Soul Love”, the unabashed theatricality of “Five Years”, Bowie’s voice breaking at the climax of “Rock ’n’ Roll Suicide”; so much care, so much passion, so much creativity was put into this record. Aesthetically, I can’t get enough of how much Bowie *wants* it. How hard is clearly trying. He never acts above it, he never tries to convince you that he’s cool, he wants to be great and he doesn’t care who knows. There are so many great musicians who love to be the slacker, who want you to think they don’t care about anything, who think trying is anathema to culture. When it works it works, but I’m so drawn to Bowie because he put everything he had into his music, never wasting a drop of his ingenuity to convince you that he didn’t (the final song on his final album is titled “I Can’t Give Everything Away” ☹️). This is why I think I like Bowie so much, whereas I have a visceral negative reaction toward Queen or certain prog and metal bands. It’s a thin line, but a lot of them try to convince you how whacky or dramatic or erudite they naturally are, while Bowie drops that pretense and pleads with you directly (my evidence for this is that someone who is genuinely singular grows restless, and Freddie Mercury never updated his persona while Bowie couldn’t keep one persona for more than a year). This dedication, this desperation, seeps into every nook, cranny, and crevice of this album. Truly beautiful.

Taking it from the top, “Five Years” sets the stage like the opening of a musical, fading in with just drums at first, then bass, then piano, then strings, all crescendoing slowly over 5 minutes. All the while, Bowie is delivering some of the most beautifully humanist lyrics I’ve ever heard as a he grows to a scream, describing the beauty of our world as he tells us it will all disappear in 5 years. He really does sound like an alien not only observing but falling in love with Earth and humanity, and “I think I saw you in an ice cream parlor/Drinking milkshakes cold and long/Smiling and waving and looking so fine/Don’t think you knew you were in this song” is one of my favorite lines about nonchalance in the face of events beyond your control. An incredible album-opener, a song that contains the multitudes of the entire album in less than 5 minutes. “Soul Love” is downtempo and tender, and it was one of the songs that took me the longest to fall in love with. But the vocal melody in the verses is so pretty, the random bars of 3/4 keep you on your toes, the saxophone solo is killer, and the choruses are explosive. “Moonage Daydream” is shockingly heavy by comparison, with an opening guitar riff taken straight from Black Sabbath. I hang onto every last word that Bowie says as he sings with phenomenal delivery, and the backing vocals in the chorus are great too. And those strings are subtle but they’re there, injecting a healthy dose of melodrama into the song. One of his best hits. Speaking of hits, next up is “Starman”, which is probably Bowie’s most well-known solo song. The repeated vowels at the ends of lines give the vocals so much personality, the two bars of quiet before the chorus is a stroke of genius in build and release, the chorus is catchy as hell, the strings are there doing their thing, and the guitar riff after the chorus is massive. This song deserves its stature for sure. It’s followed by “It Ain’t Easy”, which is unfortunately my least favorite track. It’s not terrible, but it sounds less fresh than everything else, and kills some of the momentum that’s been built.

“Lady Stardust” is one of my favorite album cuts, its cheesy on the surface but that dark chord change on “Yes he was awful nice” in the chorus is interesting, and the way Bowie stretches his voice on “Really quite outta sight” sells the song for me. I love the frantic piano of “Star”, and the stop and start doesn’t let you take a breath. The backing vocals are some of my favorite on the album, and the lyrics are a mission statement for Bowie’s hopes and dreams. Great momentum builder. “Hang on to Yourself” starts out hot, and Bowie adopts this cool as hell talkative cadence (even laughing in the second verse). We’ve really built back up since things slowed down in “It Ain’t Easy”, and this pacing adds to the theatricality of it all. “Ziggy Stardust” announces itself with an amazing guitar line and the promise that we’re going to learn some more about our title character. Let’s just say, he has a *shocking* number of similarities to Bowie (left-handed, great guitarist, crazy haircut, big cock, great ass). The chorus though, which switches to the perspective of the Spiders from Mars, is surprisingly harsh and heavy. Gives this unexpected sinister mood to the whole thing, and lets you know things won’t end well for Ziggy. “Making love with his ego/Ziggy sucked up into his mind/Like a leper messiah” are really peak lyrics for Bowie. “Suffragette City” is a driving hard rock song that I really like, but that does feel a little out of place on the album, especially between the climax of “Ziggy Stardust” and the denouement of “Rock ’n’ Roll Suicide”. Nevertheless, I enjoy the lyrics, and Bowie is clearly having fun living in the world of his influences. “Rock ’n’ Roll Suicide” is the counterpart to “Five Years”, an absolutely perfect closer that has everything that makes the whole album great. Bowie sounds wistful and defeated in the opening. This regret turns into advice quickly though as the instrumental picks up. The bridge is one of the finest moments of this album, with the voice crack on “I’ve had my share, I’ll help you with the pain/You’re not alone” stabbing me like a knife through the heart every time I hear it. Masterful.

This is obviously S tier for me. The question is would I put it above *Low* or not, which is also S tier. I actually like *Low* even more than I did when we listened to it originally, and at the time it had been a while since I had listened to Ziggy. It’s a really tough call though. I’ve been alternating both for the last 4 or so hours, and I’m really stumped. I think for now, I’m going to put it just below *Low*, but I will continue to ponder.

Favorite tracks: Five Years, Soul Love, Moonage Daydream, Starman, Lady Stardust, Star, Hang on to Yourself, Ziggy Stardust, Suffragette City, Rock ’n’ Roll Suicide

Least favorite track: It Ain’t Easy
