# Write-up

De La Soul (henceforth referred to as “DLS”) was a hip hop trio formed in 1988 in Long Island consisting of Trugoy the Dove (MC, real name David Jolicoeur), Posdnuos (MC, real name Kelvin Mercer), and Maseo (DJ, real name Vincent Mason). They are widely considered the progenitors of alternative and abstract hip hop, and this album is one of only three hip hop albums from the 1980s on our list. In a time when rappers almost exclusively told stories about life on the street, DLS were among the first to instead talk about lighter, sillier topics. The three members of DLS met in high school in Amityville, NY, as well as their future producer Prince Paul (real name Paul Huston). They became founding members of the Native Tongues collective in NYC, one of the most influential hip hop organizations of all time. Native Tongues was a loose collection of hip hop artists and groups who favored samples from jazz music and positive, uplifting lyrics. It included DLS, A Tribe Called Quest, Queen Latifah, and Busta Rhymes, among others. Native Tongues was heavily associated with the Universal Zulu Nation, a worldwide group led by pioneering 70s DJ Afrika Bambaata (one of the inventors of hip hop) that steered young black kids away from crime and into hip hop. Universal Zulu Nation also had some cult-like qualities, and much of its legacy was marred by the 2016 revelation that Bambaata had used his position to molest hundreds of young boys.

*3 Feet High and Rising* was DLS’ debut studio album, released in 1989. The title is a play on the ohnny Cash song “5 Feet High and Rising”. It came out just two weeks after NWA’s seminal West Coast gangsta rap record *Straight Outta Compton*, an album that was much more reflective of the violent and braggadocios style popular in hip hop at the time. On the other side of things, the group Public Enemy had made a name for themselves in the underground as aggressive and political warriors. DLS, by contrast, espoused the concept of the D.A.I.S.Y. Age, meaning “Da Inner Sound, Y’all”. Their lyrics were lighthearted and fun. They sampled from many more types of music than other hip hop acts. There were flowers on the album cover. All of this got DLS branded as “hippies”, a label they did not care for. It also helped them break through into the white world of critics, who were largely fans of the record. It was hugely influential, inventing the concept of skits in a hip hop album (Oppenheimer moment fr) and getting added to the Library of Congress in 2010. In retrospect, its critical stature has only grown, and it is commonly listed as one of the best albums of all time in any genre.

DLS went on to have a long career with many albums that were both commercially and critically successful but that are often overshadowed by the stature of their debut. They very quickly moved on to a more mature and serious style of rapping, but they’ll always be thought of as hippies. They would collaborate with many hip hop artists on our list, including J Dilla, Wu-Tang Clan, MF DOOM, Madlib, Nas, and Mos Def. They would also collaborate with non-hip hop artists on our list, such as indie rock band Modest Mouse with whom they went on tour in 2002. Their most famous collaboration was as the rappers on the 2005 smash-hit Gorillaz song “Feel Good, Inc.” for which they won a Grammy. They also founded the Spitkicker collective in 2000, which helped launch the career of comedian Dave Chappelle. DLS was not on streaming for a long time because of a quirk of their contracts that only cleared their samples for physical distribution. After a long legal struggle, DLS was finally added to streaming in 2023. Earlier that year, member Trugoy the Dove died at age 54.

# Hargrove's review

When we were driving to Green Key, I was playing a song from A Tribe Called Quest and McGary said “this is the definition of corny hip hop”. Now, I love A Tribe Called Quest, and McGary is dead wrong and I’ll hate him for it forever. But, his sentiment does kind of describe how I feel about A Tribe Called Quest’s brother group, De La Soul. I hate using the word “corny” because I think it’s a little unfair, but I’m just a bit too cynical to get into music this whimsical. This is perhaps a fault on my end, but it’s the truth. A Tribe Called Quest is generally positive and Afrocentric as well, but Tip and Phife keep just the perfect amount of edge to balance it out. They can be a little mean, they can get a little melancholy when they get personal, they can express their frustrations with the world. Furthermore, Tribe’s jazz rap beats are just better. They’re one of my favorite music groups of all time, so it’s not fair. But they’re just so directly comparable. I really tried to get into De La Soul last summer. I would listen to this album every day at my internship. It has some undeniable bangers. Unfortunately, it just doesn’t resonate with me. I feel like sometimes they’re so whimsical, they’re so “themselves”, that it actually prevents me from relating to Pos and Trugoy as individuals. The beats are very primitive and simple. Sometimes this is a good thing, but often it means the tracks don’t differentiate themselves too much. The verses also suffer from age, with limited creativity in the flow. Combined with the hard-and-fast structure of the major tracks, this album can get dreary quickly. I completely understand why people love this album. I get why it’s acclaimed. It’s just not for me.

“(3 Is) The Magic Number” is a banger and one of the best tracks. It kinda sounds like children’s music, but man does it go hard. I’m also a big fan of the turntablism on display, particularly in the outro. It feels like a lot of the good things about old-school hip hop. “Jenifa Taught Me (Derwin’s Revenge)” is another track I really like. The drums are particularly fantastic, but the instrumental in general is good. I love the “Jenifa, oh, Jenny” refrain as the beat drops out. And the chopsticks interlude is one of my favorite moments of whimsy. “Ghetto Thang” has a great hook and some of the best verses on the album. I love the bass line that’s all over the place. Q-Tip easiy clears everyone else in “Buddy”, but all the verses are good. The bass sample is also stellar. “Me Myself and I” has a similar feel to a lot of the mid tracks, but it’s just a little more excellent in its execution. The synth sample under the hook rocks. “This is a Recording 4 Living in a Fulltime ERA (L.I.F.E.)” has meh verses (what are you gonna do) but one of the best hooks and a cool bass sound. “D.A.I.S.Y. Age” randomly has some of the best drums I’ve ever heard on a hip hop song. That hit on the e of 4 is phenomenal every time.

Those are the songs that I liked. Everything else can be categorized as either mid hip hop or dumb quirky interlude. There are many of the former in the middle of the album and it becomes a real slog. So many tracks with the same structure, the same sampled hooks, the same flow on the unmemorable verses, the same types of drum beats. It gets old fast. The dumb quirky interludes are more evenly spaced, but I consistently find them grating. Quirk overload. I wish my heart were not so dark, but alas.

I am glad that there are so many people who love this album, and there are a few tracks that are bangers on my playlists, but there’s really no reason for me to listen to this album when I could be listening to A Tribe Called Quest instead.

Favorite tracks: (3 Is) The Magic Number, Jenifa Taught Me (Derwin’s Revenge), Ghetto Thang, Buddy, Me Myself and I, This is a Recording 4 Living in a Fulltime ERA (L.I.F.E.), D.A.I.S.Y. Age

Least favorite track: Tread Water
