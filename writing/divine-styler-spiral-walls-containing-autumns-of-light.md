# Write-up

Divine Styler (real name Mark Richardson) is a rapper from Brooklyn who was born in 1968. His father was a DJ who often worked with Larry Levan, one of the pioneers of house music. As an adolescent, Divine Styler got caught up in crime, so his family sent him away to finish school in Los Angeles, where he met a pre-fame Ice-T. Though he does not have any albums on this list, Ice-T was a major figure in the development of hip hop. He released some of the earliest West Coast gangsta rap music in the late 80s, and then in the 90s pioneered the fusion of rap and metal. You might now recognize him as one of the main cops on *Law and Order: Special Victims Unit*. Anyway, when Ice-T hit it big, he signed Divine Styler to his independent label Rhyme Syndicate. Before he could release an album, however, Divine Styler did a short stint in prison after violating his probation. While inside, he had some kind of epiphany that inspired him to abandon violence in favor of spiritual pro-blackness. He released his first album *Word Power* in 1989. By all accounts, it’s a perfectly normal hip hop record from this era. It sold poorly, but received good reviews. The horn from his single “Ain’t Sayin’ Nothin’” was later used by House of Pain for their smash hit “Jump Around”. He was informed that he would be moved from Ice-T’s independent label to the major label Giant, a subsidiary of Warner Brothers, against his will. He decided to give them something weird.

In 1992, he released his second album and major label debut *Spiral Walls Containing Autumns of Light*. It was...not a perfectly normal hip hop record from this era. It blends hip hop, funk, and electronic into a wildly experimental psychedelic rock. He cites Jimi Hendrix as his primary inspiration for learning guitar just a year before making this record. Divine Styler produced, arranged, wrote, and played all of the music by himself. He had just converted to Sunni Islam and many of the lyric’s describe the joy he finds in Allah. Needless to say, the record was a complete failure commercially and critically. Divine Styler dropped off the face of the earth not long after, intending to continue his spiritual journey. By the late 90s, however, weirdo music guys had found the album and thought it was pretty interesting. It even got a rave retrospective review from AllMusic. One teenage fan from Canada began a magazine in 1996 called *In Search of Divine Styler* trying to find the man behind the music. He has turned up a couple of times since, releasing a third (more normal) album in 1999 and a fourth in 2014. He has also worked with DJ Shadow as a featured artist. He is remembered today by artists from the West Coast scene of the 90s as a respectable oddball.

# Hargrove's review

This album is dreadful, and not at least in an interesting way like I hoped it would be. I read about it a couple months ago and got pretty excited that it would be either a weirdo experimental hip hop gem or a beautiful train wreck. Instead, it’s just…kinda bad and kinda bland? In the sonic sense, this album is only experimental insofar as this style was unexpected given its context within early 90s West Coast hip hop. The psychedelic rock and psychedelic funk sounds had already been taken into some pretty wild directions in the 60s and 70s. I will concede that the few songs that are genuinely hip hop and genuinely abrasive were novel and anticipatory, but they have been done so much better in the interceding 30 years that there’s very little reason to seek out that sound here. For the most part, this album is only still sonically interesting in that it incorporates such a broad and diverse array of genres. The style changes completely from one song to the next. But the actual songs are just unremarkable within their respective genres. It’s kinda cool that “Width in My Depth” is a folk song, but it’s like the 5,000th best folk song I’ve heard in my life. It’s kinda cool that “The Next” sounds like The Doors, but, uh, The Doors are better. Lyrically, on the other hand, this album is actually experimental. I don’t know what the fuck he’s saying, like, ever. But that right there is the issue! This album has absolutely nothing interesting to say, The vast majority of the lyrics are complete gibberish, completely indecipherable phrases that are often mispronounced or stumbled over anyway. It’s so frustrating that an album where a serious weirdo had full creative control to say whatever he wanted just says nothing. You guys know I don’t usually like to talk like this, but this album was a complete waste of my time. Five and a half hours across five listens down the drain. There wasn’t a single song I added to my liked songs playlist, there isn’t any real influence I’ve noticed from the album, and the story isn’t even all that interesting. Disappointing, disappointing, disappointing

“Am I an Epigram for Life?” is complete gibberish spoken word. “Touch” is kinda cool. I’m curious as to what scale the synth melody is using because it’s very odd sounding. The second half of this track is the only one where I think the Death Grips comparison is remotely apt. “In a World of U” is also not terrible. I like the driving, almost motorik drum beat and the jamming. My issue is that it adds nothing to the psychedelic rock canon. “Love, Lies and Lifetime’s Cries” is complete gibberish spoken word. “Livery” is by far the best track on the album and the only one I even considered adding to my liked songs playlist. Those drums are funky as hell and the chorus is quite catchy. It’s a decent funk song. “Grey Matter” is the most normal sounding hip hop song. It kind of sounds like a bad De La Soul song, and I already don’t love De La Soul. If I heard this playing out in the wild somewhere, I would assume it was some 90s backpack rap deep cut. “Heaven Don’t Want Me and Hell’s Afraid I’ll Take Over” is complete gibberish spoken word, but it’s also 9 minutes long, making it my least favorite. “Mystic Sheep Drink Electric Tea” is the song people most commonly cite as anticipating Death Grips, but I really don’t hear it. Yeah, he’s yelling, but there’s no art to it, no sense of paranoia or anger instilled in me. It’s just kind of sad. “Width in My Depth” has the only line that was intriguing to me in any way: “Why should I deserve me?”. Otherwise, bad folk song. “The Next” is a bad The Doors song. “Euphoric Rangers” somehow sounds like filler, even on this album. “Walk of Exodus” isn’t a terrible psych rock song. It would be on my favorite tracks list if he cut it down from eight minutes to four minutes. “Aura” is complete gibberish spoken word.

It’s kind of tough to rank this one because it is at least far more listenable than something like The Pop Group, Sun City Girls, or Royal Trux. I don’t find the sound actively offensive. But it’s so uninteresting to me that it’s almost just as bad. To be frank, I’d probably rather listen to Captain Beefheart than this. That album is at least way more fun.

Favorite tracks: Touch, In a World of U, Livery, Grey Matter

Least favorite track: Heaven Don’t Want Me and Hell’s Afraid I’ll Take Over
