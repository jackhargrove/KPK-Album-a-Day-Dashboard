# Write-up

DJ Shadow (real name Josh Davis) is a hip hop DJ from Davis, California, a satellite city of Sacramento. He began making beats as a high schooler in the late 80s and became a DJ for UC Davis’ student radio station (my dad and I used to listen to this station) in the early 90s. He began to release remixes and singles around this time that explored a similar vibe t artists on Mo’ Wax Records in the UK that would eventually be described as “trip hop”. DJ Shadow’s music was entirely sample-based; he would spend hours every day in record stores finding obscure records to take elements from for his music. He released the single “In/Flux” in 1993 which became a hit club record in the UK and was the song that inspired a music journalist to coin “trip hop”. It resulted in Mo’ Wax signing DJ Shadow, but he remained in the Sacramento area where he created his own independent label Solesides with local artists Blackalicious (duo of Chief Xcel and Gift of Gab) and Lyrics Born. Shadow and the other Solesides members would spend all their time at Rare Records in Sacramento (a shop my dad wants me to say he went to a lot), and the cover of *Endtroducing*… is a picture of Chief Xcel and Lyrics Born shopping there.

DJ Shadow released his debut album *Endtroducing…* in 1996. Every second of it, from beginning to end, ismade up of samples of existing records. It was an early popular example of plunderphonics, a kind of process music where the process is complete sampling. Shadow made the entire album by himself primarily using an analog sampler, but he also borrowed an early version of Pro Tools owned by his friend Dan the Automator, who would go on to be one-third of Deltron 3030. The artists sampled ranged from totally obscure to Björk, Metallica, and Nirvana, but Shadow had one rule for himself: none of the samples could be at all recognizable. The album received critical acclaim, and was also a huge commercial success…but only in the UK at first. It reached #17 on the UK albums chart and made Shadow think he was a big star when he toured there. However, when he returned to California, no one knew who he was, and he became depressed. Over time, his reputation in the US grew, and it is now considered one of the best albums of all time on this side of the pond as well.

Shadow produced the debut album of UNKLE, the third-best British trip hop group, in 1998. He has consistently released music since the success of *Endtroducing…*, but he has never reached that success again. He is commonly cited by music nerds as an example of an artist with an amazing debut and no other good albums (Fantano included him in a short on this just two days ago). However, his stature has allowed him to continue collaborating with artists like Thom Yorke, Nas, De La Soul, Ghostface Killah, and Deftones. Some have theorized that Shadow’s downfall was his own success; the sound of *Endtroducing…* proved so influential and ubiquitous that his future music no longer sounded fresh. Radiohead have cited it as a major influence on their seminal 1997 album *OK Computer*. It was listed in the 2001 *Guinness Book of World Records* as the “First Completely Sampled Album”. Shadow now lives in San Francisco and has one of the largest private vinyl record collections in the world at over 60,000 records.

# Hargrove's review

One of my dad’s all-time favorite records and one of the first new records I listened to when I was really getting into music back in 2017. Though I’ve listened to “Building Steam with a Grain of Salt” and “Midnight in a Perfect World” consistently over the last eight years, I can’t remember the last time I listened to this album in full. It’s been a long, long time. I’ve always had the general sense that those two songs are much better than anything else on the album, so I appreciate this opportunity to go back and give a deeper listen to the deeper cuts. And yeah, this is a pretty damn good album. The world is right on this one. It’s incredible how diverse the songs are given that they’re all more or less in the same style and made using the same process. The songs  be hazy, smooth and nocturnal, or upbeat, sunny, and jazzy, or fast, frantic, and restless. There are two through lines that pull this album into a remarkably unified piece. For one, no matter the type of atmosphere, every atmosphere is damn good. There’s just perfect execution on this album. The drums breaks chosen are impeccable, the layering and building are done with great care, and the moods are never half-assed. Being a track on *Endtroducing…* comes with a kind of a stamp of quality. And secondly, there’s the process. To be clear, even if this album were constructed normally, or even if I didn’t know everything was sampled, it would still be very good. But I think creative constraints often result in interesting art, and knowing what those constraints are can help add an additional thematic layer. Indeterminancy is a genre I often think of when it comes to process music. It’s a form of modern classical where the music is composed with a random factor. For example, Terry Riley’s *In C* is a cool composition where every musician is told to play their part at their own tempo. It makes every performance unique, and the ideas of individuals as part of a collective and impermanence reflect the post-modern sentiments of composers of that era. Here, with plunderphonics as the process genre, DJ Shadow picks up little discrete ideas he’s heard and pulls them into something entirely new. One might think of the pursuit of making art as creating something out of nothing, but here DJ Shadow makes something out of many somethings. It’s a style that plays with our ideas of ownership, the history and culture of hip hop, and how to transition from art fan to artist. I think it’s neat.

Usually for an album of this scope I would pick out my favorite songs and ignore the rest, but I like so many of them that I might as well just write about each track. “Best Foot Forward” is just an intro, but it does such a good job of showcasing and introducing the central idea that it’s a favorite track of mine. I also love the way that Shadow speaks through the voices of others. “Building Steam with a Grain of Salt” is a banger and a great choice for first full track. The piano is so ominous, and it’s intensified by the sparkling eight notes, intensified by the drums, intensified by the choir, intensified by the bass, all coming in one after the other to build a vibey monster. It’s haunting. It still adds some moving parts, like the Squarepusher-esque drums on the middle, but honestly the atmosphere of the core instrumental is strong enough to sustain seven minutes on its own. “The Number Song” takes things to a much more upbeat place with one of my favorite drum breaks on the album. I also like the complete shake up in the middle. “Changeling” is another great track. The bass line alone carries it, but on top of that the guitar and the sax make it so grimy, dirty, and nocturnal. “Transmission 1” is fine. I think the transmission conceit doesn’t add much, but whatever, it ties things together. “What Does Your Soul Look Like, Pt. 4” is the first composition I’m not crazy about. The vibe is fine but it’s not unique on this album (“Changeling” and even “Building Steam…” come to mind) and it’s too slow for my taste. “Untitled” is a nice skit-like thing that keeps the mood light. I enjoy the playfulness on this album, it again feels like Shadow showing his personality through samples of others.

“Stem / Long Stem - Medley” is the best track I’ve “rediscovered” in these listens. It’s so, so, so, so good. It twinkles like a dark forest. It’s honestly kind of a scary song. The “parking tickets” spoken word bit is schizo sounding too. It’s all post-apocalyptic. And the horns at the end. Devastating atmosphere, and it almost transcends the form of hip hop. “Transmission 2” is the best transmission because it’s teasing the best song. A taste of an idyllic world right after a song showing such a broken world. “Mutual Slump” is a jarring transition and the drums break my vibe a bit too much. Not terrible, but my least favorite. “Organ Donor” is short but sweet. Hearing that organ loop over a breakbeat is really cool. I like it. “Why Hip Hop Sucks in ’96” is the same idea as “Untitled” and I have the same take. “Midnight in a Perfect World” is still the best track by far. One of the best tracks in all of hip hop. It’s dreamy, ethereal, evocative of a better world, but even then there’s something heartbreaking: the piano sounds like a spiral staircase to nowhere. Beautiful. The first half of “Napalm Brain / Scatter Brain - Medley” doesn’t do a ton for me, but I do really like the EDM jungle second half. “What Does Your Soul Look Like, Pt. 1” is much better than Pt. 4 and a good end to the album. That slow beat just works so much better with the sunny vibes. “Transmission 3” is certainly another transmission.

This is a very good album. I think it sets out what it tries to do perfectly. It’s a shame DJ Shadow could never pull it off again, but we’ll always have *Endtroducing…*

Favorite tracks: Best Foot Forward, Building Steam with a Grain of Salt, The Number Song, Changeling, Stem / Long Stem - Medley, Organ Donor, Midnight in a Perfect World, What Does Your Soul Look Like, Pt. 1

Least favorite track: Mutual Slump

# Aidan's review

I had only heard Gathering Steam with a Grain of Salt going into this. Obviously an excellent track, and the album repeats a lot of what makes that track good, the smooth looped instrumentals and the excellent sample choice. I want to highlight the non musical samples, the Xanadu and Murray Roman samples in Stem/Long Stem Medley and Mutual Slump are so diverse yet fit so perfectly. The piano in particular just glides over the samples in an incredibly pleasing way. I do find the harshness in the way some of the samples are stuttered grating, the end of Midnight In A Perfect World is a good example, it's just too drawn out for my taste when compared to the rest of the song. Still a nice album, it just oscillates a bit too much between styles for me to love it.
