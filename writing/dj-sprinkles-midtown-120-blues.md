# Write-up

DJ Sprinkles is one of the various names that musician Terre Thaemlitz (who uses “alternating he/she” pronouns, which I’m going to comply with if for no other reason than it’s funny) has released music under. He was born in 1968 and raised in Missouri, where she found the rock and country culture around him oppressive and instead turned to the new genre of electronic music. She moved to Manhattan in 1986 to study fine arts at Cooper Union and began to DJ under the name DJ Sprinkles. He became a resident DJ at the transsexual club Sally’s II in Midtown in 1991. Her primary style was deep house, a subgenre of house music that incorporates more human elements from funk and soul in a return to house’s disco roots, but he has also explored a wide range of experimental music genres. She has had a long and acclaimed career as a DJ, first in New York from 1980s-1997, then in Oakland, California from 1997-2001, and finally in Japan since 2001. He finally released a full-length album as DJ Sprinkles in 2008, titled *Midtown 120 Blues*. It received rave reviews at the time and is today considered perhaps the single-best full album example of deep house, a style historically associated with singles and live performance. It features multiple spoken word reflections on the history of deep house from Thaemlitz, highlighting its origins in the black and Latino LGBT ballrooms (see: *Paris Is Burning* (1990)) of New York and Chicago in the 80s and decrying its commercialization over the years. She singles out Madonna for criticism over her 1990 hit single “Vogue”, one of the first mainstream house hits named after a popular dancing style among drag queens at ballrooms. Topics like this are a frequent subject for Thaemlitz, who is a prolific essayist in addition to his music. She commonly writes on political and social justice topics from a radical and Marxist perspective. He has since released another album as DJ Sprinkles, 2022’s *Incomplete Insight (2012-2015)*, as well as numerous records under his real name. Unfortunately, her music is extremely difficult for us Zoomers to access because he only releases via physical media and habitually removes illegal uploads from YouTube and other sites.

Because this list has a glaring lack of albums representing early electronic styles, this record with a focus on music history is a great opportunity to discuss. Almost all modern popular dance music descends from disco, so it’s worthwhile to understand disco’s origins. Disco stems from soul music, an umbrella term for popular music made by black musicians in the 1960s that took influence from older black styles like gospel and rhythm & blues. Because of their shared descent from rhythm & blues, soul and early rock generally shares instrumentation (electric guitar, bass, drum set, piano/keyboard), but soul also often incorporated horns (from jazz) and had a heavy focus on vocals (from gospel). Disco specifically evolved from Philly soul, a commercial genre with lush arrangements that often brought in brass and strings. Disco also took heavy influence from funk, a late 60s/early 70s outgrowth of soul founded by James Brown that placed danceability, groove, and rhythm over melody. Disco coalesced as a style by the mid-1970s in NYC as the preferred genre played by DJs at dance clubs known as discotheques, a French term for European nightclubs that gave disco its name. By the second half of the 70s, disco had become a dominant style in American popular music. It also became huge in Europe, where dance music has always reigned supreme. And while American disco provided the “D” in EDM, the Europeans had spent the past decade developing the “E”. Throughout the 70s, German groups like Kraftwerk and Tangerine Dream experimented with new electronic instruments like the synthesizer and drum machines. Giorgio Moroder, an Italian electronic pioneer working in Germany, took an interest in disco and set out to introduce electronic instruments to its sound. As the producer for Donna Summer, an established disco artist, Moroder created the first ever style of electronic dance music: electro-disco.

After dominating the American charts for years, disco suddenly faced intense backlash in 1979. Famously, the Chicago White Sox hosted a Disco Demolition Night that broke out into a riot. The dance scene in America was forced underground as new wave and synthpop artists conquered popular music. DJs still played disco and its variants (like boogie and hi-NRG), but largely in isolated clubs frequented by gender, sexual, and racial minorities in major American cities. As a result, numerous local scenes developed, and EDM exploded into a genre with many offshoots. The most consequential early style of EDM in the early 80s was known as “house”, named for The Warehouse nightclub in Chicago where DJ Frankie Knuckles played music. House kept the persistent four-on-the-floor bass drum pattern and medium-fast tempo (around 120 BPM) of disco, but, to make it more efficient as dance music, it focused on relentless repetition, sparser arrangements, and a heavier bass line. Though Chicago is usually acknowledged as the birthplace of house, DJ Larry Levan at the Paradise Garage in New York developed a similar style except that it kept more of the human elements from soul. This genre is known as “garage house”. Deep house, a similar style to garage, developed out of Chicago house by the late 80s. Detroit’s version of house took heavy influence from a style called electro and became techno. By the 90s, house broke into the mainstream and influenced pop. Also in the 90s, a retro house scene developed in France called French house and birthed superstars Daft Punk. Though many other styles of EDM have developed, house is still one of the most popular.

# Hargrove's review

Damn, this album is pretty good! I think I called Sd Laika last week my favorite EDM record so far, but this is definitely better. It’s interesting because a big part of what I liked about Sd Laika was how mechanical, harsh, and cold it was, but what I like about *Midtown 120 Blues* is how human, inviting, and warm it feels. I’ve been slow to understand what it is that I actually like and do not like about EDM, and I think I’m slowly honing in on the extremes: full human or full robot. I get put off by the half-assed implying-human-beauty-through-robot-sounds stuff (NOTE: it will be interesting when we get to Daft Punk’s *Discovery* because I’ve loved that album for a long time and I’m not exactly sure how it fits into my analysis here). Either give me a weird spazzy grinding abrasive metallic experimental piece, or give me a lush heartfelt warm pulsating danceable piece. This album fits the latter better than any album we’ve heard so far. The formula is very simple: a synth line, a vocal sample, a snare pattern, and, of course, that incessant bass drum. This album rarely deviates. But it just works. It makes me want to dance as I’m lying in my bed listening to it. I also appreciate how focused the thematic elements are. The spoken word elements are interesting and well-written, many of the track titles feel apt, and “Grand Central, Pt. II (72 Hrs. by Rail from Missouri)” does a better job at evoking an atmosphere and emotion than most electronic music I’ve heard. My biggest issue is the pacing. I think the middle third of the album is too chill. I would’ve loved more variety there and I think a different track order could have helped. My other issue is that as much as I did like this, there are just other genres I have a bias toward where a great record will mean more to me. But I thoroughly enjoyed listening to this album.

“Midtown 120 Intro” is really well done. The story about him not getting into a club when they were playing one of her records is pretty funny, and the background music is genuinely quite stylish. It’s a good thesis statement. “Midtown 120 Blues” is just a banger. It’s got funky percussion, an icy keyboard with great sustain, a good vocal sample, it’s just a great track. “Ball’r (Madonna-Free Zone)” starts the stretch that’s too laid back for me. There are still good tracks and moments in this stretch, but I wish it had been broken up. This track in particular is not my favorite though and I think Madonna deserves a little more grace and charity. “Brenda’s $20 Dilemma” has great snares, but is overall my least favorite track. “House Music Is Controllable Desire You Can Own” is my favorite of the chill out songs. I love the lively piano line and especially the chord changes therein. There’s also kind of a hip hop breakbeat in the percussion behind the bass drum that works well. “Sisters, I Don’t Know What This World Is Coming To” has a lot of good elements, particularly the piano and thee jazzy skittering and almost militaristic drums, but the vocal sample is overused and it drags on far too long. I’m torn. “Reverse Rotation” is yet another chill out track. It’s tasteful, but I’m ready for something new. Thankfully, “Grand Central, Pt. 1 (Deep into the Bowel of House)” provides. I just *love* the propulsion of this track. The bass line is what makes it go and go and go. I also love the snares and the ominous drone in the background. Great track. “Grand Central, Pt. II (72 Hrs. by Rail from Missouri)” is a long ambient piece but it’s actually pretty moving. The quote is heartbreaking and combined with the train sounds it really does evoke leaving a bad situation. “The Occasional Feel-Good” might be close the album as its best track. It’s just so lush. It makes me want to dance. What a banger that lives up to its title.

This was a fun one. Not much more to say than what I already have. It’s a great mix of bangers and artistic integrity.

Favorite tracks: Midtown 120 Intro, Midtown 120 Blues, House Music Is Controllable Desire You Can Own, Grand Central, Pt. 1 (Deep into the Bowel of House), Grand Central, Pt. II (72 Hrs. by Rail from Missouri), The Occasional Feel-Good

Least favorite track: Brenda’s $20 Dilemma
