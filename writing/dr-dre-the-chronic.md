# Write-up

Dr. Dre (real name Andre Young) is a hip hop producer born in 1965 and raised in Compton, California. His parents divorced when he was young and he was raised by his mother, grandmother, and stepfathers. Dre became interested in hip hop as a 16 year old in 1981 after hearing influential DJ Grandmaster Flash’s extended instrumental “The Adventures of Grandmaster Flash on the Wheels of Steel”. He began DJing himself at local clubs, adopting the name Dr. J in reference to NBA player Julius Erving. He eventually merged this with his own name to become Dr. Dre. Around this time, he befriended DJ Yella, who would be his production partner. The pair recorded their first track in 1984 and joined World Class Wreckin’ Cru, a locally popular electro collective. The track sold 50,000 copies in Compton. Dre met rapper Ice Cube in 1986. The two began making hip hop tracks together as a DJ/MC pair, releasing music on the Ruthless Records label owned by local gangster Eazy-E. This eventually coalesced into the group [REDACTED] Wit Attitudes, more commonly abbreviated to NWA. Dre was the group’s primary producer, with Yella taking a secondary role. NWA’s 1989 album *Straight Outta Compton* established the gansta rap genre; while classic NYC hip hop often had themes of inner city violence and drug abuse, LA gangsta rap actively celebrated the lifestyle instead of making a political statement about poverty. For the first time since hip hop’s inception in the 70s, the center of the genre moved out of NYC and to the West Coast. NWA was unstable and poorly managed, leading to primary lyricist Ice Cube leaving in 1989 after one album. Despite this loss and the ensuing vitriolic beef between NWA and Ice Cube, NWA remained the most popular hip hop group in the world. Dre was the next to leave, going out on his own on bad terms after a fight with Eazy-E and causing the breakup of NWA. Dre made this decision with consultation from Suge Knight, one of the most odious figures in the history of hip hop. Dre and Suge Knight co-founded Death Row Records together in 1991, which would soon dominate the LA rap scene.

Dre wanted to make solo music, but he had always been a weak rapper and even worse lyricist; his genius lay solely in production. When Dre released his first solo single in 1992, he recruited Long Beach rapper Snoop Doggy Dogg (real name Calvin Cordozar Broadus Jr.) to rap over his production. Dre’s stepbrother, rapper Warren G, had previously been in a group with Snoop and introduced him to Dre. Snoop was the perfect avatar for Dre’s production, resulting in him featuring heavily on Dr. Dre’s 1992 debut album *The Chronic*. The album’s title refers to slang for high-quality cannabis. *The Chronic* popularized the hip hop subgenre known as G-funk. Dre was a big fan of old funk music in the synth-heavy style of George Clinton’s Parliament-Funkadelic, a genre itself known as P-funk. As opposed to just sampling, Dre recreated and interpolated P-funk instrumentals to give his beats the same slow, simple, smooth feel as P-funk, with the gangsta rap lyrics creating the name G-funk. In his verses, which were primarily written by Snoop, Dre frequently dissed his former NWA groupmates Ice Cube and Eazy-E. Snoop was the most notably featured rapper on the album, delivering prominent verses on all three singles (“Nuthin’ But a G Thang”, “Fuck Wit Dre Day )And Everybody’s Celebratin’”, and “Let Me Ride”) and launching him to widespread popularity. However, *The Chronic* also introduced the world to many other major rappers of the mid-90s West Coast scene, including The D.O.C., Daz Dillinger, Warren G, Nate Dogg, Kurupt, and others on the Death Row label. While the album was critically divisive, it was a major commercial success, selling 3,000,000 copies and staying in the top 10 of the Billboard 200 for eight months. In retrospect, it is considered among the best and most influential albums of the 90s in any genre.

In 1993, Snoop Dogg released his debut studio album *Doggystyle*, produced by Dr. Dre. It went 4x platinum, outselling even *The Chronic*. Despite his immense popularity, Snoop’s career was put on pause from 1993-1996 as he was charged with and put on trial for murder (he was kinda sorta innocent, it was his bodyguard who killed the guy). G-funk was now the most popular style in hip hop, and would remain so throughout the mid-90s. Death Row Records became the most important record label in hip hop, replacing Eazy-E’s Ruthless Records and reaching the heights of NYC’s golden age Def Jam label. While Death Row put out hit after hit, Suge Knight is unfortunately one of the worst people to ever live. He personally instigated much of the East Coast/West Coast beef that cost so many lives, alongside Puff Daddy and his NYC label Bad Boy Records. Death Row signed 2Pac in 1995, making him a national star with the help of Dr. Dre’s production on singles like “California Love”. Though Death Row was at the top of its game, Dre grew tired of dealing with Suge Knight and left to form his own label Aftermath Records in 1996. Along with 2Pac’s murder the same year, Death Row went into decline, beginning the end of West Coast dominance in the hip hop scene. Artists like Nas and Jay-Z helped reassert NYC in the pop rap game, while the South began to have major success with artists like OutKast and Three-6 Mafia.

Aftermath struggled early on, but finally hit its stride after Dre shockingly signed a white rapper from Detroit who went by Eminem. Eminem’s massive success helped bolster Dre’s image again, resulting in his second solo album *2001* in 1999. Compared to the shambolic state of the rest of the West Coast at the time, *2001* was a major success, going 6x platinum. Since then, Dre has largely focused his efforts on being a record label executive, businessman, and producer for other artists. Aftermath signed 50 Cent in 2002, turning him into a star as well. Dre gave young Compton rapper Kendrick Lamar a contract with Aftermath in 2011 after hearing his independent debut album *Section.80*. Dre released his third and final solo album *Compton* in 2015 to accompany the film *Straight Outta Compton*, which told the story of NWA and was produced by Dre himself. Dre’s most well-known business venture is the Beats by Dre headphone brand. Its sale to Apple in the 2010s made Dr. Dre hip hop’s first billionaire. To this day, Dre still produces music for frequent collaborators like Snoop and Eminem. He remains one of the most influential hip hop producers of all time.

# Hargrove's review

Finally, we get to listen to some real West Coast shit after all the New York City bullshit that this list has been forcing down our throats. I had actually never listened to *The Chronic* in full because, in case you’re not familiar, it’s had a very rocky history with appearing on Spotify. In the late 2010s when I was really getting into music and exploring things, *The Chronic* was an Apple Music exclusive because of the Beats deal. It also disappeared from streaming in the 2020s when Snoop wanted to see if he could turn it into an NFT. Still, I was very familiar with all the singles and loved all of them. I was a little bit worried that the non-single tracks wouldn’t keep that same high quality, and there is definitely a bit of a lull about two-thirds of the way through the album, but overall this record is consistently stellar. I fucking love this West Coast sound, man. It’s in my DNA. It sounds so motherfucking smooth while you’re listening to it. Those high synths that have become synonymous with the sound of being a gangsta, the low synths that rumble through everything so sleazy, the flutes, the drawled rapping. There were moments of this album (particularly “Lil’ Ghetto Boy”) that sincerely reminded me of Marvin Gaye’s *What’s Going On*. Dre captures the very best sounds of the 70s and updates them for the particular circumstances of the 90s. Dre himself isn’t the most charismatic rapper in the world, but I do think he has a great voice, most of his ghostwriters were quite talented, and he intelligently let the Death Row Inmates take most of the verses. Snoop, on the other hand, sounds consistently amazing. Very few rappers have a single-album performance as strong as Snoop Dogg’s numerous features on *The Chronic*. Sometimes his bars aren’t even that good, but the sheer charisma of his vocals can turn a line like “One, two, three and to the four” into an all-time classic. The run from the beginning of the album to about “Lil’ Ghetto Boy” is absolutely immaculate. The album reaches its lowest point in the stretch from “Lyrical Gangbang” to “Stranded on Death Row” when Dre just lets the Death Row Inmates put together a bunch of posse cuts in a row. That is to say, this album isn’t perfect, and it would probably even be better had it been cut down to around 50 minutes. Nevertheless, I can’t complain about the combination of top-tier singles and overall consistent listenability. I never feel cooler than when I walk down the street with Dr. Dre in my AirPods. Uh, I mean, my Beats headphones.

“The Chronic (Intro)” is one of my favorite dedicated intro tracks I’ve ever heard on any album. Snoop just goes absolutely unhinged atop a banger gangsta synth. It was such a good decision by Dre to unleash Snoop’s “But, but, but you know what I want them to do for me?/Jerry, Eazy, check this shit right here/I want y'all to put these bizzalls in your jizzaws/And work them like a strizzaw and tell me what you sizzaw” to start the record. “Fuck Wit Dre Day (And Everybody’s Celebratin’)” is the first of the album’s three singles to appear, and, fuck, is it a great song. Beyond iconic lines like “'Cause when you diss Dre you diss yourself“ and “Bow-wow-wow, yippy-yo, yippy-yay”, that bass synth is just so FUNKY and the high synth glides all night long. What a banger. I love the laidback single “Let Me Ride”. It is so fucking smooth. It captures the vibe of the 70s so well. And I appreciate how laidback it sounds, as well as the super catchy hook. “The Day the [REDACTED] Took Over” is a really well-done conceptual piece about the LA riots. Dre curated and inserted those samples from the news coverage and the riots so perfectly. The beat also reminds me a lot of Three-6 Mafia (though, to be clear, it predates Three-6 Mafia). Those eerie horror synths complement the subject matter perfectly. “Nuthin’ but a ‘G’ Thang” is a formidable contender for greatest hip hop song of all time. It’s just a perfect track. No one has ever sounded cooler than Snoop in his opening verse. As long as I live, I will never get One, two, three and to the four/Snoop Doggy Dogg and Dr. Dre is at the door”, “Death Row is the label that pays me”, or “It's like this and like that and like this and a—“ out of my head. The same goes for the synth line, which has rattled around my head for years. And I love the chemistry between Dre and Snoop on the hook. I have to admit, the skit at the beginning of “Deeez Nuuuts” gets a real laugh out of me. This album does such a good job of making the skits engaging and reasonable. The formula still works on this track, with the synth as grimy as ever and the bass line BOUNCING. I enjoy Nate Dogg’s singing on the outro. While “Lil’ Ghetto Boy” doesn’t have many standout individual lines, I love the narrative in the verses. It’s good to have these moments of reflection. Snoop murders the first verse, while the flute line adds so much melancholy. It all reminds me so much of Marvin Gaye.

I wouldn’t necessarily call it a miss, but “A [REDACTED] Witta Gun” is the first meh song on the album. The guy with the gun in his mouth sounds a little too silly in the opening skit. Overall, though, I think my problem is that the bravado doesn’t fully land. It feels over-the-top compared to the other tracks. By contrast, “Rat-Tat-Tat-Tat” perfectly balances violence with slapstick. I like the bait-and-switch of the slow and reflective opening before returning to the normal mood of the album. And it’s another track with a hook I can’t stop humming. “The $20 Sack Pyramid” is hilarious, particularly the bit about Tim Dog. “Lyrical Gangbang” is the first track without a Snoop appearance, which of course makes it my least favorite. In all seriousness, while I do think the verses are good (RBX > The Lady of Rage > Kurupt), this track greatly suffers from featuring neither Snoop nor Dre, as well as from being this deep into the record. “High Powered” has those same issues, but RBX’s verse is so good that I’m willing to overlook it. “The Doctor’s Office” is easily the worst skit, but I don’t think it’s as bad as its haters make it out to be. The acting performances are quite good! “Stranded on Death Row” has a nice beat that feels almost East Coast with the energetic drums and the sample flip for melody. The verses are fine but nothing too special, outside of Snoop’s. It’s a fine song, I’m just sick of these posse cuts at this point. I know it’s not actually the final track, but I love “The Roach (The Chronic Outro)” as an outro. It captures the smooth, sleazy feeling of the record so well, and it’s pretty funny on top of that. I also love the transitions between the spoken parts and the sung chorus. Sure, “Bitches Ain’t Shit” is pretty misogynistic, but FUCK is it a fun listen. That hook is just so iconic. It’s also hilarious to throw a full Eazy diss verse on a song like this, and Snoop does a great job telling a story on his verse.

It’s not perfect, but *The Chronic* is one of the most fun classics out there. It’s a good time to listen to from beginning to end. It’s the perfect music to play while cruising through California.

Favorite tracks: The Chronic (Intro), Fuck Wit Dre Day (And Everybody’s Celebratin’), Let Me Ride, The Day the [REDACTED] Took Over, Nuthin’ but a “G” Thang, Deeez Nuuuts, Lil’ Ghetto Boy, Rat-Tat-Tat-Tat, The Roach (The Chronic Outro), Bitches Ain’t Shit

Least favorite track: Lyrical Gangbang
