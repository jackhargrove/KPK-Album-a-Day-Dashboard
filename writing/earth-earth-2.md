# Write-up

Earth is a metal band formed in Olympia, Washington in 1989 by guitarist Dylan Carlson, vocalist Slim Moon, and bassist Greg Babior. The lineup has shifted constantly since its formation, with Carlson as the leader and only consistent member. Earth created the drone metal genre. The group takes its name from Black Sabbath’s original name, which was also Earth. Earth released its debut album, *Earth 2: Special Low Frequency Version*, in 1993 on Sub Pop, the Seattle-based label that incubated grunge. The record features Carlson on guitar and Dave Harwell on bass, with the extremely limited percussion on the final track provided by Joe Burns. Carlson described the idea for the album’s sound as stemming from his childhood, when he would hear a band like AC/DC play a riff and wish that they would continue playing the riff forever. When he discovered minimalist composers like La Monte Young, he set out to apply their ideas to Slayer-style metal riffs. The sound was also heavily influenced by the sludge metal (doom metal + hardcore punk) of the Melvins, a sound adopted by most in the Washington music scene. The album took advantage of the still-new CD format, taking effectively one song and stretching it across the entire 74 minute maximum runtime of a CD. The album’s title and cover art both reference a series of nature recording records from the 80s. The album was not a smash commercial success, though the Sub Pop sticker did certainly attract unsuspecting buyers; over time, however, the album has inspired every band to play drone metal.

Carlson was a close friend of fellow Washingtonian Kurt Cobain. The two lived together as roommates for a few years in the late 80s. Cobain provided vocals for a demo track on the live album *Sunn Amps and Smashed Guitars*. One notable biographer of Cobain claims that Nirvana’s song “In Bloom” was written about Carlson. Sadly, Carlson played a tragic role in Cobain’s suicide: Cobain’s erratic behavior prevented him from buying a gun, so he convinced Carlson to buy a shotgun for him. Cobain used the shotgun to commit suicide less than a week later. Given the revolving door nature of Earth’s lineup, many other notable members of the Washington music scene have been associated with the band. Founding vocalist Slim Moon went on to start Olympia-based record label Kill Rock Stars in 1991, which would popularize the feminist punk genre of riot grrrl with artists like Bikini Kill and Sleater-Kinney, as well as artists on the list like Elliott Smith and Unwound. Former Earth bassist Joe Preston went on to play on five records with Sunn O))). Another Earth bassist, Karl Blau, was also a member of Phil Elverum’s group the Microphones. Cellist Lori Goldston, who toured with Nirvana and played on their 1994 *MTV Unplugged in New York* live album, was also a member of Earth for a time (Goldston has also played with jazz artist Matana Roberts, but they are not from Washington). As is usually the case for this era of Washington music, most of these people met as teenagers in the 80s while hanging around sludge metal band the Melvins.

Carlson’s heroin addiction, grief over Cobain’s suicide, and a prison sentence for burglary caused Earth to collapse in 1997. When the band returned in 2003, it had shaken up its sound considerably, adding drums and elements from country music. Carlson’s wife Adrienne Davies was added to Earth as permanent member on drums. The couple has since divorced, but Davies remains in the band. She has also played with Boris and Sunn O))). Earth continues to perform to this day, with their most recent album, their 10th overall, releasing in 2019.

# Hargrove's review

Only two weeks after Sunn O))) introduced us to pure, unrelenting drone metal, Earth takes us back to where it all began. Along with the two Boris records we listened to, there have been four albums on this list that could be reasonably called “drone metal”, one of the most out there and alien forms of music we’ve heard so much from. As such, I think it makes sense to compare these bands with each other to help elucidate what I do and don’t like about the style. Earth, given its origins in Washington’s sludge metal scene, keeps a sludgy, lethargic feel that naturally lends itself to drone music. Boris, as Melvins superfans, keep this sludginess, but complement it with dynamic, psychedelic, and progressive sounds. Sunn O))), by contrast, uses black metal accoutrements like growling vocals, tremolo picking, jarring riffs, and lo-fi production values, crafting a vibe darker than it is hazy. On this front, I prefer the sludge metal sonics of Boris and Earth, though I appreciate the diversity Sunn O))) brings to the table. Structurally, I think all three strive for different effects. Earth begins with clearly defined riffs, slowly picking them apart as the album progresses until nothing remains. It’s about entropy, the dissipation of order into chaos. It reminds me of *The Disintegration Loops* in that way. Boris similarly concerns itself with dynamics, but it greatly shifts the focus. Where Earth begins with its most coherent moments, both Boris albums we listened to place their most coherent moments exactly in the middle of the record. The structure instead interrogates both the build-up and the extended falling action, nothing into something into nothing. Sunn O))) abandons this dynamic approach entirely, using similar structures throughout the whole album to emphasize the unchanging walls of sound. I favor Boris’ approach, though there’s something interesting for me in all three. Overall, I think Boris > Earth > Sunn O))), but I’m not married to that ranking. I still feel very much like an outsider to this kind of music, slowly getting my sea legs. All of it strikes me like it would be better live, where you can feel the wall of sound smacking repeatedly into your chest. I will be seeing Sunn O))) live next week and will report back on the experience. For now, I’m ranking Earth alongside Sunn O))) and Boris’ *Boris at Last -Feedbacker-* in C tier to signal my similar feelings, and keeping Boris’ *Flood* above in B Tier because of how beautifully it integrates these feelings into a more moving sonic format.

Since I spent the first paragraph talking about drone metal generally, I’ll give my Earth-specific takes here in the track-by-track paragraph. I respect what it’s going for with the entropy approach, but as tracks I easily like each one less than the track before it. “Seven Angels” has by far the most action, making it my favorite track. It finds quite a few nice riffs, cycling between them over its 15 minute runtime. In particular, I really like the main motif riff that pops up again in the final two minutes. It actually sounds like it would go pretty hard at 3x the speed and with sharper definition. As the transitional track, “Teeth of Lions Rule the Divine” deserves credit for most demonstrating the album’s concept of decay. That being said, I’d just rather listen to the opener if I’m picking out a track. “Like Gold and Faceted” is literally just one chord sustained for over half an hour. There are little flourishes of percussion which I do enjoy, but at the same time I worry they detract from the unrelenting stillness. I’m placing it as my least favorite track because it’s the one I’d be least likely to put on, but I do appreciate what it’s going for. Overall, this was not an unenjoyable album to have playing ambiently all day. But man is it hard to fully “get”.

I hate to cluster together albums of a particular style because it feels lazy, but I really feel I have to here, at least for now. I like them all about the same amount. I’m going to have those buzzing, detuned guitars buzzing in my ears for a few hours. At least it’s a more enjoyable tone than my baseline tinnitus.

Favorite tracks: Seven Angels

Least favorite track: Like Gold and Faceted

# Aidan's review

Our 4th? drone metal album and this one is the OG. Not much else going for it. It's by far the most barebones, with the longest riffs and basically nothing going on besides some small fluctuations and the cymbal crashes on the third track. I like to imagine that they just set up a guitar next to an amp, went out for lunch and came back to find their album done. It's not quite that minimalistic in reality, but it's not that far off. With other drone metal albums, I found parts that I liked which gave me something to credit the broader album for creating the atmosphere for. Here, there's none of that. It's not unpleasant, but it isn't church bells either.
