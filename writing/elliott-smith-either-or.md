# Write-up

Elliott Smith (real name Steven Smith) was an American singer-songwriter born in 1969. His acoustic sound and alternative rock background combined to form the basis of a sound that came to be known as “indie folk”. Smith was raised in Duncanville, Texas, in a religious family by his mother and stepfather, the latter of whom may or may not have sexually abused him. He learned piano at age 9 and guitar at age 10, quickly winning a local award for his songwriting. In 1983, at age 14, Smith moved to Portland, Oregon, to live with his biological father. He was a good student, winning a National Merit Scholarship, but he also started experimenting with drugs. After graduating high school, Smith started going by the name “Elliott”, disliking both the names “Steve” and “Steven”. Smith went to Hampshire College in Massachusetts, graduating with a degree in philosophy and poli sci in 1991. While there, he formed the alt rock back Heatmiser. Upon graduation, Smith did what any sensible person who wrote a thesis titled "Toward a Post-Structuralist Feminist Jurisprudence" at a small liberal arts college would do: moved back to Portland. Heatmiser moved with him, becoming established within the Pacific Northwest scene. Smith also began recording his own songs, and a local independent label put out his solo debut *Roman Candle* in 1994. The acoustic sound was at odds with both the grunge popular at the time, as well as the rock music of his own band Heatmiser. *Roman Candle* quickly made Elliott Smith a well-known name among Pacific Northwest music fans. This earned him a deal with legendary local independent label Kill Rock Stars, famous for their feminist riot grrrl punk artists. Smith released his second album, *Elliott Smith*, on this label in 1995. Heatmiser was still going, signing to a major label itself, but Smith’s solo success was hard for the band to handle and it broke up in 1996.

In 1997, Smith released his third solo album, *Either/Or*, on Kill Rock Stars. The album is named after a book by 19th century Danish philosopher Søren Kierkegaard. The album’s sound was transitional for Smith; his first two records were sparse, dark records of acoustic guitar and vocals, while his later records showcased an orchestral sound. *Either/Or* retains the mood of Smith’s early work, but with expanded instrumentation. *Either/Or* is commonly seen as Smith’s masterpiece thanks to tracks like “Between the Bars” (later used in *Rick and Morty* and also covered by Madonna), “Angeles”, and “Say Yes”. He still wasn’t popular enough to chart on Billboard, but the album received rave reviews from critics. One noted fan was film director Gus Van Sant, who at the time was working on the film *Good Will Hunting*. He decided to use three songs from *Either/Or* in the movie, and asked Smith to contribute an original song as well. Instead of doing this, Smith gave Van Sant an unreleased track recorded during the *Either/Or* sessions called “Miss Misery”. The massive success of *Good Will Hunting* turned Elliott Smith into a nationally recognized musician. “Miss Misery” was nominated for an Oscar, earning Smith a performance at the Academy Awards ceremony in 1998, though he lost to Celine Dion.

Smith moved from Portland to New York, settling in Brooklyn. He had no desire to capitalize on newfound mainstream success, considering his Oscars performance a fun excursion before returning to a world where he was more comfortable. Still, he signed to the major label DreamWorks Records. Unfortunately, his depression worsened, and he began severely abusing alcohol and prescription drugs. He began seriously trying to kill himself, including an incident where he jumped off a cliff in North Carolina and impaled himself on a tree. Despite this, Smith released his fourth album, the lushly orchestral *XO*, in 1998. It hit number 104 on the Billboard 200 and earning him a performance on SNL. In 1999, Smith moved to Los Angeles. A lifelong Beatles fan, their influence shows up heavily in this period of his work. He performed a cover of “Because” from *Abbey Road* for the 1999 film *American Beauty* and recorded part of his fifth album, 2000’s *Figure 8*, at Abbey Road Studios in London. It was his first album to break into the top 100 of the Billboard 200. Smith’s personal life fell off a cliff after *Figure 8*. He fell into a deep paranoia, constantly fearing he was being followed by a white van. He stopped sleeping and he ate only ice cream. One of his collaborators claimed that in 2001, Smith consumed $1,500 worth of crack and heroin every single day, sometimes trying to overdose on purpose. Things came to a head in 2002, when Smith ruined a concert with Wilco and later got into a fight at a Flaming Lips show.

Smith sought medical treatment for his addictions, and started attempting a comeback with two sold-out shows in 2003. He actually got serious about recovery, quitting alcohol permanently on his 34th birthday in August 2003. He began working on a new album, *Basement on a Hill*, for which he was experimenting with noise and electronic music. Just when everything seemed to be trending upward, tragedy struck. On October 21, 2003, Smith got into an argument with his girlfriend at their LA home. After the argument, his girlfriend locked herself in the bathroom to shower. While she was in there, Smith committed suicide via two stab wounds to the chest. He was 34 years old. Conspiracy theories about Smith’s death have swirled for years due to the fact that he had been in recovery from addiction, had no hesitation wounds, and a widespread myth that he misspelled his own name on his suicide note (this was, in fact, a typo from the coroner). However, all evidence points to suicide. *From a Basement on a Hill* was released posthumously in 2004, followed by the compilation *New Moon* in 2007 that hit number 24 on the Billboard 200. Smith’s legacy has only grown since his suicide, especially as indie folk became one of the most popular genres in the world in the late 2000s. Countless artists cite him as an influence, perhaps the most notable being Phoebe Bridgers.

# Hargrove's review

I love Elliott Smith. I loooooooooooooove Elliott Smith. I have largely shut myself off from this album over the last year and a half as I have with other albums on the list, and that was a bummer. I listen to *Either/Or* all the time. Few works of art so reliably elicit this strong of an emotional reaction in me. I listen to “Between the Bars” at the end of the night when I’m drunk, “2:45 AM” when it’s late and I can’t sleep, “Alameda” when I’m mad at myself, “Pictures of Me” when I’m mad at the world, and “Say Yes” when I need a little taste of hope, no matter how doomed. Elliott Smith lays bare every emotion I’ve ever felt more powerfully than I’ve ever felt it. This was one of the first albums I listened to in 2017 when I decided I wanted to get more into music, probably less than a month into that journey, and it hit me like a sack of bricks. How could anything be this beautiful? Smith took a tried-and-true formula, depressed guy with a guitar, and turned it into 12 perfectly-written songs to rival Lennon-McCartney. I mean, seriously, the consistency on this record is remarkable. Yeah, there are standouts, but there is not a bad song. But on top of the sadboy vocals and guitar, he adds subtle, haunting keyboards and perfect, distinctive, paper-thin drums (which I actually learned today he played himself. I assumed he had a friend be a session drummer). I had never really thought before that drums would add much to singer-songwriter music, but it adds another layer of expressiveness that I think really kicks these songs into a higher gear. His lyrics are almost unbelievably poignant, excelling in both stylish individual lines (“Everybody’s dying just to get the disease”) and longer form poetry (“Between the Bars” is perfectly written from beginning to end). And his vocals…fuck, man. I was struck from the first moment I heard him. He sings so delicately, as if his voice could shatter it into a million jagged pieces at any moment, and yet he can slip into immensely powerful line deliveries without missing a beat. Every last track has a memorable, novel melody, emotionally devastating lyricism, overpowering atmosphere, and simple but effective arrangement. It’s genuinely a perfect record. Listening to it again after over a year has been such a revelation and I cannot get enough.

“Speed Trials” is such an interesting pick as an opener; the lyrics are much more cryptic than other Smith songs (dare I say Dylanesque?), while the lo-fi production gives it an ominous, otherworldly atmosphere, as if it were a tape left behind by a ghost in a condemned house. For this reason, it took me longer to get into than the rest of the album. Now, though, I adore it. I love how you can hear the tape start rolling at the beginning. I love Smith’s icy cold vocals and the tinny drums. It has a delicate, beautiful chorus. What a very cool track. Because I didn’t get it when I was 16, I often thought of “Alameda” as the true opener, and it became my favorite Smith track. Today, I think that opinion holds up. That bright backbeat gives the song such a strange swagger, the arrangement slowly building like a frog in boiling water until you realize what a lumbering behemoth the song has become. I adore the vocal melody, as well as the line “Nobody broke your hear/You broke your own, ‘cause you can’t finish what you start”. Smith often sings in the second-person, giving his songs a uniquely intimate feel. Little touches like the hand sliding over the fretboard at the end add to that. “Ballad of Big Nothing” begins with a lovely guitar intro before the drums turn it into a fully fleshed-out track. The bittersweet chorus slaps me in the face every time I hear it. It sounds nice on the surface, but then you realize that you absolutely can just waste your life if you want and no one will make you do otherwise. Smith exhibits some of the greatest songwriting ever on “Between the Bars”. The writing is so smooth and fluid that I’m pretty sure I had the full thing memorized after just one listen. There is not a single word wasted! Smith sings in such a haunting whisper over a guitar line that feels like it’s lulling me into a sleep from which I may never wake. It’s truly one of the greatest songs ever. The persistent strumming and organ stabs on “Pictures of Me” give the track such power, a nice shift from the quiet beauty of the previous song. Once Smith kicks his voice up an octave on “Saw you and me on the coin-op TV”, the song transitions into pure power. He bares his soul on the chorus with some of the most moving vocals I’ve ever heard, as angry as anything Nine Inch Nails has ever released. “Who’d like to see me down on my fucking knees/Everybody’s dying just to get the disease” is a highlight and demonstrates the power of sparing profanity use.

“No Name No. 5” is the closest thing to a misstep on this album. It’s a little too reserved after the smoldering anger of the last track, and the two tracks that follow take a while to build back up. Still, I like this song a lot, and if this is the worst song on an album, it’s a fucking phenomenal album. Traditionally, “Rose Parade” is my least favorite track because it doesn’t stick with me as well as the others. However, I’ve been liking it a lot on this set of listens. It reminds me of Phoebe Bridgers’ “Scott Street” (or, rather, the reverse). My main complaint is that it contributes to the slightest of lulls here in the middle of the record, but I enjoy the structure of the songwriting. It’s also got a few nice melodic and rhythmic ideas. I like the country flavor of “Punch and Judy” and the chorus gets stuck in my head. It has the brightest atmosphere excepting “Say Yes”, but still has a hanging air of disappointment and hopelessness. “Angeles” has always struck me as a particularly menacing song. I love that intricate guitar line, especially in the opening. There’s so much bite to the way Smith sings here. There are no standout lines in particular but I find it very well-written in the way it translates the apprehension he felt toward the music industry. It makes me uncomfortable in the best way. I love the placement of “Cupid’s Trick” at this point in the album, just a loud, forceful track to start the homestretch. It’s a complete shock to the system, knocking me on my ass with the ferocity of the instrumental breakdown. I find that it has a kind of a hazy, psychedelic quality to it, almost hypnotic. “2:45 AM” is my favorite of the deep cuts. The start is just so mellow, with the ascending guitar line capturing the vibe of this time of night perfectly. When the drums come in for the outro, it’s pure, cathartic emotional release. The album closes on “Say Yes”, one of the most gorgeous songs ever written. It has just beautiful lyrics, an awesome psychedelic bridge, and sublime melodies. “I'm in love with the world/Through the eyes of a girl/Who's still around the morning after” takes a lovely sentiment and elevates it with stylish execution. And it’s a needed note of hope to end the album on.

God, this album is just so, so, so good. *Roman Candle*, *Elliott Smith*, and *XO* are all phenomenal as well. I highly recommend anyone check out Elliott Smith’s work. Everyone needs some tragic, heartbreaking beauty every once in a while.

Favorite tracks: Speed Trials, Alameda, Ballad of Big Nothing, Between the Bars, Pictures of Me, Rose Parade, Punch and Judy, Angeles, Cupid’s Trick, 2:45 AM, Say Yes

Least favorite track: No Name No. 5
