# Write-up

Eric B. & Rakim was a hip hop duo formed on Long Island in 1986 composed of MC Rakim (real name William Michael Griffin Jr.) and DJ Eric B. (real name Louis Eric Barrier). *Paid in Full* is the oldest hip hop album on our list, though its sound was far ahead of its time; Rakim’s cool and calculated style of rapping more or less invented the idea of “flow” and by the early 90s had completely supplanted the energetic, shouted rapping style of old school hip hop. Eric B. was born in 1963 and grew up in Queens. He discovered the infant genre of hip hop as a teenager at parties in NYC in the late 70s and learned how to DJ. In the 80s, he became a DJ for local radio station WBLS and decided to look for an MC to rap over his beats. His first choice was Freddie Foxxx, but when Eric went to his house to find him, he wasn’t there. So he pursued a second recommendation instead, an 18-year-old MC from Long Island who went by Rakim (pronounced like raw-KIMM). “Rakim” was an Arabic name that he had adopted after he became a member of the Five Percent Nation, an offshoot of the Nation of Islam that was very important to New York hip hop. In contrast to the basic rhymes and high energy of popular acts like Run DMC and Beastie Boys, Rakim rapped calmly and methodically and included complex rhyme schemes (internal rhymes, polysyllabic rhymes) that ignored bar lines. He also notably wrote and memorized his verses beforehand, in contrast to the hip hop custom of MCs improvising their verses. The two joined together as a duo in 1986 and quickly recorded the single “Eric B. Is President”. The beat is based around a James Brown funk sample, a signature of Eric B.’s DJing. It generated enough buzz for Russell Simmons to sign Eric B. and Rakim to Island Records.

*Paid in Full* was made over the span of a week in 1987. The label put them under significant time pressure; Rakim wrote all of his verses in an hour and read them off a piece of paper as he performed them. There are also three instrumental tracks that were largely included as filler, though that doesn’t necessarily mean they’re bad. The album was named after a the Paid in Full Crew, a group of rappers and criminals in New York that included the original 50 Cent (after whom the 50 Cent we know named himself). The album was a commercial success at the time, launching Eric B. & Rakim to the top of the hip hop game. In retrospect, its stature has only grown, with it still being named as one of the greatest hip hop albums of all time. It’s certainly one of the most influential. Eric B. & Rakim would release three more records between 1988 and 1992. All three were successful both commercially and critically, particularly their third album which went gold. They split up in 1993 after Eric B. refused to sign a new contract, worried Rakim would leave him. They wouldn’t reunite until 2016. Both have released solo records, but none of them have made the same impact as their early duo records.

Eric B. was a gifted and respected DJ, but Rakim is one of the most important figures in the history of hip hop. His groundbreaking flow almost immediately rendered existing rap groups obsolete and they all sound extremely dated today. *Paid in Full* can even be seen as the first modern hip hop record (you could also give this title to Run DMC’s *Raising Hell* in 1986, which largely solidified the form of hip hop but still had outdated rapping). A generation of younger rappers looked up to Rakim, including the members of Wu-Tang Clan, A Tribe Called Quest, De La Soul, and, more than anyone else, Nas, who sometimes called himself “the new Rakim”. By the late-90s and 2000s, rappers like Tupac, Notorious B.I.G., Jay-Z, Eminem, and 50 Cent were still citing Rakim as a major influence, but, by that time, Rakim’s style had become synonymous with East Coast rap.

# Hargrove's review

This is such a cool record from a historical perspective. It’s outrageous that there’s a more or less fully formed boom bap record released all the way back in 1987, with singles from 1986. It really doesn’t compute that this came out mere months after Run DMC’s *Raising Hell* or Beastie Boys’ *Licensed to Ill*. From that point of view, I’ve always found this record super cool. On the other hand, it’s far from a perfect record. It’s rushed and rudimentary. “Eric B. Is on the Cut” comes in at track 2 to suck out any momentum that “I Ain’t No Joke” created. While Rakim’s flows are pretty much 90s, his lyrics are not all that interesting. He doesn’t even get into the fun storytelling stuff that Tribe would a few years later, let alone the conscious street poetry of Nas by 94. In fact, I would say the subject matter of the lyrics is what dates this album the most. “Paid in Full” has a cool set-up with some spoken word stuff and a backstory for Rakim. Otherwise, every track is pretty standard party stuff. Rakim still has some good lines in that milieu; “I hold the microphone like a grudge” and “To me, MC means Move the Crowd” come to mind, and “fish, which is my favorite dish” is always funny. Eric B.’s production can also leave something to be desired. His greatest skill is his ear for bass lines, but when the bass line is meh, his beats walk the line between minimalist and empty. And this was made before people really had an idea of how to craft a good hook, something that is palpably missing here. This album has flaws up and down and is certainly not a go-to record for me when I’m in the mood for hip hop. Still, it has a certain allure. I have those action figures, after all. I think to make a case beyond just the context, it’s sonically interesting to have an album that creates such old-school vibes with more listenable modern techniques. I think that should count for something. It’s much easier to listen to this than any other rap album from the era. So I don’t know. I like it. But I’m under no delusion that it’s perfect.

“I Ain’t No Joke” starts things off with a banger. It’s got a simple beat, good drums, a good hook, good bars from Rakim. It’s exactly what i’m looking for. “Eric B. Is on the Cut” is my least favorite instrumental track and its placement is terrible. “My Melody” is too long, and that almost prevented me from listing it as a favorite track, but man is it cool. I love how grimy Rakim’s vocals sound. It also has one of my favorite drum beats. “I Know You Got Soul” has perhaps the best overall instrumental on the album. Fantastic, fantastic bass sample from Eric. Just a funky song. “Move the Crowd” is my least favorite of the tracks with vocals. i don’t love the beat and Rakim doesn’t do anything too impressive. “Paid in Full” is awesome though. It opens with a great skit, has a bop of an instrumental with the bass, the drums, and the flute, and Rakim lays down one insane verse. It’s short, but that has to be the best verse laid in all of hip hop through 1987. “As the Rhyme Goes On” has a bass line that reminds me of “Like That” from last year. Rakim’s flow also has a sing-song quality that I like. The production on the drums is also cool, it sounds like they reversed the cymbals or something. “Chinese Arithmetic” is my favorite of the instrumentals. All the different sounds are cool and I wouldn’t have expected them to be used at this point in hip hop.  “Eric B. Is President” has some of Rakim’s most memorable lines. “Can you detect what’s coming next from the flex of the wrist” exemplifies everything he brought to the hip hop game. “Extended Beat” is a complete dud of a closer. We’ve already heard it, and it was one of the worst beats so far.

Tough one to rank. It has so much aura, but also so many flaws. Everyone should listen to this record at least once. It’s both an anomaly and incredibly important. And beyond all of that, Rakim just sounds fucking cool.

Favorite tracks: I Ain’t No Joke, My Melody, Paid in Full, As the Rhyme Goes On, Chinese Arithmetic, Eric B. Is President

Least favorite track: Eric B. Is on the Cut
