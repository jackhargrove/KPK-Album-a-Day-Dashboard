# Write-up

Fishmans were a Japanese rock band whose best known releases were with a lineup of singer/guitarist Shinji Sato, bassist Yuzuru Kashiwabara and drummer Kin-Ichi Motegi. The band was founded by Sato, Motegi and guitarist Kensuke Ojima in 1987 in Meiji Gakuin University, a private Christian college in Tokyo. Their early music was heavily influenced by the Jamaican-orignated genres of ska, dub and rocksteady. The historical order of development was ska, then rocksteady, then reggae, then dub, but all of these genres had already been developed by 1987.  Kashiwabara joined the group in 1988, and keyboardist Hakase-Sun joined in 1990. In 1990, they signed with Virgin Records Japan and released their first album, Chappie Don't Cry. They began to move away from their initial Jamaican-influenced sound due to collaboration with producer ZAK (real name Kazuyuki Matsumura), who pushed them to incorporate a variety of styles, including funk, jazz rock and pop. Still, they maintained Jamaican influences mostly in their rhythm section of Kashiwabara and Motegi.

Ojima left the group in '94 due to their stylistic shifts, and in '95, the group signed with Polydor Records, with whom they would release their best known albums. Polydor gave the group their own recording studio, and soon after Hakase-Sun left the group, leaving a trio, who would work with ZAK and studio musicians for the three albums they would release on Polydor. It was on Polydor that the band settled into the dream pop sound that they are best known for, starting with their fifth studio album, Kūchū Camp. Long Season, today's album and their most famous work, was their sixth studio album. The idea for the album started as "a song that never ends". The band expanded a previous song, "Season", into a 35 minute song that took up an entire album. Fishmans also performed the song live, and had a policy of never playing it the same way twice. Their final studio album, 1998's Uchū Nippon Setagaya, saw them shift into ambient pop. After that, ZAK decided to stop working with the band and Kashiwabara decided to leave music all together at the end of 1998.

In December of that year, the band embarked on a farewell tour, of which the final concert, recorded at Akasaka Blitz in Minato, Tokyo, was released as a live album, 98.12.28 Otokotachi no Wakare,  which was among the first 18 albums we listened to. Despite Kashiwabara's departure, Sato and Motegi still planned on continuing the band. However, in March of the following year, Sato died of a heart attack, and without its frontman, the band split up. In 2005, the band reunited, and they have continued to play their old songs to this day. For their entire original run, Fishmans were virtually unknown, both in their Native Japan and in the West. After Sato's death, some better known Japanese bands such as OOIOO (fronted by Yoshimi P-We, the drummer for Boredoms and the "Yoshimi" from the eponymous Flaming Lips album") covered their music. It was only in the early 10s that Fishmans began to be talked about on music forums liked /mu/ and RYM. Their acclaim has since grown to great heights, with Long Season and 98.12.28 Otokotachi no Wakare both ranking among the top 100 albums of all time according to RYM. Their fame spread from there onto other sources, with their music being added to Spotify in 2018 and there now exists a sizable group of amateur historians who document the history of the group online.

# Hargrove's review

I had never listened to Fishmans before when we listened to the live album *98.12.28 男達の別れ [98.12.28 Otokotachi no wakare]* all the way back in February 2025. I didn’t find that album to be absolutely terrible initially, but man is it just not that interesting. I can understand why someone with a pre-existing connection to the band might find its final performance moving. For anyone else, including me, it’s absolutely brutal to sit through 90 minutes of mind-numbingly boring Japanese reggae all for the reward of a relatively interesting 40 minute track at the end. Today’s album is just the studio version of that final track, something that initially annoyed me when I discovered it (which, to be clear, was a year-and-a-half ago, not this week). Like, I have to review something I’ve already reviewed again? But after thinking about it, given that it was easily the best part of the live album, I warmed to the idea of being able to review it on its own without the 90 extraneous minutes of indescribable drudgery attached. In practice, though, the closer scrutiny of listening to *Long Season* on its own has exposed some flaws to me that I hadn’t noticed before. Don’t get me wrong, I prefer *Long Season* to *98.12.28 男達の別れ [98.12.28 Otokotachi no wakare]* by several orders of magnitude, and there’s plenty to like about it: the catchy synth melody in the first and final sections, the relentless piano ostinato, the lively bass line, the krautrock drums, the violin and accordion. By a wide margin, part I (0:00 - 8:43) and part V (25:26 - 35:15) are the best parts of this composition. My main issue with them is that I don’t much care for Sato’s voice at all. I don’t connect with his squishy, formless wail whatsoever. But for the most part it’s fine, and I’m a fan of both of these sections. The much bigger issue comes in the middle, which takes up a full half of the runtime. The composition devolves into a collage of solos and sound effects, none of which interest me. Worse, they often actively irritate me. I’ll get more into those specifics in the next paragraph. This middle section is an anchor that drags everything down and makes me question whether this really needed to be a 35 minute track. There’s a solid 10 minute track in here! That’s an album closer right there. Extending it past that was pure folly. It just made me want to listen to Sufjan Stevens’ “Impossible Soul”, which does the super long multi-section progressive pop thing so, so, so much better.

“Long Season” starts with a strong bass line that continues throughout the entire composition. I’m not enthralled by the Indian-sounding guitar that accompanies it, but it’s not the worst thing in the world either. I greatly enjoy the central synth melody as well as the piano ostinato, both of which start fairly early on. This section before the first vocals is my favorite part of the composition. I’m indifferent toward Sato’s vocals on the first verse. My opinion becomes more negative at the chorus. Still, the drums have a great krautrock beat and I like the violin solo. “Long Season” starts to go off the rails with part II at 8:43. I don’t love how the sound effects are integrated, nor am I crazy about the “I get around in the season” refrain. The wordless soul vocal section really makes me roll my eyes. The drums and bass remain good to at least keep the composition afloat, but it’s hanging on by a thread. Part III severs that thread starting at 14:06. I HATE the water sound effect. It straight up sounds like someone taking a shit in a toilet. Is this a Mr. Bungle album? It’s fucking horrible in the vibe of this composition. The video game sound effects are hideously annoying as well. The drum freakout may be an improvement on the sound effect collage, but I’m at a loss for what it adds to the composition either. I don’t find this abstract section evocative or rewarding in any way. Part IV is an immediate improvement at 20:40, but it doesn’t seem to recognize the urgency with which it needs to pick up steam again. The ba ba bas and whistling just don’t cut it. Finally, the composition gets good again around 25:26 for part V. It’s nice to hear the central melody recapitulate alongside the piano ostinato. The guitar solo feels a little contrived, though; who thought we needed a Hendrix imitation here? And then the stupid moaning at the end feels like one of those things where I’m expected to sense a larger intensity of emotion than actually exists, a musical equivalent to Jeb Bush’s “please clap”. Still, this section has all of the things that I enjoyed about the first, and I don’t want to overstate my annoyances.

This composition is clearly impressive on a technical level and has some elements that resonate with me. It also just doesn’t make me feel much of anything at all. I’m putting it in C Tier because to reflect its redeeming qualities, but it’s not strong enough to break through the classic rock wall into the promised land of B Tier. I also re-listened to *98.12.28 男達の別れ [98.12.28 Otokotachi no wakare]* expecting that I would get the urge to at least move it up to D Tier, but, honestly, F Tier is where it belongs. The first 90 minutes made me want to kill myself just as much as *THIRD SIDE OF TAPE* did. The live version of “Long Season” is better than the album version, but not by an appreciable amount, and it still has most of the same problems. It’s not worth sitting through the rest of the album for at all, and probably wouldn’t break through the classic rock wall into B Tier even on its own either.

Favorite tracks: Long Season

Least favorite track: Long Season

# Aidan's review

Kinda funny that we have a solid number of dub influenced albums on this list but not a single reggae or dub album from Jamaica. Regardless, this is just an immensely pleasurable album to listen to, with a tremendous rhythm section and just enough going on the entire time to keep it moving while also allowing you to enjoy the terrific production. It's a really fun album with a lot of random production experimentation, from whistles to static to the fuzzed out drums in the middle. Nothing else really sound like it. The only real weakness is that the middle section roughly from 17:30 to 21:00 gets bogged down a little bit, but the album gets right back on track after it. The vocals are solid and give the whole album an air of genuine fun with the scattered refrains and cries. It's not quite as good as the live version in my book, but there's no lesser songs tacked onto it, so it comes out even overall. I've loved this album for a long time, and happy to finally listen to it again.
