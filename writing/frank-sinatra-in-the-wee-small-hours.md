# Write-up

Frank Sinatra was a jazz and traditional pop singer born in 1915 in Hoboken, New Jersey. He is one of the most famous musicians of all time. He was born to Italian immigrants in tenement housing. A difficult birth scarred his face for life. His illiterate father and domineering mother ensured he got an education, but Sinatra ultimately chose music over school, looking up to Bing Crosby. After working odd jobs, he forced his way into vocal trio 3 Flashes in 1935, becoming the Hoboken Four. They won a radio contest and toured the US, giving Sinatra a taste of fame, but he was forced out and went back to odd jobs. Sinatra was hired to sing for trombonist Tommy Dorsey’s swing band in 1940. The band’s young drummer, Buddy Rich, would go on to become one of the most legendary drummers of the 20th century. Sinatra had his first hits with Dorsey’s band. Their fourth single “I’ll Never Smile Again” took the #1 spot on the first ever Billboard chart. By 1941, Sinatra had become quite popular, amassing a fanbase of teenage girls known as “bobby-soxers”. “Sinatramania” hit overdrive in 1943 after he left Dorsey’s band and started a solo residency in NYC. After dozens of hits, he reached his first peak in 1945. His debut album, 1946’s *The Voice of Frank Sinatra*, was one of the first true “albums” ever released. Sadly, a new generation of Sinatra clones crowded out his popularity by 1949; his alleged mafia ties (partly anti-Italian racism, but mostly true) and his divorce didn’t help. At rock bottom, Sinatra moved to Las Vegas in 1951 to perform concerts for as few as 150 people. Almost 40 and a washed-up has-been, Sinatra took a role in the 1953 film *From Here to Eternity*, winning an Oscar. He parlayed this into a recording contract and began working with arranger Nelson Riddle. Sinatra released two albums in 1954, resulting in two top 5 singles and a full popularity resurgence.

In 1955, Sinatra released his ninth album, and first on a 12” LP, *In the Wee Small Hours*. A set of standards all concerning broken hearts and nighttime melancholy, it is often considered the first concept album. Sinatra had recently separated from his second wife, actress Ava Gardner, and his sadness inspired what he nicknamed “the Ava album”. He recorded in late night sessions with Riddle where he allowed more experimentation than on his singles. The title track had been written earlier that year and was introduced by Sinatra; every other song was a standard from 1929-1942. These standards were all written in either Broadway or Tin Pan Alley, the commercial music writers of NYC who wrote the original pop music. The popularity of swing in the 1930s heavily influenced this traditional pop, resulting in Sinatra’s style of vocal jazz in the 40s and 50s. The album was Sinatra’s highest charting since 1947, popularizing the 12” LP (now synonymous with “album”). Tom Waits, Marvin Gaye, and Elvis Costello have all named it among their favorite albums.

This era, the “Golden Age”, culminated in 1959’s *Come Dance With Me!*, the most commercially successful record of his career. He left his label in 1960 to start his own, Reprise Records, which estranged him from Riddle. Reprise was a massive success, kickstarting the career of Quincy Jones among others. Sinatra reached his second peak of popularity in 1965 and climaxed with his 1969 recording of “My Way”, his now signature song. Unfortunately, rock music began to fully overtake Sinatra and other crooners. He “retired” in 1970 but returned to music in 1973 and spent the decade recording nostalgic tunes and performing for world leaders at massive charity events. He had a rare late career classic with the 1980 song “Theme from New York, New York”. He spent the 80s racking up achievement awards and performing despite a failing voice. He spent the 90s dealing with serious health issues, singing for the final time in 1995. He died of a heart attack at age 82 in 1998.

# Hargrove's review

“My Way” is an unbelievable banger, but otherwise I had never actually listened to Sinatra. His presence on this list feels like kind of an anachronism; he might be the only artist on the list to fall outside the “hip music” tradition of rock/folk/bebop-derived jazz. There are plenty of albums on this list that can be described as pop, but virtually all of them bear the distinct influence of rock, even someone like Michael Jackson. Sinatra’s pre-rock n roll pop, on the other hand, almost feels like music out of a different world to me. It sounds dated in a way that early Beatles albums or even Elvis Presley does not. In a way, that made *In the Wee Small Hours* one of the more interesting listens for me, lacking the tropes and conventions I hear in 99% of the music I listen to. Overall, I thought it was…decent, fine, alright. I was quite pleased when I realized that this was like an actual, honest-to-God album and not a collection of Sinatra singles like I had assumed it would be going in. I love the thematic cohesion, with nearly every song evoking the same dour mood. Sinatra looks so fucking cool on the cover of this album, and listening to it makes me feel like I’m out smoking a cigarette with him in Las Vegas at 2:00 AM. On the other hand, I wasn’t terribly impressed with most of the standards themselves. Outside of a select few, the melodies, chords, and lyrics just were not super memorable. Make the Great American Songbook Great Again (MGASGA). Instead, I derived most of my enjoyment from Sinatra’s delivery and the arrangements. In my review I just did for the Who, I complained that they often sound too theatrical for my taste. By contrast, this album is cinematic; the highly dynamic, flowing, almost arhythmic string sections score a noir film that by its nature can be much more calm and staid than anything on Broadway. I’m saying a lot of nice things here about an album that I don’t think I’ll much go back to, and that will be reflected in my ranking. That being said, I’m glad my visit down Sinatra’s way was a coherent, impressive artistic statement instead of just a history lesson.

As a song, “In the Wee Small Hours of the Morning” is easily the strongest on this album. It’s much catchier and more interesting than any of the standards on the album. I love how Sinatra holds his vocals on “count” in “And never, ever think of counting sheep”. The rhythm on this album as a whole is very loose, and Sinatra’s command of his own timing creates a lot of the best moments. “Mood Indigo” is my favorite of the standards. The melody whenever he says “mood indigo” has been stuck in my head for days. I also just like the line “You ain't been blue 'til you've had that mood indigo”. “Glad to Be Unhappy” has some nice guitar and a couple good lines but overall is not too memorable. “I Get Along Without You Very Well” opens with highly cinematic strings and continues a great call-and-response between Sinatra and the violin. The lyrics are written very well, particularly the verse “I get along without you very well, of course, I do/Except perhaps in spring/But I should never think of spring/For that would surely break my heart in two”. I adore the storytelling with the cigarette in “Deep in a Dream”, and the way Sinatra sings We glide through a lover's refrain” has really stuck with me. “I See Your Face Before Me” is fine, it has a dynamic arrangement and loud vocals from Sinatra but is just fine. The intro of “Can’t We Be Friends” catches my attention but it doesn’t hold it into the main song. “When Your Lover Has Gone” ends side 1 nice and breezily.

The clarinet that opens “What Is This Thing Called Love?” is a real album highlight for me. Sinatra matches the energy to make this one of the best tracks. Sinatra’s delivery of the bridge on “Last Night When We Were Young” moves me, but it’s otherwise not a great track. I really like the arrangement on “I’ll Be Around”, particularly the chord progression on the verses. The chord change on the 6th beat of the recurring verse melody sounds a half step below where I expect it to be, and I really like that. I like the jazziness of “Ill Wind” (there’s even a sax solo), but I dislike the indirectness of the lyrics. The frankness about relationships has been nice, so I’m not crazy about the hiding behind metaphors. “It Never Entered My Mind” has some funny lyrics, particularly “You had what I lack myself/Now I even have to scratch my back myself“. Sinatra’s delivery of that line also blows me away each time. I found most of the tracks inoffensive and difficult to distinguish, but “Dancing on the Ceiling” is my least favorite because it’s the only one that doesn’t actually deal with a break-up. It very much sounds like the lover he’s imagining is still with him! I do not appreciate the thematic inconsistency. “I’ll Never Be the Same” is largely forgettable. “This Love of Mine” closes things quite well though, with lots of strings and serious force behind Sinatra’s voice. I love the line “It's lonesome through the day, but, oh, the night”.

Really, I’m just pleasantly surprised that I respect this record. I was so worried that it would shatter Sinatra’s aura. Thankfully, it still lets me see him as super cool. In fact, it reinforces that image. Just not a musical style that I get a ton out of, though.

Favorite tracks: In the Wee Small Hours of the Morning, Mood Indigo, I Get Along Without You Very Well, Deep in a Dream, What Is This Thing Called Love?, I’ll Be Around, It Never Entered My Mind, This Love of Mine

Least favorite track: Dancing on the Ceiling

# Aidan's review

Along with MJ and the Beatles, Sinatra is one of the most culturally important artists on this list. At only 118 pounds, Old Blue Eyes managed to seem like more of a man than anyone but John Wayne. He's also possibly the only non-jazz artist on this list who did not write a single one of their songs. The artistry is in his voice and what a voice it is. "Sinatra with a cold is Picasso without paint, Ferrari without fuel - only worse". Sinatra seems like he's been cut out of some storybook, from his features to his generosity, to his perfectionism to his temper. I think he's best when his emotions are a tad more tinged with irony, we get a taste of it on "I Get Along Without You Very Well", but "Something Stupid" and "I Get a Kick Out of You" are so good that I wish there was just a tad more edge. The instrumentation feels extremely cinematic, and I think at times something sparser would better highlight The Voice.
