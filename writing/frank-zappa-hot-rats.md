# Write-up

Frank Zappa was a American musician from Baltimore, Maryland. As a child, Zappa was often sick, possibly due to his exposure to mercury that his father brought home from his job and radium from the doctors treating him (the 50s were very pro-nuclear). He also developed a strong interest in music, espeically modern composers and black R&B guitarists. On his 15th Birthday, Zappa's mother arranged for him to call one of his idols, modern composer Edgard Varése. Varése never picked up the phone, but eventually wrote a letter to Zappa, which Zappa would keep for the rest of his life. In high school, Zappa befriended Don Glen Vliet (better known as Don Van Vliet/Captain Beefheart). The two would go on to be heavy collaborators throughout each of their careers. Zappa started writing his own songs in high school and credits some of his high school music teachers on his first album, "Freak Out!". After high school, he dropped out of college, and found sporadic work as a copywriter, songwriter, film composer and musician. He eventually scraped together enough money to buy a studio from a friend, and moved in, often 12 hours a day, experimenting and recording the music of his friends. Due to a misunderstanding, the police suspected that he was making pornographic films in the studio, and he was propositioned in a sting operation. Zappa was offered $100 ($1000 today) for a raunchy audio tape, which he made (clothed) with a female friend. He was promptly arrested and briefly put in jail, and the police seized most of the recordings he had made. While he only spent 10 days in prison, Zappa's hatred of government and authority would last the rest of his life.

After being arrested, Zappa was asked by one of his friends to join an R&B band, the Soul Giants, which had kicked out their original guitarist. Zappa accepted and quickly managed to gain control of the band, changing its name to "The Mothers" (due to their first gig being on Mother's Day) and playing Zappa's own compositions. They quickly established themselves on the LA underground scene and attracted the attention of Tom Wilson, producer for Dylan and Simon & Garfunkel. Wilson signed them to MGM, which forced them to change their name to avoid the resemblance to a shortening of "Motherfuckers", so Zappa's band became "The Mothers of Invention". Wilson produced their first album, "Freak Out". "Freak Out" was a groundbreaking experimental rock album, combining standard rock with more avant-garde genres such as musique concréte and sound collages. The album only sold well in Europe but it gained a cult following on both sides of the Atlantic, and has since gained recognition as one of the greatest records ever. Zappa continued to put out music with the Mothers. All of their albums tended to be satirical, boasting titles like "We're Only In It For the Money", "Cruising with Ruben & the Jets" and "Lumpy Gravy"

In 1966, the Mothers moved to NYC for a regular gig at Garrick Theater, which was part of Warhol's orbit and sat over a popular club, Cafe au Go Go. It is now an apartment building siting over a CVS. Zappa began to have more commercial ventures, forming his own record label, making a film for one of their albums and appearing on the Monkees TV show. Eventually, the band moved back to Los Angeles, and due to a lack of financial success, Zappa disbanded the group. He then made Hot Rats. Hot Rats was a stark divergence from the style of music the Mothers had played. It was mostly instrumental, had nothing satirical about it and was recorded on Zappa's own custom (and cutting edge) 16 track recording set-up. It featured two members of the Mothers, Ian Underwood and Lowell George, and a host of other musicians, including 15-year old bassist Shuggie Otis. The album was dedicated to Zappa's son, Dweezil (yes that's his real name). We will continue with Zappa when we cover the last album of the Mothers of Invention (which came out just before Hot Rats), Uncle Meat.

We left off with Zappa with the dissolution of the Mothers of Invention and the release of Hot Rats. While in NYC, Zappa began trying to make a movie, titled Uncle Meat. The movie, which eventually came out in 1987, blurred the lines between documentary and fiction, featured a section about the making of the movie and starred the Mothers of Invention. The music for the album was recorded in the same sessions as the Mothers' previous three albums, We're Only In It for the Money, Lumpy Gravy and Cruising with Ruben & The Jets. When the album was rereleased on CD in 1987 to go with the movie, it was augmented with 40 minutes of audio clips from the movie. The album (in both versions) also featured several live recordings of the Mothers performing. After Uncle Meat was released in 1969, Zappa broke up the band due to financial concerns and dissatisfaction with its members. In the following year, he released two more albums from previous recordings the Mothers had done.

Zappa reformed the band in 1970, featuring only one of the original members, Ian Underwood on keyboard. It also featured three members of the Turtles, a band best known for their song "Happy Together". They put out two studio albums and a film, 200 Motels, before going on tour in Europe. While on tour, the band was beset by misfortune. First, while performing in a casino in Switzerland, a fan fired a flare gun, causing a fire and burning up all of the Mothers' equipment. The band Deep Purple was intending to record there the following week, and ended up writing the song "Smoke on the Water" about the fire, which became their best known song. A week after the fire, the band played in London, where a fan, enraged by his girlfriend's infatuation with Zappa, pushed him off the stage, nearly killing Zappa. Zappa took a year to recover, and was left with chronic pain, a deeper voice and legs of different lengths. He continued to record with the Mothers during his recovery, but the band eventually ended in 1975, although many of its members would go on to play with Zappa in later projects.

In the 80s, Zappa founded another label, Barking Pumpkin records and put out several instrumental albums as well as more of his usual fare of satirical rock records. He also put out his most successful song, "Valley Girl". He also remastered many of his old records for rerelease on Barking Pumpkin, including Uncle Meat. In 1983, he stated using an early prototype of a synthesizer called a Synclaiver. He would use it more and more after his diagnosis with incurable prostate cancer in 1990. His last public appearance was at the Frankfurt Festival, but by that point, the pain from his cancer made it difficult to perform. He died in 1993 at age 52.

# Hargrove's review

Frank Zappa is one of the largest artists who I have never once listened to and knew next-to-nothing about. Going in to this, I knew that he was kind of a comedy rock guy, he was relevant across multiple decades, and he produced *Trout Mask Replica*. Given all of that, *Hot Rats* was very much not the album I expected. It’s more or less just 40 straight minutes of instrumental jazz jamming. There’s no fluff, no distraction, no ego, just some good old-fashioned guitar wanking. I didn’t find it transcendent or anything, but I did find it quite enjoyable to listen to. I would get pretty zenned out each time and let it wash over me. Everything flows together nicely and each track feels connected to the last. Multiple tracks have a 70s TV show theme song feel to them, which I can’t imagine is a coincidence. The solos are consistently quite good, with the guitar a recurring highlight, but lots of good fiddle and winds as well. The melodies are a bit of a weak point in my opinion; they’re all nice enough while I listen, but this record really could have used an all-time memorable melody to kick it up into A Tier. As it is, *Hot Rats* is just kind of a fun, enjoyable, vibey jazz fusion record that I had a good time listening to and that I’m currently finding difficult to say things about.

“Peaches En Regalia” is definitely a good track, but I don’t entirely understand its status. It’s my third favorite on the album, as well as the one that most sounds like TV music. I like every timbre used and each solo, with the clarinet part being my favorite. I like “Willie the Pimp” even better. I’m a big fan of the Beefheart vocals because it’s just the perfect situation for them, appropriately bluesy and greasy. The central melody, whether in Beefheart’s vocals or the fiddle, has serious swagger. The extended instrumental section is full of super funky guitar solos and immaculate drumming. I particularly love the recapitulation of the fiddle at the end. The album peaks with “Son of Mr. Green Genes”. The opening section of this track is so grandiose that it wins me over right off the bat. There are a lot of great moments, and I feel satisfied when it ends. “Little Umbrellas” is a cooldown for the best part of the album. It’s the first true slow track, with a spy jazz feel. I especially like the flutes. “The Gumbo Variations” is the most pure jazz of all the tracks, reminding me of *Headhunters* in particular. The sax, fiddle, and guitar solos are all good in isolation, but they go on for way too long, and it makes this track feel incredibly bloated. I don’t love it. The album ends nice and chill with “It Must Be a Camel”. It’s probably the weirdest track, going all over the place and never falling into any one groove, but it’s consistently enjoyable to listen to.

This album is a solid addition to B Tier. Nothing revelatory, but another mood I have at my disposal when I want to.

Favorite tracks: Peaches En Regalia, Willie the Pimp, Son of Mr. Green Genes, Little Umbrellas

Least favorite track: The Gumbo Variations

# Aidan's review

For all of Zappa's reputation as a provocateur, this record goes down easy. Besides Captain Beefhart's brief intervention on "Willie the Pimp", the album sails along on Zappa's excellent guitar. For all of his skill, the best bits are supported by an excellent backing arrangement from Ian Underwood, particularly on the first three tracks. There are sins on the back half, "Little Umbrellas" gets aimless, "The Gumbo Variations" overdoes the sax in the beginning and the percussive sections on "It Must Be A Camel" aren't my speed, but the first three tracks are so good, they buy my goodwill for the rest of it when listening straight through. Enjoyable, blissful album.
