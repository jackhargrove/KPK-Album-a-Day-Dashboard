# Write-up

See above for the full history of Fugazi that I put together in my write-up for the band’s fourth album, *Red Medicine*. This write-up will focus narrowly on *Fugazi*, the group’s debut EP. *Fugazi*, often referred to as *7 Songs*, released in 1988 and features vocalist and guitarist Ian MacKaye, vocalist Guy Picciotto, bassist Joe Lally, and drummer Brendan Canty. MacKaye sings lead vocals on “Waiting Room”, “Bad Mouth”, and “Suggestion, while Picciotto sings lead vocals on “Bulldog Front”, “Burning”, “Give Me the Cure”, and “Glue Man”. Though Picciotto would play guitar alongside MacKaye on later Fugazi records, he does not do so here. Recorded at the legendary Inner Ear Studios in Arlington, Virginia, in 1988, the EP features the first examples of Fugazi’s mid-tempo blend of hardcore punk with influences from funk, ska, and reggae that would help form the basis for the sound of post-hardcore. The EP received critical acclaim upon release and has retrospectively been regarded as one of the most influential albums in all of post-hardcore, though Fugazi would intentionally limit its commercial success throughout its existence (see my previous write-up for more detail on that). After the release of Fugazi’s second EP, 1989’s *Margin Walker*, the band released a compilation of both EPs as *13 Songs*. This compilation has likely sold more than a million copies, with some sources estimating it at as many as three million copies sold, making it Fugazi’s best-selling album. Despite being a compilation, many point to it as one of punk’s greatest albums. Multiple songs on *Fugazi* have been covered by more famous bands, with Pearl Jam including “Suggestion” on its setlists at 90s concerts. However, the most well-known track by far is “Waiting Room”. Originally performed by Fugazi in 1987 before Picciotto had even joined the band, it was an easy choice to open Fugazi’s debut record. The track is particularly loved for the circular, funky bass line played by Joe Lally. A truly impressive number of artists have covered the track in the nearly four decades since its release, including Red Hot Chili Peppers, Arcade Fire, Ryan Adams, and TV on the Radio. It has become a stereotype in DC that bands on tour cover the song while they’re here. “Waiting Room” is also still played at nearly every Washington Commanders home game to this day.

# Hargrove's review

When we listened to *Red Medicine*, I found myself puzzled by how much I did not care for it. I’ve always strongly felt that I *should* like Fugazi: pioneering post-hardcore, spindly interlocking guitars, often aggressive and confrontational music, empathetic lyricism, and a legendary status within DC. But try as I might, I’ve never been able to connect with them. Thankfully, this EP has finally given me something I can appreciate from Fugazi. On the one hand, that makes me a little sad. Why does none of the band’s later work sound like this? Why do *Repeater*, *Red Medicine*, and *The Argument* leave me so cold? I like post-hardcore because while the spirit of hardcore punk appeals to me greatly, I find the sloppy and simplistic execution often uninteresting. Post-hardcore generally solves this problem by applying more complex compositional techniques, fuller arrangements, and better melodies. Ironically, though Fugazi was one of the first bands to have the idea to this, I just don’t think it’s all that good at it. The interwoven spindly guitars on *Red Medicine* pale in comparison to *Spiderland*. MacKaye’s aggressive vocals aren’t tailored to the music in the way that At the Drive-In’s are on *Relationship of Command*, while Picciotto’s less aggressive vocals can’t measure up to those on *Leaves Turn Inside You* by Unwound, a record I’m only mildly positive on. What makes this *Fugazi* debut EP work so well is how close it hews to the hardcore sound and formula. Sure, it’s noticeably an evolution from pure hardcore punk. I won’t dispute that. But it’s much less so than later Fugazi stuff or other great albums of post-hardcore. MacKaye and Lally both inject elements of ska and reggae on guitar and bass, respectively, and the songs have well-written choruses. Outside of that, though, the band sticks to what it’s made to do: deliver searing hardcore punk bangers. Canty delivers ear-piercing drumming, with cymbals crashing through the choruses like shattering glass. MacKaye’s hardcore grunting fits the music here much better than on *Red Medicine*, where he consistently sounds out of place to me. And, of course, Lally’s bass brings the most important contribution in bass lines as memorable as most song’s melodies. The weakest link here is clearly Picciotto; while he had clearly come into his own with the band by *Red Medicine*, his songwriting here is still a little immature, and his delivery pales in comparison to MacKaye’s. He’s not awful or anything, but when this record falters, it’s usually his fault. For the most part, though, this short EP is brimming with bangers that I had a fun time listening to again and again and again.

It pains me to admit it, but everyone was right about “Waiting Room”. It took me a few listens for it to really seep in, but the bass line and chorus melody have been permanently imprinted into my brain. That’s no small feat in a week spent listening to the immortal melodies of the Microphones and Songs: Ohia. MacKaye grunts out powerful lyrics over a hypnotic bass line and razor sharp cymbal crashes in the chorus. It even ends on a high note with one of the most energetic parts of the song. This is an easy add to my regular rotation. Picciotto hasn’t fully found his voice on this album, but “Bulldog Front” is a great addition to the track list. MacKaye brings a wonderful post-punk riff in the verses before a shockingly pop punk guitar riff in the explosive chorus. I particularly like MacKaye’s backing vocals. MacKaye contributes his second of three bangers with “Bad Mouth”. I like how the lyrical theme of “You can't be what you were/So you better start being just what you are/You can't be what you were/The time is now, it's running out, it's running out, it's running, running, running out/You can't be what you were/So you better start living the life that you're talking about” complements “Waiting Room”. The ska influence is pronounced in the guitar, making it sound straight up like skate punk. I’ve never really thought of Fugazi as particularly influential on pop punk, but you can hear it clearly here. The drums sound shrill in the best way. While I like the click-clacking of drumsticks and wonderful dissonant guitar on “Burning”, Picciotto’s awkwardness in singing and songwriting becomes too noticeable here. There’s a lot to like about the track, and it’s certainly memorable, but it always leaves me unsatisfied and wanting more. Picciotto still leaves a little to be desired on “Give Me the Cure”, but Lally’s bass line and MacKaye’s intense strumming are enough to win me over. I love how MacKaye’s guitar grows to a fever pitch both within the chorus and progressively through the song, eventually accruing intense momentum. “Suggestion” begins with a wonderful intro that grows into another heavily reggae-inspired track. Lally once again goes crazy on bass. What happened to these bass lines in *Red Medicine*? There’s another heavy, pop punk-adjacent chorus with a stellar descending guitar riff. I quite like the transition to “Glue Man”, but unfortunately it’s by far my least favorite track. It starts so strong with pulsating guitar distortion and driving drums, but Picciotto frankly sounds awful here, his voice serrated like a kitchen knife with nothing to slice through as the instrumental goes quiet. I could see one of his better vocal performances from *Red Medicine* making this a great song but, as it is, I dread the ending to this record.

I enjoyed listening to this record a lot this week. It has a crazy high hit rate for such a short album. Unfortunately, as I’ve done with super short albums like mbv’s *you made me realise* in the past, I have to knock it down a peg. It’s just easier to make a consistent project when it’s so short and doesn’t feel fair to compare to full LPs. As a result, I’m placing it in high B Tier instead of low A Tier.

Favorite tracks: Waiting Room, Bulldog Front, Bad Mouth, Give Me the Cure, Suggestion

Least favorite track: Glue Man

# Aidan's review

I've never been a huge fan of the DC punk output, but the guitar lines on this are great. "Waiting Room" is obviously great, but I was surprised how much I liked the rest of the album. Post-hardcore will never be the most intricately lyrical genre, but I really like the line "I never thought of dying before/I never sucked on the dying" off of "Give Me the Cure". My favorite moments on the record are the calmer ones. The pause in "Waiting Room", the guitar solo when the drums stop for a second in "Bad Mouth", and the buildups to every single track. "Bulldog Front" in particular starts off sounding a lot like a Strokes song and takes whole quarter of its runtime before crashing into a very punk chorus. I slightly prefer Picciotto's vocals to MacKaye's more yelling style, but neither is poorly suited for this album. It's a fun, groovy, tightly packed 25 minutes without a bad song. This is what a punk album should be like.
