# Write-up

Fugazi is a post-hardcore band formed in 1986 in Washington, DC consisting of guitarist and vocalist Ian MacKaye, guitarist and vocalist Guy Picciotto, bassist Joe Lally, and drummer Brendan Canty. It was formed from the ashes of two highly influential hardcore groups, Minor Threat and Rites of Spring. Ian MacKaye grew up in DC and was introduced to Sex Pistols and other early British punk bands at age 16 in 1978. Local DC group Bad Brains pioneered an extremely fast and loud style that would become hardcore punk, causing MacKaye and some friends from high school to form Teen Idles in 1979 with a similar sound. The band broke up after one year and one EP, but they were the first DC hardcore group to tour on the West Coast (the other stronghold of early hardcore). MacKaye formed and fronted Minor Threat in 1980; though it only released three EPs from 1980-1983, it became the quintessential East Coast hardcore group of the era, comparable to LA’s Black Flag (who were fronted by MacKaye’s childhood friend Henry Rollins). MacKaye’s DIY record label Dischord became the center of the DC sound and scene. As MacKaye famously abstains from drugs, alcohol, and casual sex, Minor Threat also inadvertently spawned the hardcore subculture of Straight Edge. By 1983, the DC hardcore scene had attracted violent, mindless bigots, causing MacKaye to dissolve Minor Threat and step back from the scene. In 1985, MacKaye and other Dischord employees decided to rejuvenate DC hardcore by kicking out the assholes and making more innovative music; this movement became known as Revolution Summer. The most important Revolution Summer band was Rites of Spring, with Picciotto on vocals and Canty on drums. Rites of Spring only released one album in 1985, but Picciotto’s sad lyrics, wailing delivery, and melodic style was dubbed “emotional hardcore” or “emocore”, the origin of the emo genre.

In 1986, MacKaye, Lally, and drummer Colin Sears started Fugazi with the intent to expand hardcore through funk and reggae influences and progressive song structures. Canty replaced Sears on drums in 1987, causing his former bandmate Picciotto to hang around Fugazi rehearsals. He eventually wore MacKaye down, who accepted him as a second lead vocalist in 1988 with the intention of having a hip hop hype man dynamic; instead, they ended up alternating vocals and lyric-writing. Fugazi released its first EP *Fugazi* (also on our list) in 1988 and its second *Margin Walker* in 1989, combining the two into a compilation album known as *13 Songs*. After touring, Picciotto felt underutilized, so he started playing guitar as well. The intertwining of MacKaye’s low-pitched, chunky riffs with Picciotto’s high-pitched melodies became a Fugazi trademark. Fugazi released its debut LP *Repeater* in 1990, a record still considered by many to be their best. Since then, they’ve released five more LPs, with their last being 2001’s highly acclaimed *The Argument*. They became quite popular in the 90s but refused to ever sign to a major label despite 8-figure offers because of their commitment to their anti-commercial principles. They famously would charge only $5 for tickets to their shows, even when they could have sold out large venues with ticket prices an order of magnitude higher. Fugazi (along with Slint) helped standardize the sound of post-hardcore in the 90s, which previously ranged from Minutemen’s goofy funk punk to Big Black’s industrial rock. Bands that followed in their footsteps included Nirvana, At the Drive-In, American Football, the Dismemberment Plan, Modest Mouse, and many others. They have a truly ridiculous fanbase of musicians both before and after their time, including Elliott Smith, Johnny Marr of the Smiths, Efrim Menuck of Godspeed You! Black Emperor, Joe Strummer of the Clash, and Lorde. For all intents and purposes, Fugazi broke up in 2003, but the members still insist they’re just on hiatus and that they often play together privately for fun.

*Red Medicine* is Fugazi’s fourth album, released in 1995. It marked the beginning of the band’s second half, where they began incorporating more experimentation that combined post-hardcore sound to the approach of art rock. These early experiments included use of sampling, non-standard instrumentation, alternate tunings, and ambient techniques. They would continue this new approach through the 90s, culminating with *The Argument*. *Red Medicine* charted on the Billboard 200 and received rave reviews, often being considered their masterpiece by critics in the 90s and early 2000s before *The Argument* released and *Repeater* grew in reputation. Picciotto sings lead vocals on “Do You Like Me”, “Latest Disgrace”, “Forensic Scene”, “Fell, Destroyed”, “Target”, and “Downed City”, while MacKaye does so on “Bed for the Scraping”, “Birthday Pony”, “Back to Base”, and “Long Distance Runner”. Lally sings lead vocals for the first time on “By You”. The album cover includes an upside down photo of the band members swimming in a lake, an intentional reference to Slint’s *Spiderland*.

# Hargrove's review

Man, I so wish I liked Fugazi. I adore post-hardcore as a genre, with many of my favorite albums being post-hardcore or downstream of it like emo. I love aggressive vocals, cathartic lyrics, dissonant dueling guitars, and grim atmospheres. Given Fugazi’s unrivaled influence on the genre from the 90s onward, it’s a band I’ve tried to get into in the past. I also respect the members’ sincere, steadfast, nearly psychotic commitment to their principles, whether they be abstaining from alcohol or turning down generational wealth. And, they’re from DC! But, fuck, I just don’t like their music all that much. I mean, it’s fine; *Red Medicine* isn’t offensive by any means, nor is *Repeater*, nor is *The Argument*, the three Fugazi albums I’ve listened to. They all have competent playing, high points, and strong atmospheres. And yet, they lack memorable, impactful, good songs. Some tracks stick more than others, sure, but the songwriting is just so lackluster. I don’t know what exactly they do wrong. Maybe it’s the simplicity that just makes them sound like a nameless four-piece guitar band instead of seminal post-hardcore pioneers. Maybe MacKaye’s genuine hardcore background left him without the ability to write above replacement level rock songs. I still haven’t heard “Waiting Room”, so I’ll hold off complete judgment until we listen to the *Fugazi* EP. As of right now, however, they are unmoved from my “influential but not worth listening to” bucket.

“Do You Like Me” begins with a bunch of feedback, but the song that begins in earnest is one of the better ones. I prefer Picciotto’s vocals and he kills it here, chanting “Lockheed, Lockheed, Martin Marietta” in a way that gets lodged in my brain. He wrote a catchy hook too, and I’m a sucker for a Bethesda shout out. “Bed for the Scraping” is upbeat, has razor sharp and super fast guitar lines, and is certainly memorable. It would be the best track if I didn’t find MacKaye’s vocal performance so grating. I don’t dislike his voice generally, I just really don’t like it here. With that in mind, it’s still pretty good, but handicapped. “Latest Disgrace” has a great aimless ascending guitar line in the chorus, but otherwise it’s pretty unremarkable. After a minute of bullshit, “Birthday Pony” turns into a pretty good song that’s perfectly tailored to MacKaye’s voice. I like the choppy groove and the catchy hook. Fugazi cooks up a banger chorus on ”Forensic Scene” that gets completely undercut by the awful verses. They’re based around this chord progression that reminds me of Fiona Apple’s “Waltz (Better than Fine)”, but wow does it not work here. It kills all momentum. “Combination Lock” is the album’s low-point, an instrumental time-waster built around a breakbeat. I like “Fell, Destroyed” a lot though. It sounds like it could be an Unwound or even a Slint song with those loose, dangly, jagged, dissonant guitars, as well as the sharp stabs in the (frustratingly underwhelming) chorus. It’s the platonic ideal of great instrumentation with complete dud songwriting. “By You” continues this with its cool atmosphere enveloping five minutes of effectively dead air. It’s cool that “Version” has a clarinet, but it adds little. My favorite song on the whole has to be “Target”. It’s the perfect Picciotto track, allowing him to wail “Skin is cracked and I realize/That I hate the sound of guitars” over a song structure that reminds me of NEU!’s “Hero” off *NEU! ’75*. I also like the lyrics about the pretense of 90s alternative. “Back to Base”, a song that keeps things short and rough, suits MacKaye’s voice. It’s fine but not great. “Downed City” drowns Picciotto’s vocals in instrumentation, while “Long Distance Runner” provides a fittingly formless, messy closer.

I wanted to like this album so bad when Aidan sent me this week’s albums last Saturday. I just don’t. On the plus side, I did go back to basically every post-hardcore album we’ve listened to so far because of it. This is long overdue, but I’m moving *Relationship of Command* up a tier. After hearing it for the first time in September, it’s quickly climbed the ranks to equal some of my favorite albums since childhood. I also enjoyed *Leaves Turn Inside You* much more this time around, so I’m bumping it up a bit as well.

Favorite tracks: Do You Like Me, Bed for the Scraping, Birthday Pony, Fell, Destroyed, Target

Least favorite track: Combination Lock
