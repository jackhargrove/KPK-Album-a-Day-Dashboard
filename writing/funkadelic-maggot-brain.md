# Write-up

Funkadelic was a funk rock band formed in Plainfield, New Jersey in 1968. While the group acted as a collective without set membership over time, this album features bandleader and vocalist George Clinton, keyboardist and vocalist Bernie Worrell, lead guitarist and vocalist Eddie Hazel, guitarist and vocalist Tawl Ross, bassist and vocalist Billy Bass Nelson, drummer Tiki Fulwood, vocalist Raymond Davis, and backing vocalists Fuzzy Haskins, Calvin Simon, Grady Thomas, Garry Shider, Pat Lewis, Diane Lewis, and Rose Williams. Along with Parliament, Funkadelic was one of the two groups led by George Clinton who, alongside James Brown and Sly Stone, was one of the primary forefathers of funk music. Clinton was born in 1941 and raised in Plainfield, where he formed a doo-wop vocal quintet called the Parliaments in 1956. Clinton owned a barbershop in Plainfield known as the “Silk Palace” which became a major hangout for the burgeoning soul and proto-funk scene. By the 1960s, the Parliaments began playing soul music, adding a five-piece rhythm section to complement the five singers in 1964. Motown Records hired Clinton as a songwriter, a job he performed in addition to his band work. The Parliaments finally hit the charts after over a decade with “(I Wanna) Testify”, released on Revilot Records in 1967; due to travel conflicts, Clinton was the only member of the group to perform on the recording. Despite this success, Revilot went bankrupt, and legal disputes prevented Clinton from using the name “the Parliaments”. As a result, Clinton renamed the group Funkadelic in 1968, with only the rhythm section as full-fledged members initially. This name change coincided with a change in musical direction toward funk rock inspired by Jimi Hendrix and Sly Stone, as well as relocation from New Jersey to Detroit. Then, in 1970, Clinton launched Parliament as his less rock-oriented, more commercial funk project with the vocal quintet from the Parliaments added back as members. Though Parliament and Funkadelic were marketed as different groups, they featured the same lineup and are together referred to as the Parliament-Funkadelic collective, or P-Funk for short. The first record released from the new P-Funk collective was 1970’s *Funkadelic*, followed the same year by Parliament’s *Osmium*. Not long after, Parliament was forced into hiatus, leading Clinton to focus on Funkadelic for a few years. *Free Your Mind… and Your Ass Will Follow*, Funkadelic’s second album, also released in 1970.

Funkadelic’s third and most famous album, *Maggot Brain*, was released in 1971. The album is best known for its title track, a 10-minute epic which revolves around a guitar solo played by Eddie Hazel. As the story goes, Clinton dropped acid and then instructed Hazel to play as if his mother had just died. Clinton heavily edited the recording in production, removing accompanying musicians from the mix and adding delay effects for a psychedelic feel. The album’s cover, featuring model Barbara Cheesborough’s screaming, disembodied head, gave Funkadelic an edgy reputation with the general public. The photo was taken by Joel Brodsky, who previously shot the famous “Young Lion” photographs of Jim Morrison that accompanied the Doors’ self-titled debut album. While *Maggot Brain* initially received mixed reviews, it has since gained a reputation as the greatest example of psychedelic funk. The title track has proven incredibly influential on a wide range of musicians; Dean Ween of Ween and J. Mascis of Dinosaur Jr. have both played the solo in full, while the Mars Volta included a solo inspired by it on the album after *Frances the Mute*. André 3000 of OutKast also cites it as one of his favorite records.

Shortly after the release of *Maggot Brain*, the original lineup of Funkadelic collapsed as Ross, Nelson, and Hazel all left. In 1972, Clinton poached bassist Bootsy Collins from James Brown’s backing band. Collins became Clinton’s second-in-command of the P-Funk collective and one of the biggest names in funk. After a four-year hiatus, Clinton revived Parliament again in 1974 as an outlet for the collective’s smoother, less guitar-focused funk music. Parliament started getting significant mainstream attention in 1975 with the album *Mothership Connection*, which peaked at #13 on the Billboard 200. This album also began the P-Funk “mythology”, an expansive science fiction lore about aliens and outer space that was developed on future P-Funk records and heavily influenced Afrofuturism. Funkadelic reached its commercial peak in 1978 with *One Nation Under a Groove*, which peaked at #16 on the Billboard 200. P-Funk began to fall apart at the end of the 70s as membership became a revolving door and Clinton became more tyrannical. Clinton dissolved Parliament and Funkadelic as legal entities in 1981 and formed George Clinton and the P-Funk All-Stars, though many of the members remained the same. P-Funk continues in this form today. The 80s were a nadir for the group, but significant interest returned in the 90s when West Coast hip hop producers led by Dr. Dre created G-funk using P-Funk samples. Original Funkadelic keyboardist Bernie Worrell became a de facto member of Talking Heads in 1982, performing on *Speaking in Tongues* and the concert film *Stop Making Sense*.

# Hargrove's review

I’ve been really looking forward to this album because I love funk as a sound, but I’ve never had the chance to actually systematically dig into the work of funk titans like P-Funk. For better or for worse, though, *Maggot Brain* isn’t an archetypal funk album in the way that P-Funk’s later work would be (I listened to Parliament’s *Mothership Connection* and Funkadelic’s *One Nation Under a Groove* this week to get a sense of golden age P-Funk; both were quite good, but particularly *Mothership Connection*, which has so broken through into the public consciousness that I recognized more than half of its content). Instead, I would break it into two parts: “Maggot Brain”, which belongs to the psychedelic hard rock world of Jimi Hendrix, and the rest of the album, which belong to the progressive and psychedelic soul sounds that presaged funk in earnest à la Sly Stone or even Marvin Gaye. These latter tracks have a sleazier feel than other soul artists, certainly, and “You and Your Folks, Me and My Folks” in particular builds the rhythm section that I think of in my head as the defining factor of funk, but the barbershop vocals consistently remind me of how early 1971 still was. This isn’t a bad thing at all. In fact, I like the last six tracks more than I like the opener. I’ll get into that more in the next paragraph, but while I respect the hell out of “Maggot Brain”, it’s just not designed to move my particular sensibilities. On the other hand, the entire run of “Can You Get to That” to “Super Stupid” is pure bliss for me. The combination of strong rhythms with complex vocal harmonizations and just enough edge was designed in a lab to put me in a good mood. Unfortunately, this album’s inconsistency is its greatest pitfall, as the final two tracks just don’t work for me much. But for four beautiful songs, this record puts me at peace in a style that I love but that this list largely neglects.

There are a lot of things to love about “Maggot Brain”. I can’t say that I fully understand it, but George Clinton sounds cool as funk in the opening narration, and the “come on, Maggot Brain” comes in at just the right time. There’s also the rising and falling arpeggio pattern in the left ear that reminds me of “I Want You (She’s So Heavy)” which provides as good of a solo accompaniment as any guitarist can ask for. Moving to the main event, I simply cannot deny that Hazel fucking shreds the guitar. The psychedelic effects Clinton adds in post make it all the better, emphasizing the already considerable expressive power of the solo. But sadly, I’m just not someone who gets too excited by a guitar solo on its own. I love solos on any instrument that complement a song, as when I raved about the keyboard and guitar solos in the middle of “Light My Fire” by the Doors last week. But as the song itself? A guitar solo will always leave something to be desired for me if it’s the main event instead of a side dish. All respect in the world to people who love it, but I am who I am. Instead, I find myself much more excited by the barbershop vocal layering on “Can You Get to That”. I particularly love the bass vocals, both in the chorus and especially when it takes over for one of the verses. “Can you get to that?” is just a great phrase to that I want to work into my lexicon. What a fun track. “Hit It and Quit It” is another banger. I love the funky ass guitar riff, the amazing dynamic shifts in the vocals, and, more than anything, the way this track struts around with such swagger. The hits keep rolling with “You and Your Folks, Me and My Folks”, which brings the densest rhythmic groove yet. It’s got slower drums, loud bass, emphatic vocals, and a catchy hook. What’s not to like? This run climaxes with “Super Stupid”, my favorite track on the album. This track uses a guitar solo as part of a greater whole in exactly the way I wanted out of “Maggot Brain”. On top of that, Hazel’s vocals match his shredding, telling a perfect story. I don’t know what the bouncy percussion is on “Back in Our Minds”, but I like it. That being said, I don’t like much else about this track. It’s not an interesting song and it lacks energy. I find “Wars of Armageddon” a frustrating missed opportunity. It’s so cool, for the most part! The cowbell beat drives everything else, creating the platform for slow instrumental layering and enjoyable sound effects. But then, near the end, there’s just a minute of fart noises that kills my vibe. It’s so needles and unpleasant! It’s a shame because otherwise this track combines the progressive structure of “Maggot Brain” with the funk rhythm section of the rest of the album and could easily have been my favorite.

Overall, the part of this album I most enjoyed is directly comparable in sound to Marvin Gaye’s *What’s Going On*. I have to give the edge to Gaye’s record, as its highs are similarly high, but it’s far more consistent throughout. *Maggot Brain* instead will sit somewhere in the top half of B Tier. I highly recommend anyone and everyone check out Parliament’s *Mothership Connection*, which might just be the quintessential funk album. More importantly, it’s a damn fun listen.

Favorite tracks: Can You Get to That, Hit It and Quit It, You and Your Folks, Me and My Folks, Super Stupid

Least favorite track: Back in Our Minds
