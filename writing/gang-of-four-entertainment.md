# Write-up

In many ways, Gang of Four were the prototypical post-punk band. They were art school kids. Their lead singer, Jon King, went to private (public in the UK) school in Kent with bandmate Andy Gill, documentarian Adam Curtis and three members of fellow left-wing post-punk band, the Mekons. While Curtis left to go to Oxford, the rest of the bunch attended college in Leeds, in the north of England. There, a loose collective formed of left wing students playing post-punk music. Out of that collective sprung the Mekons, Delta 5 and the topic of today, Gang of Four. The band took its name from the recently ousted hardline "Gang of Four" in China which consisted of Zhang Chunqiao, Yao Wenyuan, Wang Hongwen and Mao's widow, Jiang Qing. "Jon King" sounded enough like "Jiang Qing" to make a joke, and from there, a band was formed, with King on vocals, Gill on guitar, Hugo Burnham on drums and Dave Wolfson initially on bass, to be replaced by Dave Allen.

The band got signed in the frenzy to sign the next Clash and had early success with their singles "Damaged Goods", "Anthrax" and "Armalite Rifle", garnering them an invite to a BBC live music show, which could have made them household names. Right before playing, the band was asked to change "rubbers" to "rubbish" in one of their songs, and walked off the show. The band would never again come as close to stardom, although they did managed to get censored again with their anti-military single "I Love A Man In Uniform" during the Falklands war. Entertainment! was their debut album and featured most of their songs up to that point. It’s accrued significant respect over the years. Kurt Cobain listed it among his favorite albums, Frank Ocean has sampled “Anthrax”, Run The Jewels has sampled “Ether” and Sofia Coppola featured “Natural’s Not In It” in “Marie Antoinette. After their debut album, Gill and King, the two creative forces behind the band began to argue, causing the band to split up and several times, releasing 9 albums along the way, none of which hold a candle to Entertainment! Gill was one of the first victims of COVID during a tour in China, while Dave Allen died in April of last year. The band has mixed up its lineup on virtually every reformation, including Slint alumnus David Pajo, indie rocker Ted Leo and Bowie regular Gail Ann Dorsey.

Gang of Four were the most loud about their politics, but they weren't an exception among the UK punk scene. Leeds at the time was a politically turbulent city, with the fascist National Front holding rallies and the local left wing (composed of mostly students) rallying against it. King's lyrics drew from a growing school of thought in punk, primarily inspired by the fringe French Marxist group Situationist International. Influenced by Dadaism, Surrealism and opposing most other Marxist strains for being insufficiently anti-authoritarian and left-wing, Situationist International(SI) attempted to expand Marx's ideas about economic production to the realm of popular culture, attempting to achieve revolution via creating a counter-culture to radicalize the masses.

The organization was short lived, but managed to play a key role in the May '68 uprising in France. Some of their slogans included "They buy your happiness. Steal it.", "It is forbidden to forbid.", "I came. I saw. I believed." and "Be realistic, demand the impossible." SI was fond of contradictory statements and the alteration of pop culture, a tendency which would influence Adbusters, the organizers of Occupy Wall Street. Situationism would make its way to punk via several college students during '68 who would go on to manage punk bands including Joy Division and the Sex Pistols a decade later. Gang of Four also drew inspiration from the Frankfurt School (yet another anti-authoritarian Marxist group), black music including funk and disco and British pub rock band Dr. Feelgood.

# Hargrove's review

This album is a favorite for both Aidan and my dad, which means I have to hate it. Just kidding. It’s pretty good. It combines that wonderful post-punk atmosphere with guitar that sounds like steel wool, disgusting bass lines, driving beats, and sneered vocals. The tracks all share both a similar sound and related themes, making *Entertainment!* tight and focused as an album. My biggest complaint is that, honestly, it’s a little too British to me. That sounds dumb, but I do mean it. Many of my favorite albums are post-punk, but I tend to prefer the more abstract, genre-bending stuff you get from America (Television, Talking Heads) or Britain later into the 80s (The Cure, New Order). I have a lot of respect for the mainline late-70s-to-early-80s post-punk sound of Britain, but there’s something about it that limits my actual enjoyment of bands like The Fall (don’t get me started on The Pop Group). Here specifically, I think the sloganeering sometimes adds a choppiness to the lyrics and vocals that do the album a disservice, and some tracks don’t properly incorporate the dissonance of the guitar into the groove, leaving them a mess. I also found the song structures a bit repetitive after a while. But honestly, this is a good album. It’s a little bundle of energy that I had a great time listening to. I guess my dad and Aidan have to be right sometimes.

Gang of Four showcases its strengths right off the bat with “Ether”. I love the traded vocals, the banger bass line, and the cool use of dub echo in the breakdown before the outro. “White noise in a white room” distills the horrors of torture into a simple, repeatable line. “Natural’s Not in It” sounds pretty good, but I just can’t put it on my favorites. God, do I hate the way he says “migraine”. I do enjoy the way the drums strike on the and of 2, though. The bass line and clanging guitar drive “Not Great Men”, as do the infectious skank beats. However, it gets held back by its weak vocal line. “Damaged Goods”, on the other hand, kicks ass. From the fantastic intro that weaves the guitar skanks through the bass line, to the lunacy of the guitar in the main song, to the motorik beat, to the way King spits out “Sometimes I’m thinking that I love you/But I know it’s only lust” with such disdain for the world, this song rules. “Return the Gift” drove me crazy for a while because I recognized the “Evenings and weekends” line; eventually, I remembered that they used it on the title track of Massive Attack’s *Mezzanine*! It’s a catchy line that gets stuck in my head and makes the song memorable enough for favorite track status. “Guns and Butter” has a kind of a tribal tom tom drum type thing going on that just does not work for me. The guitar also loses the plot a bit, making it my least favorite track. On the flip side, it’s followed by my favorite track, “I Found That Essence Rare”. It’s what I live for! I love the line The worst thing in 1954 was the bikini”, as well as the way it incorporates the jagged guitar into such an upbeat and catchy pop sensibility. “Glass” has a crazy driving beat on the ride cymbal reminiscent of jazz and a 60s pop feel to its guitars and vocals. This would be a top-tier track, especially with the “Nicotine really goes to my head” line, but the choppy transitions from verse to chorus keep it in low-tier favorite track category. I do not care for “Contract” at all. It’s too prickly and dissonant without the rhythm and melody to pull it off. “At Home He’s a Tourist” is fun but forgettable. I don’t fully understand its popularity. While I’ve generally just shrugged off the commie lyrics, I actually really like the ones on “5.45” that point out the stupidity of assassination. It’s also got a great, slow groove to it. “Love Like Anthrax” reminds me a lot of “The Murder Mystery” from *The Velvet Underground*. King absolutely murders it on his vocal delivery, and I adore the parts where the two channels sync up.

Overall, this is a quite good album that unfortunately has a limited ceiling for my enjoyment. I do very much see myself going back to it, though. It’s just a fun listen.

Favorite tracks: Ether, Damaged Goods, Return the Gift, I Found That Essence Rare, Glass, 5.45, Love Like Anthrax

Least favorite track: Guns for Butter

# Aidan's review

Perhaps the only political rock group who manage to be both groovy and intelligent, Gang of Four knock it out of the park here. The interplay between the steady drum and bass lines with the horrifically scratchy lead guitar is good enough to anchor a record without the lyrics, and lyrically, Jon King manages to turn Marxism into dance songs like few else can do. The best songs are the ones who go beyond the lefty hobbyhorses of railing against the Troubles, H-Bombs and jingoism and embrace the more incisive criticisms borrowed from the Situationists. The sexual frustrations on "Damaged Goods", "At Home He's a Tourist" and "Contract", the mockery of televised violence of "5:45" and most of all the anti-love of "(Love Like) Anthrax" strike a rare balance between witty pop songwriting and well thought out political criticism. "Sometime I'm thinking that I love you/ But I know it's only lust" manages to be more poignant than some emo band's entire discographies. The formal experimentation on "Anthrax" is particularly brilliant and the anti-love tract is far better than the dreary technical report on the single version. There's little gems of vocal delivery like the rapid-fire bridge on "Glass" (who else can fit "paracetamol" into a song) and the robotic chant on 5:45. They list influences Lukas, Mauss, Debord and Satre yet it's not pretentious at all as a record. Probably the album on this list I've listened to the most total times. Even though we're not even 2/3 of the way through the list, we're down to 2 more records that are close to a dear to me as this one. One of a kind album and has pretty much single-handedly managed to get me to read 3 books (one's mostly pictures) on the French '68 riots.
