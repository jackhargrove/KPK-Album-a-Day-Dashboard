# Write-up

GAS is the primary musical project of German minimal techno and ambient producer Wolfgang Voigt. He is considered one of the primary pioneers of minimal techno, a subgenre of techno with a stripped-back sonic palette and simple, repetitive grooves which developed in the early 90s as a reaction to the loud and danceable genre techno had become. However, most of his work in techno was prior to *Pop*, although its influence can still be found in “Pop 4” and “Pop 7”. Voigt started the GAS project in 1995 as a way to translate his experiences with LSD as a teenager in the forests of Germany into music. Voigt chose the name GAS because he believes his music flows and streams in a free-form way, like a gas. From 1995 - 1999, GAS released three albums and two EPs that primarily combined ambient, nature-inspired sounds with a 4/4 bass drum beat; these albums have been described as “an outdoor rave, heard floating through the air from a neighbouring village”, so, if that interests you, it appears that *Zauberberg* and *Königsforst* are both well-regarded. *Pop* was the fourth album released by GAS and is so-titled because Voigt wanted it to bring a “pop” sound to the German techno scene (I’m not exactly sure how this album does so but 🤷‍♂️). The album largely abandons the techno sound that GAS had explored up to this point in favor of purely ambient, texture-focused music. All seven tracks are technically untitled, but digital releases choose to number them to avoid confusion. The album was very well received upon release in 2000, garnering many comparisons to Brian Eno and Aphex Twin; Pitchfork ranked it the 12th best album of 2000, and in 2016 it placed 11th on their list of the best ambient albums of all time. The GAS project went on hiatus after the album released, with its next release not coming until 2014. Voigt has made music under at least 35 different names, though, so it’s possible that he released from other projects in that time period, but it’s hard to keep track of and I’m not entirely certain. He is still alive at 64 years old. These ambient artists are really hard to find info on.

# Hargrove's review

Similar to jazz, pure ambient stuff has been difficult for me to rate because it’s not really my wheelhouse and I don’t have much basis for comparison. Occasionally, there will be a record that just speaks to me right off the bat (Tim Hecker’s *Ravedeath, 1972* for ambient, Ornette Coleman’s *The Shape of Jazz to Come* for jazz), but often I spend a lot of time trying to figure out how to compare it to music that I might find inferior but will inevitably listen to more because it’s more my style. Interestingly, this record reminded me a lot of that Hecker record in that they’re like opposite twins; the structure and textures are very similar, but Hecker uses them to make a hellish, apocalyptic wasteland of a soundscape, while *Pop* uses them for brightness and peace. Of course, the former is more suitable to my sensibilities, but I did find a lot to like in *Pop*, and having experience with Hecker’s music made it much easier for me to digest this record. In fact, that’s a big reason why I’m doing this project: to give myself a stronger foundation in genres that I wouldn’t even know where to start in the first place.

My overall take is that I think tracks 1-4 are varying levels of good, 5 and 6 are forgettable and drag on too long, and 7 is a great culmination. “Pop 1” is a great use case for ambient music, as I find it both enjoyable in the background and interesting in the foreground, particularly the looping and the broken wisps of melody. “Pop 2” is more or less a continuation, but with even more interesting textures, and with a watery and warbling track that kicks in and out at random. “Pop 3” is perhaps my favorite track; I really enjoy how it begins to hint at a rhythm that was buried much deeper in the first two tracks. It’s pulling from techno while still being decidedly not techno. “Pop 4” fully kicks in a beat, which feels natural. I actually find the beat makes the music more effective as ambient because my brain can more easily follow along while focusing on other things. “Pop 5” is where I start to get fatigued from the lack of movement. It’s decent enough to have playing, but I found myself fully tuning it out and unable to pull many coherent thoughts away from it. “Pop 6” was more or less the same. “Pop 7” expands on the ideas from “Pop 4” and is the closest to achieving that faraway rave vibe that the quote in my write-up mentions. I think “Pop 4” does more with less, but I still number “Pop 7” among my favorite tracks.

Ranking this is hard. The absolute floor was *Biosphere* in the middle of C Tier as I found this to be much more enjoyable. The ceiling was *Ravedeath, 1972* in A Tier, which blew my mind in a way that *Pop* never even approached. I reaaaaaaally wanted to find a way to justify putting this in B Tier, but I couldn’t bring myself to put it above *Led Zeppelin IV*, *Rumours*, or *Unknown Pleasures*, which are at the top of C Tier, as I know I’ll return to those more frequently. So, at least for now, I’m putting this up there in the top section of C Tier. I don’t think C Tier on its own reflects the quality of the album, but it does reflect my biases toward certain styles of music. I am excited to continue listening to more ambient and hopefully put it on similar footing in my brain with other styles.

Favorite tracks: Pop 1, Pop 3, Pop 4, Pop 7

Least favorite track: Pop 6
