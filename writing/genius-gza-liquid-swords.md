# Write-up

GZA (real name Gary Grice, previously known as The Genius) is a rapper born in 1966 in Brooklyn. As a child in 70s NYC, he immersed himself in the early hip hop block party scene. He dropped out of 10th grade in 1982 and formed his first rap group FOI: Force of the Imperial Master in 1984 with his cousins Robert Diggs (later RZA) and Russel Jones (later Ol’ Dirty Bastard). Though GZA and ODB lived in Brooklyn, they would travel to Staten Island to make music with RZA. GZA was the first of the proto-Wu-Tang Clan to sign to a label, releasing his debut album *Words from the Genius* under the name The Genius in 1991. Unfortunately, the record flopped, and he left the label. In 1992, he became one of the founding members of Wu-Tang Clan. As the oldest and the most experienced rapper, he was a natural leader in the group and got a rare solo track on 1993’s *Enter the Wu-Tang (36 Chambers)*. He earned a reputation as Wu-Tang’s strongest lyricist, often including scientific and philosophical themes in addition to tracks about street violence (this century, he’s actually gotten really involved in academic physics and has lectured at MIT and Harvard).

He released his second album *Liquid Swords* under both his old name The Genius and his new name GZA (pronounced “jih-za”, it’s what the word “genius” sounds like when scratched on a record. Similarly, RZA’s name comes from his old rap name Prince Rakeem) in 1995. He recorded it in RZA’s basement studio in Staten Island. RZA produced every song but the bonus track "B.I.B.L.E. (Basic Instructions Before Leaving Earth)”. That bonus track was included on CD releases but not vinyl releases and was credited to Killah Priest instead of GZA. Most of the dialogue comes from the 1980 Japanese kung fu movie *Shogun Assassin*. The title comes from the 1993 Hong Kong kung fu movie *Legend of the Liquid Sword*, which features a sword so sharp that when you cut someon’es head off, it remains unmoved on his shoulders. The cover depicts a sword fight on a chess board; GZA is a prolific chess player, later releasing a concept album about chess called *GrandMasters*. It was the fourth Wu-Tang solo album, following Method Man, ODB, and Raekwon. It featured every single member of Wu-Tang Clan at the time. The album had four singles: “I Gotcha Back”, “Liquid Swords”, “Cold World”, and “Shadowboxin’/The 4th Chamber”. It received rave reviews and sold very well. It was certified platinum in 2015 and is commonly considered the best Wu-Tang solo album.

GZA released his third solo album *Beneath the Surface* in 1999 to further commercial success. In 2002, he released his fourth album, *Legend of the Liquid Swords*. It is a direct sequel to *Liquid Swords*. He released his fifth album *Pro Tools* in 2008, which contains a diss track aimed at 50 Cent; the two have a longstanding feud. Since 2010, GZA has focused on his charitable work promoting education for underprivileged youth. He has also been working on an ambitious solo album for over 10 years, a concept album about astrophysics, quantum physics, and special relativity.

# Hargrove's review

I have never completely gotten the hype behind Wu-Tang, so I was a little happy to be closing the door on them by finishing the fourth and final affiliated album from our list. I liked the ODB record well enough, but *Enter the Wu-Tang (36 Chambers)* has never lived up to its all-time classic reputation for me and *Supreme Clientele* was really not that interesting. All of that being said, I was pretty impressed by *Liquid Swords*. I was relatively less familiar with GZA just because he has so few verses on Wu-Tang’s debut (5, compared to Raekwon’s 8, Inspectah Deck and Ghostface Killah’s 7, and Method Man and ODB’s 6. Hell RZA, the producer, has the same number of verses as GZA), but I had read a lot about him being the group’s best lyricist. Well, at first I was disappointed in that regard, as this record has so few quotables or lines that stick with me. After a few spins, however, I came to see GZA’s greatest asset as a lyricist: whatever he wants to say, he says in the most eloquent, economical, natural way possible. He doesn’t fall back on crutches like intentional mispronunciation or nonstandard phrasings to make his rhymes fit the meter. This allows him to sound relaxed, almost conversational, a quality I quite like. Along with his consistent, controlled flow, he’s the perfect MC to guide you through this album’s stellar production. Seriously, RZA was really on one here. RZA’s production style is very hit-or-miss for me because of its simplicity; so many of his beats are raw and barebones, relying heavily on repetition of the melodic element (often piano) for as long as it takes to get through all the verses. If the melodic element is strong enough (as in “C.R.E.A.M.”) or the drums filthy enough, his beats can be some of the best in the world. But when they miss the mark, as much of *Supreme Clientele* did for me, it gets dreary fast. On *Liquid Swords*, RZA hits far more than he misses. I really like his use of more identifiably rock samples like guitars and backbeat drums as they add to the album’s grimy character. I also think the relative brevity of each track and the strength of GZA’s emceeing help make them really shine. The features are generally good and placed tastefully. The album keeps a really consistent grimy atmosphere throughout its full run time. Really, my biggest gripes are the relatively lackluster run near the end from “Investigative Reports” through “I Gotcha Back”, and the fact that I prefer a personally charismatic lead MC like ODB to GZA’s more clinical style. But overall, pretty much as good as a Wu-Tang project could be for me.

The album opens with the best kung fu sample I’ve heard in any Wu-Tang records. The movie samples in this album are quite well-done compared to RZA’s other work. After the sample ends, “Liquid Swords” begins in earnest and man does it have one of RZA’s best beats. It’s so simple, just a guitar and a bass alternating eighth back and forth between pitches each bar, but it just works. GZA sounds great here too. It’s a very strong opening. My favorite part of “Duel of the Iron Mic” is its rock backbeat drum sample. It’s certainly not motorik, but it drives the track along in a way I don’t hear much in hip hop. In addition to GZA, Inspectah Deck drops a stellar verse for another good track. “Living in the World Today” has nice horns and a good bass line, but overall it’s pretty forgettable. “Gold” contains another rock sample, this time a heavily feedbacked guitar under the hook. The line “And how I changed a [REDACTED] name to John Doe” is one of the few single lines from GZA that stands out to me as clever on its own. This track stood out to me each listen and I definitely like it. “Cold World” has a crazy catchy hook; it was written by Stevie Wonder so I sure hope it would be catchy, but it’s nice to have nonetheless! “It was the night before New Year's, and all through the fuckin' projects/Not a handgun was silent, not even a TEC” from GZA is a super funny way to open a verse, and Inspectah Deck’s line “Some [REDACTED] in a jet-black Galant/Shot up the Chinese restaurant for this kid named Lamont/I thought he was dead, but instead he missed the kid/And hit a twelve-year-old girl in the head and then fled” got to me. This track has a super catchy hook, icy strings, and cold verse. What more could I ask for?

I’m still impressed by how smooth GZA’s puns in “Labels” sound. It’s like actually an achievement. This song is good for that alone. “4th Chamber” is another one to be based around a guitar sample. It’s got a few great verses, but Ghostface Killah’s stands out to me as the best. Method Man steals the show on “Shadowboxin’” and he nails it, but I don’t care for the beat and that kills the track for me a bit. The skit that opens “Hell’s Wind Staff / Killah Hills 10304” is really funny to me. It’s a genuinely good acting performance from RZA! Then the second part is probably my favorite track on its own. I love the way GZA tells this story. “First rule, anyone who schemes on the gold in Syria/I want they small intestines ripped from they interior” is a bar. And RZA’s progressive electronic beat rules. “Investigative Reports”, “Swordsman”, and “I Gotcha Back” all feel like middling filler. “Swordsman” is the best because of its creepy atmosphere and “I Gotcha Back” is the worst because of his repetitive and insubstantial it feels, but all three are a bit of a drag. “B.I.B.L.E. (Basic Instructions Before Leaving Earth)” isn’t really part of the album, and its sunny, optimistic beat heavily conflicts with what came before. But honestly it’s a pretty good track. Yeah, the lyrics are dumb as shit, but the flow is smooth, the hook is catchy, and the beat is nice as hell.

At this point, I think I’ve talked myself into putting this album over ODB’s. It’s not perfect, but it’s a pretty great listen from front to back.

Favorite tracks: Liquid Swords, Duel of the Iron Mic, Gold, Cold World, Labels, 4th Chamber, Hell’s Wind Staff / Killah Hills 10304

Least favorite track: I Gotcha Back
