# Write-up

Story/Fun Facts: The Germs were a punk band formed in Los Angeles in 1976 whose most well-known lineup consisted of leader and vocalist Darby Crash (real name Jan Paul Beahm), guitarist Pat Smear (real name Georg Ruthenberg), bassist Lorna Doom (real name Teresa Ryan), and drummer Don Bolles. Crash and Smear met each other while both were students at an alternative high school in LA; both were expelled after starting an “LSD Cult” where they got other students to take lots of acid, follow them around, and be destructive. Inspired by early punk bands like The Stooges, Crash and Smear decided to form their own band, originally called Sophistifuck but later called The Germs because the shorter name cost less to put on t-shirts. They put out a newspaper ad asking for two women with 0 musical experience to play bass and drums for their band, and they found Lorna Doom and Belinda Carlisle, respectively. Carlisle would leave the group after only a year (and would go on to found the incredibly successful group The Go-Go’s in the 80s) and was replaced by Don Bolles on drums. From the very start, Darby Crash had a 5 year plan for the band (inspired by a David Bowie song): they would become well-known as an insane, transgressive live band, release exactly one record, and then Crash would commit suicide at their peak to ensure the band would always have an immortal legacy.

Crash’s 5 year plan started off about as perfectly as any 5 year plan could. Throughout the mid-to-late 70s, The Germs terrorized the LA music scene, playing their unique brand of punk at clubs across Southern California and getting banned from each club one by one. Crash would perform the concerts extremely high on heroin, the band would scream and break things, they would encourage the audience to commit violence against property and each other, and they would play as loudly and as fast as possible. After The Germs were banned from every club in LA, they began to go by the name G.I. (Germs Incognito) to fool booking agents. Their fellow LA punk bands like Black Flag and D.O.A. took note, and they began to form the LA hardcore scene. Next, The Germs had to translate these live performances into an album. The band convinced future rock legend Joan Jett (formerly of The Runaways, who made the song “Cherry Bomb” that you’ll all know) to get into the studio with them to produce it and capture the vibe of their raucous live performances. The resulting LP, *(GI)*, came out in 1979 and was the first time hardcore punk had been put into full album format. The final part of Crash’s plan was to kill himself. On December 7, 1980, Darby Crash and a female friend intentionally took lethal doses of heroin in their house in LA. While she survived, Crash succeeded in killing himself at only 22 years old. Unfortunately, the best laid plans can come apart at the last second: the very next day, John Lennon was murdered in New York City. The newspapers talked about John Lennon’s death for weeks, and Crash’s suicide was completely buried, ruining his plan to achieve immortality for himself and The Germs. After Crash’s death, The Germs split up; Pat Smear went on to become a touring member in Nirvana and later the Foo Fighters, while Lorna Doom and Don Bolles continued to be active in the LA punk scene.

While I don’t think it would be fair to call this record or any hardcore punk record a “context record” as hardcore punk to this day still has a dedicated fanbase, it is undeniable that hardcore became one of the most influential movements in underground music ever. In one branch, hardcore progressed into post-hardcore in the late 80s, which itself progressed into post-rock and emo. Another genre to emerge from hardcore was grunge; Kurt Cobain of Nirvana was heavily influenced by Tales of Terror, a Sacramento-based hardcore group. Hardcore also merged with metal in the mid-80s to create thrash metal (the most well-known subgenre of metal), speed metal, death metal, and black metal. And hardcore also reunited with post-punk and New Wave to create the genre known as alternative rock in the late 80s and 90s, which eventually became indie.

# Hargrove's review

We’ve already been over how hardcore punk is just not my genre, but I’d like to focus on the things that I’ve come to appreciate over my repeated listens today. I think my issues with the record (and the genre as a whole) are both obvious and a matter of different taste: the songs are shorter and simpler than I’d like, Darby Crash’s vocals get exhausting after a while, the songs are samey and blend together, and you very much get the sense that you would be having a lot more fun if you were at a live performance (and the type of person willing to mosh). So though most of my review will read as purely positive, keep in mind that there’s an implicit “but” after everything, and that a lot of the praise is more things I respect about the record rather than enjoy. At the same time, I did appreciate this a lot more than I thought I would. It was relentlessly visceral and made me feel more feelings than many other records we’ve listened to. So let’s call this review “mixed” instead of “middling”.

There are some genuine banger tracks on this thing, a few of which I even added to my liked songs playlist. “Land of Treason” was the first song that really caught my attention, I like both the cadence and melody of Crash’s singing in the verses, and the interplay between the guitar and drums is entrancing. At 2:09, it’s somehow one of the longest tracks, and this makes it feel a little more fleshed out than some others. “Richie Dagger’s Crime” contains my favorite vocal quirks from Crash, I really like the twisted doo-wop of the “oh whoa whoa” in the chorus. The repeated guitar like is relatively complex, and the rhythm is driving. “Lexicon Devil” has the most streams, and for good reason in my opinion; the chorus is genuinely catchy, the way Crash says “that” at the end of the chorus is bizarre in the best way, and the cymbals are fantastic. “Manimal” has the best bass line on the album. “We Must Bleed” opens with a guitar riff that it definitely seems like Black Flag ripped off for “Rise Above” a couple years later. Beyond that, though, it also has this visceral descending chorus, and this insane pulsating guitar groove in the verses. “Media Blitz” sounds like the poppiest song to me and is enjoyable, if harsh, punk. “The Other Newest One” is almost a ballad, the only song that communicates sadness to me more than anger. “Shut Down (Annihilation Man)” is way too long, but it’s the one that I imagine mist directly replicates a live performance, and that’s cool.

On a less granular level, there were other aspects of this album that I appreciated that I have not come around to in my previous experience with hardcore. The overarching focus on rhythm instead of melody appeals to me. There are stretches where it feels like I’m meant to feel this album more than listen to it. I appreciate how relentless it is; there are no interludes, no slow songs, no quiet songs, just nonstop in-your-face aggression. I appreciate the insanely fast tempos and the volume that get me pumped and make me want to break shit. I appreciate that I’ve never heard anyone who sings like Darby Crash before.

I really made an effort for this album, more than usual, because I really wanted to like it. And I found a lot of things that I do appreciate. That being said, I still like post-punk and post-hardcore and post-rock and math rock and emo and y’allternative and the other things that came out of hardcore much more. Visceral feelings are cool, but pairing them with music that’s more melodic, more complex, and more subtle will always be more my bag. When placing this album on my ranking, I ended up putting it higher than I initially expected I would; there was just a string of albums that I enjoyed but that didn’t leave an impression on me in the same way this one did, so I felt compelled to put this above those records. I reserve the right to move this down after a few days of reflection if I feel that’s justified.

Favorite tracks: Land of Treason, Richie Dagger’s Crime, Lexicon Devil, We Must Bleed, The Other Newest One

Least favorite track: Dragon Lady
