# Write-up

Ghostface Killah (real name Dennis Coles) is a rapper born in 1970 who is a member of Wu-Tang Clan. He grew up in a housing project in Staten Island and attended middle school with Raekwon. He took his stage name from the 1979 kung fu film *The Mystery of Chess Boxing*. See the write-up above for background on Wu-Tang Clan and their seminal debut album *Enter the Wu-Tang (36 Chambers*). After that album’s success in 1993, RZA, the group’s producer and leader, assigned the other members to make solo albums that he would then produce. These album’s are referred to as “first generation” Wu-Tang solo albums and include Method Man’s *Tical* (1994), Ol’ Dirty Bastard’s *Return to the 36 Chambers: The Dirty Version*, Raekwon’s *Only Build 4 Cuban Linx…*, GZA’s *Liquid Swords* (all 1995), and Ghostface Killah’s *Ironman* (1996). All were critical and commercial successes, with Ol’ Dirty Bastard’s and GZA’s first generation solo albums also appearing on our list. In 1997, they regrouped to release the second full Wu-Tang Clan album, *Wu-Tang Forever*. It received further good reviews and sold well, but certainly not to the same level as their debut. The Wu then split again to record more solo albums, but this time RZA would be less involved. However, RZA called dibs on producing Ghostface Killah’s album; the two had always been on the same wavelength about comic books and kung fu movies, and Ghostface was also considered the most technically gifted rapper of the crew. In 98 and 99, Method Man, GZA, Ol’ Dirty Bastard, and Raekwon all released their second solo albums, while Cappadonna, Inspectah Deck, U-God, and even RZA all put out their first. In stark contrast to the first generation Wu solo albums, the second generation albums all received disappointed, middling reviews. It seemed like their moment had finally run out.

Ghostface and RZA began the album recording process with an extended visit to Africa in 1997, which inspired Ghostface to stop rapping about crime and money. When they returned, two incidents caused major delays to writing and recording: RZA’s basement studio flooded and destroyed all of his equipment, and Ghostface had to serve a 6 month prison sentence for a robbery charge from 1995. Ghostface and RZA accepted many beats from various producers, with only six of the beats on the final album coming from RZA himself. However, they took the unusual step of remixing and re-producing these beats after receiving them from the initial producers, giving the album a cohesive feel and flow. Lyrically, Ghostface perfected the storytelling style he was known for. The skit “Clyde Smith” features Raekwon dissing then-unknown rapper 50 Cent in response to 50 Cent saying he would rob members of Wu-Tang Clan on a track the year before. The album, *Supreme Clientele*, released in 2000 and was the first critical success for the Wu in three years. It was also a commercial success, hitting #7 on the Billboard 200. Today, it is usually considered one of the finest Wu-Tang solo records, and also perhaps the last great Wu-Tang record.

The trajectory of Wu-Tang Clan has largely been downhill since 2000, although Ghostface has been the most acclaimed in that time period. His 2006 album *Fishscales* is often highly regarded as well. He also became a favorite of the *Pitchfork* indie kid set who were first starting to take hip hop seriously in the early 2000s; *Pitchfork* ranked *Supreme Clientele* the 11th best album of the 2000s decade in 2009, and numerous of his subsequent albums got good reviews from them. As of late, Ghostface appeared on Kendrick Lamar’s “Purple Hearts” from *Mr. Morale & The Big Steppers*, and just released *Supreme Clientele 2* earlier this year.

# Hargrove's review

I’ve never been the biggest Wu-Tang guy in the world, and this is the first time I’ve ever listened to one of their solo albums. Unfortunately, *Supreme Clientele* takes a lot of my issues with *Enter the Wu-Tang (36 Chambers)* clan (and East Coast hardcore hip hop writ large) without the fun lo-fi feel or any track on the same level as “C.R.E.A.M.”, a song so good it transcends any of my aesthetic preferences. Ghostface is a supremely gifted rapper, and RZA a masterful producer. But unfortunately, *Supreme Clientele* never grows beyond those talents. The result is track after track of proficient rhymes without any bits or phrases that get stuck in my head and soulful beats that blend together. There are a lot worse things to be! There are basically no bad songs, nothing to pick apart or complain about. I just wish Ghostface had more of a memorable personality, or a distinctive voice, or an inventive approach to flow, or tighter and snappier lyrics. I wish he were more than the platonic ideal of a good rapper. He’s often described as a “storyteller” by critics, but I never felt drawn in and mesmerized like I do by great story rap songs like Kendrick’s “The Art of Peer Pressure, or even non-rap story songs like Dylan’s “Lily, Rosemary and the Jack of Hearts” earlier this week. He’s really good at stringing words together that fit perfectly in the moment but fall out of your head immediately after. I saw praise for the cohesive style of the beats on this album, but that just added to the indistinguishability issue. There are a few songs that did stand out to me, and I’ll get into them in the next paragraph. But my overall takeaway is that *Supreme Clientele* is a perfectly satisfactory rap album that I can’t criticize all that much, but that I can’t praise all that much either. It would fit perfectly in a textbook to exemplify a style, but does much less for me when I’m listening for enjoyment. Also, it’s too long.

Like other albums with this many tracks, I’m only going to go over ones I found especially good or especially bad. If I don’t mention a track, just imagine I said “good rapping, soulful beat”. I came to enjoy “Nutmeg” the more I listened for being the first real track. It’s got the good rapping and the soulful beat, but it does it first, so it’s the most enjoyable of the style. The bounciness of the beat and the lush instrumental under the hook stand out to me most. “Ghost Deini” is easily my favorite track on the album. The twinkling beat sounds like a dark forest and reminds me of the atmospheres on *Endtroducing…* by DJ Shadow. Ghostface’s vocals are more animated and varied than usual, particularly the reference to Rakim and then the sad section about Biggie and Tupac. Really cool track. I like “The Grain” for being nice and tight, as well as uniquely upbeat. “Stay True” is even tighter at a minute-and-a-half and features Ghostface at his most charismatic. “We Made It” stands out for its lush strings and catchy hook. I thought “Stroke of Death” was super cool upong first listen because I had never heard a skipping record used as a sample before. However, repeated listens made me realize why I had never heard a skipping record used as a sample before. It gets really grating! This might sound dumb, but the “Iron’s Theme” intermission and conclusion tracks are two of my favorites. I just really like the melody and choral singing here. It’s catchy and groovy, sue me. “Malcolm” is by far the best track since “Ghost Deini”. Those two are the main contribution from this album to my music listening going forward. It’s got a super interesting piano loop sample that alternates with this awesome echoic sample and a down and dirty drum beat. The verses are solid too. Unfortunately, “Who Would You Fuck” follows, downright one of the worst rap skits I’ve ever heard, and I’ve heard some bad ones. It makes me really uncomfortable; they had to have known these women in real life, no? “Cherchez La Ghost” stands out from the samey rap tracks at the end of this record. It has a very busy bass line with a complex drum beat that makes it all so bouncy and danceable, and I like the female singer a lot as well.

I don’t want to give the impression that I think this is a bad record, which I don’t. It’s a good record that I hope stays good somewhere away from me. I feel the exact same way about Duster’s *Stratosphere* and Megadeth’s *Rust in Peace*

Favorite tracks: Nutmeg, Ghost Deini, The Grain, Stay True, We Made It, Iron’s Theme - Intermission, Malcolm, Cherchez La Ghost, Iron’s Theme - Conclusion

Least favorite track: Who Would You Fuck

Least favorite track (non-skit): Stroke of Death

# Aidan's review

The 4th album this week from an artist who we've heard before in some form or another, this album manages to capture most of the best aspects of Enter the 36 Chambers. RZA's production is worth enduring his not great verses, and there's the same boyish sense of fun at the end of "One" when the song is over and it fades out to Ghostface and his friends dicking around with the beat with stuff like "how many nuts you bust? - One". It's this goofy feeling that makes this album really fun, and while the Iron Man samples don't make as much sense as MF DOOM's sampling, it still makes this fun. It loses this lighthearted feeling at points, "Clyde Smith" and "Who Would You Fuck" both are just annoying, and Ghostface's tough guy persona is only interesting when it's feeding into some ludicrous lyrical boast. The rapping stays in the range of acceptable and is rarely good enough to deserve RZA's amazing production, but the result is still pretty good. Reminds me a lot of MM FOOD in that there's a tremendous 35 minute album in there. Still a fun album and "One" is a banger.
