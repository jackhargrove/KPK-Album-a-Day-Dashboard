# Write-up

1970s New York City was a dirty, crime ridden place but while Attica and Serpico rattled around the collected unconscious, Glenn Branca was doing what nobody else had ever done with guitars. Born in Harrisburg and majoring in theater at Emerson, Branca was an unlikely composer, he was a guitarist since age 15 and originally wanted to pursue theater in NYC as well. But after meeting members of the punk scene and being introduced to bands like Suicide (on the list), he decided to start his own band. He was initially part of the broader No Wave movement, which rejected the commercialism and sleekness of New Wave in favor of DIY ethics, difficult music, and above all else, noise. Branca was one of the main figures in a scene that included bands like Teenage Jesus and the Jerks, the Gynecologists and Communists. Sonic Youth and Swans also rose out of this scene, but most of it faded away, only recorded in Brian Eno’s definitive No Wave New York compilation album. Branca grew his work out of that style, but progressed to longer compositions that resembled symphonies, while keeping the electric guitar central to his work. The Ascension was the first one of this composition and features Lee Ranaldo of Sonic Youth (Thurston Moore joined the group later and met Ranaldo there). The piece was recorded at bargain bin prices, using connections from the bassist to record with friends at the Power Station and being produced by local record store owner Ed Balhalm, who would later mentor Rick Rubin to start Def Jam records. The album is likely not on Spotify because of this low cost effort, as the label 99 which it was recorded under went out of business within 4 years thanks to litigation by the Sugar Hill Gang (which 99 won). The Ascension was seen as inferior to Branca’s live works when it was released, but it has grown to a near mythical status in the current age. In true No Wave fasion, the cover was originally supposed to have two men having sex, but was changed to have Branca dragging the dead body of another man. Branca went on to record more records included the also acclaimed sequel to this album, and his music shifted more and more towards traditional orchestra, but never abandoned his focus on composing for the electric guitar. He died in 2018

# Hargrove's review

You know it’s gonna be fun when one song has to specifically point out that it’s consonant. But actually unfortunately it wasn’t as fun as I had hoped. I fucked up and let myself build expectations around this one, as I was so intrigued by the genre marker “totalism” when I first heard about it a few weeks ago. I was so curious what it would sound like. One guy described it as a “symphony of chainsaws”. This is why I try not to read too much about the sound of something before I listen. I felt pretty underwhelmed overall. I was hoping for something completely alien, but it just ended up being more dissonant post-rock. It was still good though, and I’d love to talk about the parts I liked. Some albums cause me to see a color very vividly, and this album made me see brown the entire time. Everything about this album is a dark, muddled brown. The sounds are dull and loose, and they all blend into each other like a pile of rubber bands (or exhaust pipes). The opener “Lesson No. 2” was one of my favorites, as especially the first half really leans into that puddle of mud atmosphere. I also respected the repeated chord in the second half, as it was one of the few parts of the album that genuinely hurt my ears. I also really enjoyed the closer, “The Ascension”. It brings back that overwhelming muddiness from the opening, and it features an awesome drum breakdown early on. The use of silence is brilliant (eat your heart out, John Cage), really making you feel viscerally just how much noise is going on at once. I also really like the way that it build, feeling more and more immense like a wave that never crashes. Parts of it feel like if the orchestra in “A Day in the Life” by the Beatles were the entire song.

The entire middle of the album didn’t do too much for me though. I like the first melody in “The Spectacular Commodity”, but once it gets into a groove it just feels a little too…upbeat? Feels weird to say, but there was a brightness I didn’t love. I did like how it sounded like melodies were being passed back and forth. “Structure” didn’t really stand out to me in any way despite repeated listens, it felt very insignificant. “Light Field (In Consonance)” had a cool surf-y descending main guitar line, and I liked the staticky guitars in the foreground, but again the crescendo/accelerando does little for me. I do really like the use of stereo with the cymbals though at the end.

Overall, I think I need more time to digest and explore this. I will definitely listen more in the future, but as of right now, I’m underwhelmed.

Favorite tracks: Lesson No. 2, The Ascension

Least favorite track: Structure

# Aidan's review

I’m kind of ashamed I’ve never listened to this album. I guess I thought it would be as harsh as the other No Wave I’ve heard, and just wrote it off, but upon listening to it, it’s fantastic. You can hear its influence on Sonic Youth, but it also foreruns post rock in general, and it’s impossible to not hear Godspeed in the crescendos. A lot of it is harsh, but it does the same thing I loved so much about Daydream Nation, the combining of dissonant parts into something beautiful. The ending of The Spectatular Commodity was the pinnacle of this style for me as the guitars race back and forth between the slow repeated chords, but the entire thing was just amazing. So glad I listened to this.
