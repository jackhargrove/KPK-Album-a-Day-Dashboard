# Write-up

The vinyl for F#A#∞ costs $23 and comes with one of three cover images glued to the front of a burgundy case. The image on streaming services is a darkened version of the "highway lights" image which comes from the CD cover, but you may also receive a photo of a water tower at a dutch angle seen through an alleyway or a close up on the wheels of a train. The batch number is hand drawn in silver ink, as is the glyph on the back cover which resembles a cross with a long tail and three lines emanating upward from each of the shorter ends. The logo for Constellation records is stamped in the same silver ink. Inside, in addition to the record, you will find a reproduction of an early playbill for GY!BE, a blueprint for the album, a credit sheet, a hand drawn image by Efrim Menuck, the band's de facto leader and a photo of a train silkscreened into various colors, dedicated to blues musician Reverend Gary Davis. The track titles are not on any of these notes, instead they are scratched into an unused groove of the record itself. Last and most famously, there is an American penny, crushed by a train. This was how the record was first released in a 500 pressing run that was the 3rd release by Constellation, a record company run by their producers, and the first record that wasn't just a self produced release. Godspeed's entire catalog to this point was 33 cassette tapes of All Lights Fucked on the Hairy Amp Drooling, an album that was thought to be lost until 2022 and bore little resemblance to F#A#∞. The record has been released in the same way since the original 500 pressing run, as GY!BE has grown in reputation from a niche band in Montreal to the defining band of post rock, only swapping out the penny from Canadian to American due to Canada discontinuing its one cent piece.

GY!BE was a motley crew from the beginning. The band formed around a loft in Montreal, first leased by Mauro Pezzente, who plays bass on F#A#∞, and later by Efrim. The space was dubbed Hotel2Tango after the postal code of the area, H2T, and became one of the centers of the Montreal alternative music scene. GY!BE was more of a collective than a band, swelling to 15 people before being cut down to 10 for the recording of F#A#∞. The original release was only 2 tracks and 38 minutes long (not counting a lock groove loop at the end), and the vinyl version is still released that way. In order to raise funds and interest for touring the United States, the band released a CD version through the label Kranky in Chicago, which also has released Tim Hecker and Stars of the Lid. This version, which you most likely listened to, was amended to be 63 minutes long, reworking much of the material. The first two tracks, "Dead Flag Blues" fka "Nervous, Sad, Poor" and "East Hastings" fka "Bleak, Uncertain, Beautiful", are largely similar, but have been retitled, while the final track, Providence, is almost entirely new, only featuring 2 of its 6 movements heisted from the second track. While the LP version was well reviewed, it was the CD version that became a classic, only matched in reputation by the following record Raise Your Skinny Fists Like Antennas To Heaven.

The record features the typical GY!BE crescendos and tape samples, with a theme of destruction and apocalypse. The opening spoken word section was taken from an unfinished movie script by Efrim, and the opening section on Providence is from a speaker referred to as Blaise Bailey Finnegan III on a following EP. Other samples are from obscure films and musicals. The music was an inspiration for the creation of the zombie movie 28 Days Later (the third film, 28 years later, is due to be released soon), and single movement from East Hastings features in the film (but not the official soundtrack). That 10:44 feature in a minor zombie film represents the entirety of GY!BE's engagement with the mainstream. They have done 2 interviews during their 30 year history and never made a music video. Their music stands alone.

# Hargrove's review

I listened to the two canonical GYBE records back around freshman year of college, and I’ve only ever really gone back to *Lift Yr. Skinny Fists Like Antennas to Heaven* since. I couldn’t really have told you why, as *F♯ A♯ ∞* did leave an impression on me, or so I thought. I remembered the apocalyptic atmosphere, I remembered a decent number of the spoken word samples, and I remembered enjoying it. So why did I never really go back? I now know it’s because “The Dead Flag Blues” fucking sucks. Well, that’s an overstatement. But man is that an all style no substance composition and it’s really frustrating to listen to. It does a great job of building up the atmosphere that the rest of the record will get deeper into, but there’s very little by way of actually enjoyable music. It takes *so long* for anything real to form, not until after the 10:00 mark do we get the quasi-Western section. The outro is a nice bit of music, but it feels completely unearned after there was no real build or musical intensity prior. I was having trouble working up the will for re-listens on this, and it was super surprising to me. I came to the realization earlier today when I drifted off to sleep during a listen. I woke up at some point during a faster section on “Providence” and I was absolutely floored, it was amazing. I really think the opener holds this album back, requiring too much buy-in up front before you’ve seen what they can really do.

“East Hastings” shows more good sense right off the bat than “The Dead Flag Blues”, with a tighter sample and some nice bagpipes. “The Sad Mafioso…” still drags in its intro, but at least the unresolved guitar line and drums make for a genuinely ominous atmosphere. Eventually it builds into this heavy, lethargic groove that I really enjoyed, almost reminiscent of some doom metal. The violin, viola, drums trio is really pretty, and the accelerando is extremely well done, very impressive work for what is probably the first accelerando they ever put on wax. The droning strings in that section also add a lot of atmosphere. Unfortunately, “Drugs in Tokyo”/“Black Helicopter” goes back to extended noodly noise. I can put up with it more because it comes after Godspeed have finally demonstrated what they’re capable of, but man does this album have a much worse killer-to-filler ratio than *Skinny Fists*.

“Providence” is by far the finest composition on this album, and really impressive how it holds my attention for half an hour. The guy in the sample sounds like a mixture of Trump and Bernie, and the ambient instrumental sounds like *Kid A* before *Kid A*. “Dead Metheny…” is perhaps my favorite movement, the violin in the beginning is really stellar and I love the part with the smoldering back-and-forth between the bass and the guitar. The bells remind me of *Tubular Bells*, and I love that record so it’s a great compliment. The tribal drumming at the end of the build is very well-done too. “Kicking Horse on Brokenhill” is a breakdown done well, where steam starts to build again immediately. The directionality here is nice given the aimlessness of some other parts of the record. The overarching melody in this movement is my favorite in the record, and I’m intrigued by the surf-y tremolo in the guitar. The silence is an unfortunate remnant of the CD era that is frustrating in streaming (looking at you “Irene” by Beach House) but I’ll look past it. The outro is one final explosion with an interesting effect on the panning of the drums that causes them to be out of sync. Great composition.

I really don’t care for “The Dead Flag Blues”, but some of “East Hastings” and all of “Providence” makes up for it. Definitely not as good as *Skinny Fists*, but I think they work great in tandem, with the apocalyptic atmosphere in this one contrasting with the hope that is to come.

Favorite tracks: East Hastings, Providence

Least favorite track: The Dead Flag Blues

# Aidan's review

I’ve always been in awe at how cinematic this album feels to me. Not only the cuts to field recordings and samples but also the way it shifts from movement to movement, panning from the cliches of westerns to the mechanical running of trains to the doomed military marches. The droning guitars are so alien they feel like forces of nature and the antiquated feeling of some of the sung samples adds to the feeling of discovering something in the ashes of the old world. You can almost see the interviews at the side of a highway or looking out over a burned city. The crescendos in East Hastings and Providence feel like they are racing over empty plains, as the whole record places the symbols of civilization expanding from the East coast, the trains, the military, the highways, and places them at the center of an apocalyptic suite. The samples weave in and out of the music and production so well, as it shifts from mechanical to analog. More than any other Godspeed work, it has roots in the popular imagination, drawing on the constant fear of civilization slipping away, slowly then all at once. It’s a singularly impressive record, and while it lacks the groundedness of its sequel, I prefer it for the raw power of its imagery. This is how the world ends, not with a whisper but with gorgeous crescendos.
