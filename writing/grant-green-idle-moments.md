# Write-up

Grant Green was a jazz guitarist born in 1935 and raised in St. Louis. His father, an amateur blues and folk musician, taught him the guitar when he was child. As a teenager, he began playing with a gospel (precursor to R&B) group. He got really into jazz in his 20s, particularly the hard and fast style of bebop. His favorite artist was bebop sax legend Charlie Parker, who inspired Green to play his guitar in a similar style to the saxophone (melodically, not harmonically). Green moved to NYC in 1959 after being discovered at a bar in St. Louis. In 1961, he became the in-house studio guitarist for Blue Note Records, the most important jazz label of all time. Despite his inexperience and anxiety, Blue Note had him record both backing other musicians and as a bandleader from himself. Blue Note was able to get Green to record a shitload of music because of his intense heroin addiction that always left him looking for more cash. Green released 23 albums as bandleader in his lifetime and countless more as a sideman. By the late 60s, Green’s drug abuse and personal issues began to catch up with him. He left Blue Note for a few years, but returned in 1969. His health seriously deteriorated in the late 70s, but he refused to stop playing; he died of a heart attack at age 43 in 1978 in his car after a show. Blue Note has continued to release the massive backlog of recordings they have of him even after his death.

*Idle Moments*, released in 1965, was Green’s 7th album as bandleader. In addition to Green playing electric guitar, the album also features tenor sax player Joe Henderson (who appears on three other jazz albums on our list, which is the record from what I can tell), pianist Duke Pearson, vibraphone player Bobby Hutcherson (same one from Eric Dolphy’s *Out to Lunch*), double bassist Bob Cranshaw, and drummer Al Harewood. Because guitars are capable of playing chords by themselves, it’s slightly unusual for a jazz group to have both a pianist (which can do the same) and a guitarist; however, Pearson’s chord playing on piano allows Green to use his guitar entirely as a melodic and soloing instrument. Pearson wrote the album’s title track. At 15 minutes, it was accidentally much longer than intended, but everyone liked the recording so they kept it and shortened the other tracks. Pearson also wrote the closer, “Nomad”, while Green wrote the second track “Jean De Fleur”. “Django” is a cover of a jazz standard written by John Lewis in 1954. It is among the most famous and well-liked standards of the post-swing era. *Idle Moments* sits at the intersection between cool jazz and hard bop, the two major styles that solidified out of bebop in the 50s. “Idle Moments” and “Django” both exhibit the laidback, melodic tendencies of cool jazz, while “Jean De Fleur” and “Nomad” resemble the faster blues- and R&B-influenced style of hard bop. Given Green’s background in blues, folk, and R&B, as well as the much higher popularity of guitar in those genres than jazz, Green commonly worked influences from those genres into his jazz playing. Later in his career, he would even make soul jazz, a style that developed out of hard bop that leaned into R&B (specifically soul) influences so heavily that it became a fusion genre of the two.

# Hargrove's review

This was a solidly above average jazz album. I didn’t really care for last week’s jazz album, but I was a fan of how it had a multiple instruments that I don’t usually hear in jazz. Well, this album also had that going for it, but this time I also enjoyed the actual music those instruments were playing. First and foremost is Green’s guitar, which sounds truly great on this record. I especially like how it sounds on the two slower tracks, “Idle Moments” and “Django”. For whatever reason, electric guitar just complements the laidback, city-at-night atmosphere of cool jazz really well. It’s also nice to hear a guitar continuously play freeform melody, unconstrained by the repetition of the rock riff. Don’t get me wrong, I love a good riff, but it’s a welcome change of pace. This album also has a vibraphone, in fact one with the same vibraphonist as last week. I praised it then, and I’m praising it now: the vibraphone solos are consistently the second best after the guitar solos. It also adds a bright resonance that stands out when paired with the sleazy nightclub jazz. Though the other four instruments are pretty standard, the guitar and vibraphone bring enough novelty for me. There’s nothing too special about the bass or drums, but I was impressed by Henderson’s saxophone playing. Given the guitar takes the normal sax spot of primary melodic duties, you might expect the actual saxophone role to suffer, but Henderson shows great restraint while the guitar is playing and lays down some sleek solos when it’s not. The wildcard for me was Pearson’s piano; I enjoyed how playful it was during the head sections, especially its interplay with the guitar, but I found Pearson’s solos to consistently be the weakest. As for the songwriting, all four tracks had decent head melodies, and I appreciated that the head melody recurred between each solo on a few tracks. Overall, this is easily in the top half of jazz albums we’ve listened to. It doesn’t wow me in the same way that Matana Roberts or Charles Mingus did, but it’s a deeply pleasant listen.

“Idle Moments” sets the pace slow and low immediately and does not budge for 15 minutes. The guitar and piano intertwine to create a gorgeous head melody. Green plays a strong solo on his guitar, but Pearson’s on the piano does not match it. Henderson’s sax picks things back up when it finally enters 8 minutes in, and then Hutcherson nearly reaches Green’s heights with his vibraphone. The track does suffer slightly from its length, but honestly given how nice the atmosphere is, I wouldn’t mind just letting it play forever in the background. After 15 minutes of slow and smooth, “Jean De Fleur” feels positively like punk rock in comparison. Its head provides the catchiest melody of the album, followed by much quicker solos from each instrument. It’s a good track, but I think the slower tracks utilize the guitar better. Pearson kicks off “Django” with a short piano melody that reminds me of Bill Evans before the rest of the band comes in at a renewed glacial pace. While the head is great, particularly the vibraphone part, Green steals the show during the solo section through his dissonant, tense playing. His first solo, my favorite from the album, lays the blueprint by alternating between dissonant build sections and playful release sections. The other solos are fine on their own, but Green keeps coming back to inject that tension again. It’s a great effect that completely makes the track.

Django: begins with piano melody that reminds me of Bill Evans, much slower again, vibraphone really pretty in the head, gives a resonant sound, really cool sounding guitar solo starts around 2:00, it sounds a little odd against the chords but in a good way, very tense, then gives way to a more playful atmosphere before returning to tension again, favorite solo on the album so far, sax 3:55, vibraphone 5:05, there are small bits of the head between each solo, and now the guitar is contributing that tension again underneath the vibraphone, really cool chords, then piano 6:30, second guitar solo 7:20, great use of dissonance in this track. “Nomad” returns to the speed of “Jean De Fleur” to close the album, but it’s not quite as good and more than twice as long. I don’t care for the head section in the opening, and the solos feel scattershot (though I do love the short section starting at 6:00 where the guitar and vibraphone play against each other). However, the head sounds much stronger at the end to make up for it somewhat, though it’s still my least favorite track.

Though it mixes styles, the standouts of this album were cool jazz so I’m going to consider it mostly that. This was our 5th cool jazz album, so I re-listened to the other 4 today. *A Charlie Brown Christmas* is still my favorite, the most consistent all the way through and the one that makes me happiest. Brubeck follows on the strength of the songwriting alone. After that, I put *Idle Moments* and Bill Evans’ *Waltz for Debby* on just about equal footing, though I have to give the ever-so-slight advantage to Evans for that thick nightclub atmosphere. Cannonball Adderley’s *Somethin’ Else* remains comfortably at the bottom.

Favorite tracks: Idle Moments, Django

Least favorite track: Nomad
