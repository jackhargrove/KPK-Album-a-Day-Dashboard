# Write-up

Henry Cow was a British rock group formed at Cambridge University in 1968. The list of members was constantly changing, but this album featured organist/clarinetist/pianist Tim Hodgkinson, guitarist/violinist/xylophonist/pianist Fred Firth, bassist/pianist John Greaves, bassoonist/oboist Lindsay Cooper, and drummer Chris Cutler. In addition, this album was made in collaboration with the band Slapp Happy, which at this time consisted of vocalist Dagmar Krause, guitarist/clarinetist Peter Blegvad, and pianist Anthony Moore. Henry Cow played a form of music now called avant-prog, a genre related to progressive rock but even more experimental and more closely related to jazz and folk. Avant-prog developed from the Canterbury Scene, a group of bands in England that played a highly improvisational jazz-rock inspired by Frank Zappa, and Rock in Opposition, a collective of European anti-capitalist experimental rock bands founded by Henry Cow.

Henry Cow was formed by Firth and Hodgkinson, who met at a blues club at Cambridge in 1968. By June of that year, they opened a concert for up-and-coming prog rock band Pink Floyd. They named themselves Henry Cow because it was the silliest name they could think of. The band would add and subtract members, and they all got really into Captain Beefheart and Frank Zappa. They started to intentionally write and improvise music that was as difficult to play as possible in order to challenge themselves. Throughout the late 60s and early 70s, the band would play at artsy venues like universities and galas in Britain until they were signed to Virgin records in 1973. They released their debut album *The Henry Cow Legend* (or *Legend* or *Leg End*) to little notice. They added classically trained bassoonist Lindsay Cooper in 1974. They toured with krautrock group Faust, and later with our favorite blues singer Captain Beefheart. They released their second album *Unrest* in 1974, which received some classical music world buzz thanks to Cooper; the group then temporarily kicked her out for the tour as a challenge to force them to write new music. At the end of 1974, Henry Cow unexpectedly began working with German art pop group Slapp Happy. The two groups recorded two albums together in 1975, *Desperate Straights* (more Slapp Happy) and *In Praise of Learning* (more Henry Cow). The latter album received some intense praise from a few critics, including comparisons to high art figures like Bertolt Brecht, and is their most well-known album today.

Henry Cow (plus Krause, who left Slapp Happy) would tour nonstop until 1978. Their sound was largely rejected in their native Britain, but they were well-received in continental Europe, where challenging art is more broadly popular. Though they had always been leftists (today’s album contains multiple references to Chairman Mao), their money struggles in the music industry made Henry Cow even more extremely anti-capitalist. In 1978, they decided to break up, but planned to do one more tour of continental Europe first. There, they assembled an international group of fellow traveler bands to start a movement they dubbed Rock in Opposition (RIO). The goal was solidarity between anti-commercial music groups against the music industry. They never did a tour, but they did play a festival together (which, of course, lost money). RIO as an organization, as well as Henry Cow, disintegrated in 1979, but RIO became a genre term for their style of music. Henry Cow and RIO have had a few reunions, and they also have had much influence in the avant-garde scene; Joanna Newsom, for example, considers Henry Cow among her favorite artists.

# Hargrove's review

I was completely flabbergasted by this album my first three listens. It is chaotic and complex to the extreme. I thought it was nearly unlistenable. I pushed through though because I knew Joanna Newsom considered them a major influence on her early records and I wanted to hear whatever it was she did. So I kept listening. And eventually, sometime around listen 4 or 5, all of a sudden things began to click. I would listen to “War” and I wouldn’t hear two and a half minutes of unmitigated, incomprehensible chaos; instead, I began to hear a tight and memorable vocal melody, an ever-shifting but grokable rhythm, so many sections and ideas packed into such a short time. The chorus is straight up catchy once your brain acclimates. The jazzy section in the middle reminds me of the jazzy section in “21st Century Schizoid Man” but much better (largely thanks to it being concise). “Living in the Heart of the Beast” was still a daunting, sprawling epic, but it finally hit me how transcendent the last 5 minutes are. Dagmar sounds like she’s summiting a huge mountain peak as she delivers the ascending lines in the verses. Everything is upwards, onwards, triumphant. I still struggle with some of the noodling in the 10 minutes that precede, but knowing that I’m in for such a release makes it much easier to stomach. And “Beautiful as the Moon; Terrible as an Army with Banners” is much simpler and (dare I say) more conventional than those other two, but it just sounds good. It has an ending almost as good as the second track! It also has a fantastic bass line.

What I have not found myself warming to, even after all these listens, are the free improvisation tracks “Beginning: The Long March” and “Morning Star”. What a crock of bullshit. I hated “Moonchild” on *In the Court of the Crimson King*, but listening to “Living in the Heart of the Beast” I heard some similar sounds used in an interesting way, and it made me respect the King Crimson song at least a bit. The free improv songs on here are such a waste of time though. “Morning Star” is the better one if only because it just has more meat to its sound. The other is so empty. In this sense it reminds me a lot of *NEU!*, which had three interesting and good experimental tracks and three unlistenable ambient pieces of garbage.

I could ultimately hear many aspects, particularly in the unorthodox melodies and lilting rhythms, that would make their way into Joanna Newsom’s music. This was cool for me, as I really really love Joanna Newsom. Her music is strictly better, certainly. The ideas are all packed into perfectly written and executed units. I lost hope long ago and that I would find any artist that outshines her, and even her influences don’t come particularly close. But I did enjoy hearing those early ideas, and I did think there were some seriously impressive moments on this completely independent of anything to do with Joanna Newsom. It’s a cool record, one full of both ideas and triumphs, and one that I will certainly revisit. I am so glad I kept pushing.

Favorite tracks: War, Living in the Heart of the Beast, Beautiful as the Moon; Terrible as an Army with Banners

Least favorite track: Beginning: The Long March
