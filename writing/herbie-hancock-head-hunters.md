# Write-up

Herbie Hancock is a jazz pianist and bandleader originally from Chicago. He was born in 1940 to working class parents and was classically trained in the piano as a child. A prodigy, he performed with the Chicago Symphony Orchestra at age 11. Hancock attended Grinnell College in Iowa and graduated in 1960 with a dual degree in music and electrical engineering. Hancock released his debut album, *Takin’ Off*, in 1962 on Blue Note Records. The song “Watermelon Man” (remade ten years later on *Head Hunters*) would become a major hit for drummer Mongo Santamariá, and the album caught the attention of legendary trumpeter Miles Davis. Davis’ old band, the First Great Quintet (consisting of Miles Davis on trumpet, John Coltrane on sax, Red Garland on piano, Paul Chambers on bass, and Philly Joe Jones on drums) had just fallen apart, and he recruited Hancock into the Second Great Quintet (Davis on trumpet, Hancock on piano, Wayne Shorter on sax, Ron Carter on bass, and Tony Williams on drums) in 1963. Hancock recorded 6 albums as a member of the Second Great Quintet from 1963-1968, as well as numerous side albums with other musicians like Wayne Shorter, Freddie Hubbard, and Eric Dolphy (just the ones who are on our list). He also composed the score for Michelangelo Antonioni’s *Blowup* in 1966, and he would go on to score many more films. In 1968, he was kicked out of the Second Great Quintet and replaced by pianist Chick Correa. Regardless, Hancock played on Davis’ 1969 album *In a Silent Way* and would later play on Davis’ 1972 album *On the Corner*. Though Hancock did not play on Davis’ seminal 1970 album *Bitches Brew*, he became heavily influenced by Davis’ embrace of rock and pop, as well as his use of electronic instruments. Hancock put together a sextet retrospectively called Mwandishi and released three albums from 1971-1973. These albums experimented heavily with electronic pianos and helped popularize jazz fusion, a genre that fuses jazz with various popular styles like rock and R&B.

After the Mwandishi albums, Hancock put together a quintet in 1973 called the Headhunters, consisting of Hancock on keyboard, Bennie Maupin on sax, Paul Jackson on bass guitar, Harvey Mason on drums, and Bill Summers on various instruments. They released *Head Hunters* that same year, pioneering the form of jazz fusion known as jazz-funk. Funk was a highly rhythmic form of dance music that developed in the late 1960s from the soul music popular with black people at the time. James Brown is credited as the creator of funk, and George Clinton of Parliament and Funkadelic (the latter has an album on our list) and Sly Stone of Sly and the Family Stone helped perfect it by bringing in psychedelic influences. Hancock combined funk with jazz on *Head Hunters*. “Chameleon” and “Watermelon Man” both became jazz standards, the latter being a reworking of a popular song Hancock originally released in his hard-bop days in 1963. The whistle sound on the song is actually Summers blowing into a beer bottle. “Sly” is a tribute to funk artist Sly Stone, who died last month. The album was a critical and commercial success, helping to reinvigorate the American jazz scene after it waned in popularity in the late 60s. It ranks highly on multiple lists of the best albums of all time.

Hancock released another album with the Headhunters, but by 1975 he left the group to make more pop music. He would endure as one of the most famous jazz musicians in the US over the next few decades, though his critical reputation sank. A 1983 experiment in hip-hop, “Rockit”, was the first mainstream single to feature record scratching. He recorded a duet album with Wayne Shorter in 1997 called *1+1*. In 2007 he released an album of Joni Mitchell covers featuring a spoken word composition by Leonard Cohen called *River: The Joni Letters* which shockingly won album of the year at the Grammys in 2008. He performed at Barack Obama’s first inauguration and is still alive today.

# Hargrove's review

This is the first album we’ve listened to that comes out of the 60s and 70s soul and funk scene (unless you count Talking Heads lol) and I was honestly pretty floored to hear it. This is such a fun style of music. I couldn’t get enough of it, and with each listen I was excited to tap along and move my body. There are 4 tracks on this song which go in order from most funk to most jazz. The first 3 tracks are all bangers, but unfortunately I really do not care for the closer, which drags the entire album down with 10 minutes of slow, aimless noodling. It’s the quite the dirge. I like the synth undulations in the second half that remind me of Pokemon Mystery Dungeon, but those last for about 15 seconds, and the less said about this track the better. So just remember, everything positive I say about this album going forward comes with the caveat that “Vein Melter” takes up a quarter of the space and does almost nothing with it.

“Chameleon” is an unbelievable banger. What a way to start an album. That bass synth vamp is so goddamn good. Over the next 15 minutes, it plays for 10, and that is absolutely earned. BOW, BOW, BOW, bow bow bow, BOW, BOW, BOW, bow bow bow. So simple, yet so effective. The main sax line is wonderful as well. The bones of this track’s structure are so solid. I also love the frantic strumming in the bass guitar that is so typical of funk. I don’t love the keyboard solo, but it is at least futuristic and spacey. The middle section is much jazzier than the main song and drops the main vamp. It starts with a piano and bass guitar duet that I adore. The synths are gliding and the drums get very rhythmically interesting. The jazzy part builds and builds until things get so groovy, and then that bass vamp comes back in at the end like an old friend. Stellar, stellar track. “Watermelon Man” is also quite good. I really love the sound of that beer bottle blowing. I even did back before I knew it was a beer bottle and just thought it was some kind of weird flute! The rest of the song is nice as well, with a great sax lead and a clean bass line. “Sly” opens very smoothly, but then it breaks into by far the most chaotic section of the entire album. This is the first song where things feel like true fusion instead of mostly funk. I appreciate the avant-garde solos in the sax and the piano, with the former in particular reminding me of Ornette Coleman. I think the final minute is one of the prettiest parts of the whole album; it’s so nocturnal and gorgeous, the saxophone can evoke the night like nothing else.

Overall, I was very impressed by 75% of this album. I foresee myself coming back to those three songs quite often going forward. Unfortunately, that closer is a millstone around its neck, and prevents me from putting this album in A Tier. Still, really cool, really fun record.

Favorite tracks: Chameleon, Watermelon Man, Sly

Least favorite track: Vein Melter
