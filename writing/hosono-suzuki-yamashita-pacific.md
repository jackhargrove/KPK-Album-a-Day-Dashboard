# Write-up

Haruomi Hosono, Shigeru Suzuki, and Tatsuro Yamashita are Japanese male solo artists born in the late 40s/early 50s. All three are known for their influence on and performance of the genre of city pop. Suzuki and Hosono met while university students and formed the seminal band Happy End in 1969, the first rock group to sing in Japanese and an ancestor of nearly all Japanese pop music. Happy End broke up in 1972, and Hosono and Suzuki would form multiple other bands together throughout the 70s. Yamashita began his career in 1975. At some point, he met the other two, and they made *Pacific* in 1978. After *Pacific*, Suzuki would largely fade into obscurity, but Hosono and Yamashita would become super famous. In late 1978, Hosono founded Yellow Magic Orchestra, a pioneering synthpop group whose impact on electronic music is comparable to Kraftwerk. Yamashita became one of the biggest pop stars in Japan in the 1980s. He is referred to as the “King of City Pop”.

*Pacific* is a 1978 collaboration between Hosono, Suzuki, and Yamashita. Hosono and Suzuki each contributed three tracks while Yamashita contributed only two, though one of them is a long track in two parts. It is considered an early and influential example of a genre that would eventually be called “city pop” and would become the most popular style of music in Japan during the 1980s. *Pacific* achieves its signature sound through exploration of genres that all fall under the umbrella category of “easy listening”, a broad and often derogatory term to describe music that is designed to be inoffensive background music. You might ask yourself, “But wait! Ambient music is meant to be listened to in the background!”. Though you are correct, Brian Eno explicitly created ambient music to provide an alternative to easy listening. One type of easy listening explored on *Pacific* is exotica, which developed in the mid-50s and made use of Polynesian instruments and rhythms as Hawaii was becoming the 50th state. Another is lounge, which also developed in the 50s from jazz and traditional pop and was often played in the lounge areas of hotels. *Pacific* also pulls from space age pop, which was basically lounge but with a futuristic sound inspired by the space race. A final major easy listening style to influence this album is smooth jazz, an easily absorbed form of jazz that might be the most hated genre of all time. Easy listening had a semi-ironic revival in the American alternative scene of the mid-90s called Cocktail Nation, and Stereolab are known for incorporating lounge and space age pop sounds into their music.

*Pacific* combined these nostalgic easy listening genres with the burgeoning electronic sounds of the late 70s like electro-disco and early synthpop. Through this album and their other work, Hosono, Suzuki, and Yamashita would all be instrumental in the development of city pop. City pop takes these sounds and turns them into an upbeat pop explosion that reflected the economic prosperity of Japanese urban areas during the late 70s and 80s and had an outsized influence that reached the West. Through Yellow Magic Orchestra, it helped herald the electronic music heyday of the 80s. It also had significant influence on the music of Japanese video games which gained worldwide popularity. And with the advent of the internet in the 21st century, city pop’s optimistic 80s nostalgia and Japanese origin would make it influential on vaporwave. Much of Kero Kero Bonito’s sound comes from city pop and, through them and others, it had an influence on hyperpop. If you’re interested in the quintessential city pop sound, Yamashita’s “Sparkle” and “Ride on Time” are great examples. As the 80s ended and Japanese economy took a turn for the worse in 1990, city pop was largely supplanted by the indie/alternative rock of Shibuya-kei, a movement that included Fishmans. Still, city pop is likely the most important genre to originate in Japan.

# Hargrove's review

I’ve always liked the idea of city pop, largely through the lens of Kero Kero Bonito and the music of some Japanese video games I played as a kid. I have never consciously listened to the genre though, so I was pretty excited for this album. I don’t think it’s the most impressive thing in the world, but I think it’s fun and really well-crafted. I also think it has unexpected depth for this style of music. Obviously the closer is decently experimental, but honestly there are kinda harsh timbres, busy rhythmic ideas, and weird improvisational melodic ideas all over this thing. You really have to focus to pick them out, though, because the artists did such a great job of folding everything together into one dreamy, idyllic musical outing. Every second of this album sounds like the beach. There’s nothing profound or challenging here, and this is certainly not one of the albums I’ve been most floored by. But it is really nice, really fun, really sunny, and I definitely see myself coming back to it when the vibe is right. Also, you should check out Yamashita’s solo stuff, it’s quite good.

The main synth in “最後の楽園” (track 1) has a timbre that I just love. It kind of reminds me of a theremin, and the whole thing kind of reminds me of *SpongeBob SquarePants*. There’s a great melody too. The whole thing is chill and peaceful. “コーラル・リーフ” (track 2) has a higher-pitched synth playing the secondary melody that I feel like is such a classic of city pop and city pop-inspired music and I really like it. This track does a cool job of approximating vocals with the instrumental, having defined verses and choruses. The string swells in the chorus bring much more warmth and energy to this album than I could have expected. “スタルジア・オブ・アイランド” (track 3) is nice, but I don’t think a 10 minute song suits the style well at all. Outside of the amazing moment where there are vocals near the end, I’m mostly hoping to get to the next side of the record. “スラック・キー・ルンバ” (track 4) is my least favorite. There’s just really not much to grab onto. “パッション・フラワー” (track 5) brings the album back in a big way though. It’s the only certified banger on the thing. Suzuki was on one for this album, man. It has such a catchy melody, the sax builds so perfectly, and again there’s just an insane amount of energy and, dare I say, tension built for this kind of music. I am a sucker for the Spanish guitar, and it’s the main instrument on “ノアノア” (track 6). And yeah, it’s gorgeous. But the song overall doesn’t work for me. I generally liked “キスカ” (track 7) on most of my listens, but it failed to grip me on my final one. It does kind of sound like one of those songs in a Jon Bois video though. “コズミック・サーフィン” (track 8) is a phenomenal closer. It’s crazy to have a genuinely experimental piece after all of the easy listening. And yet, despite the starkness of the synth, the swirling of the melody, and the complexity of the rhythm, it still just works. Masterful.

Look, this is not a particularly striking work of art. It doesn’t leave me thinking about it for hours after I stop listening. But it achieves exactly what it sets out to do. This is music that is optimized for utility only during the brief moment that the waves are past your left ear but haven’t quite left through the right. The only unit of time it’s concerned with is the moment. And I think there’s beauty in that. I think we can find inspiration in letting go, turning off, enjoying right now. As the great Carly Rae Jepsen once said, “Don't think it over/Oh/I just think we're overthinking it”.

Favorite tracks: 最後の楽園 (track 1), コーラル・リーフ (track 2), パッション・フラワー (track 5), コズミック・サーフィン (track 8)

Least favorite track: スラック・キー・ルンバ (track 4)

# Aidan's review

While this is technically not city pop, it boasts the most influential name in city pop and the sparkling plastic production that the genre is known for. The modern revival of city pop irks me to no end. It's the endpoint of a search for the most naively commercial genre. That is to say, I'm not a fan. Still, this album is really well executed, and I found myself enjoying it in spite of my prior biases. It's not ambitious, but it achieves pretty much everything it sets out to do. If on-hold music were a category for the Grammies, this would be a shoe in. This is just such a slick record that any sort of emotion that I could have for it simply slides off. It's fine but I never find myself attached to any individual element, the melodies are generic but well done, the ocean effect on the third track is cliche, the entire thing evokes a feeling of deja vu, as if you've heard this in the elevator, while shopping for taupe wallpaper, or filling out insurance forms for your orthodontist. The production is really the highlight of this album, it's incredibly well done. Every note sounds great, but the whole is less than the sum of its parts. Track 8 is the sole exception, and serves as a breath of fresh air and makes enough use of the stereo to pique my interest. I don't regret listening to this album and I won't regret forgetting it within a week.
