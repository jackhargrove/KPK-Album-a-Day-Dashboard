# Write-up

"Hūsker Dū" means "Do you remember?" in Norwegian and Danish. It's fitting for a band that chose to reconcile punk with the classic rock that the likes of Johnny Rotten had so loudly rejected. The Hüskers (they changed the macron to an umlaut) met in St Paul, Minnesota in 1978 and originally started as a quartet, with Bob Mould on guitar/vocals, Greg Norton on bass, Grant Hart on drums and Charlie Pine on keyboard. The first three met at a Ramones concert and the Queens punk legends featured among the many covers that the Hüskers started out playing. As the band began to venture to write original songs, the trio disliked having a keyboard, and kicked Pine out of the band. The remaining trio found themselves at home among the Twin Cities punk scene. Black Flag and Jello Biafra were among the fans and the band was eventually signed to Black Flag guitarist Greg Ginn's SST Records. At this point, Hüsker Dü's live sound was known for speed and aggression above all else.

While the Hüskers were ready to release their first album, the poorly managed SST was not, and so the Hüskers were referred to D. Boon and Mike Watt of the Minutemen, who released "Land Speed Record" on their label, New Alliance. They self released their next album, "Everything Falls Apart" before finally managing to get their third album, "Metal Circus" on SST. While the first two albums were breakneck hardcore, the band had more wide ranging interests than many of their peers. Their debut single sounded more like Public Image Ltd than the Pistols, and Metal Circus featured a bit of classic rock harmony, something uncommon in the hardcore scene at the time. This blend of old and new drew attention from college radio DJs and major label Elektra Records, who tried and failed to sign the band.

The success gave the band members confidence to explore musically even more, and this lead to the creation of Zen Arcade. Made on a shoestring budget of $3,200, 40 hours of studio time and all but 2 tracks being recorded on the first take, the record was about as DIY as they come. It was also as ambitious as they come, pushing punk boundaries by being a concept album, including a variety of musical styles and running a whole 70 minutes. The album was a critical hit, selling out SST's limited pressings nearly immediately and inspiring their peers, the Minutemen, to make their own ambitious double album, "Double Nickels on the Dime".

After another two records were released on SST, the band grew frustrated with the limited size of the indie label, often finding that they didn't have enough records to sell. With the clout to demand creative control, the band saw no downside to signing with Warner Bros, and released their most commercially successful album with them, "Candy Apple Grey".

9 years into their time together, the band began to run into problems. Creative differences between Mould and Hart began to arise, Hart's ongoing drug use began to become a major issue, and the suicide of their manager under the newfound pressure of being part of a major label was the nail in the coffin for the band. The band was at their peak of fame when they disbanded, booking gigs with Joan Rivers and the Today show. The band is widely seen as among the most influential on alternative rock, with Zen Arcade along with Double Nickels and Daydream Nation being refereed to as the founding holy trinity of alternative rock. The Pixies and Nirvana were among their biggest admirers. All 3 members formed their own bands, while Norton also opened a restaurant in Minneapolis. Hart died of liver cancer at 54.

# Hargrove's review

I’ve been really excited for this one ever since we listened to Minutemen because that album really piqued my interest and was a direct response to this album. I’ve also been wanting to hear some melodic hardcore (I can’t wait for The Descendents) because it seems like a style I would like. Now that I’ve listened to it, I have to say, I’m pretty torn on *Zen Arcade*. I certainly like it less than I do *Double Nickels on the Dime*, although I don’t think they sound much alike at all. In fact, as I listen to more hardcore, it becomes clear that that Minutemen’s only connection to hardcore was the scene. They don’t sound like hardcore at all. I might even go so far as to say that Minutemen are spiritually hardcore but not sonically hardcore, while Hüsker Dü are sonically hardcore but not spiritually hardcore. That last stipulation might be hard to justify, but they were injecting pop into such a defiant genre, while Minutemen were making their own sound unlike anything before or since. It’s that pop sensibility though that makes for the best moments on this album. As a collection of songs, I think *Zen Arcade* is pretty decent. “Pink Turns to Blue” is an all-time great track that I’m kicking myself for never having heard before, and there are more than a few proto-pop-punk bangers. When they’re combining catchy pop structures with supersonic post-punk instrumentals and screamed vocals, they’re at their best. However, I do not think any of the adventurous or experimental shit panned out at all. I respect them for taking risks and pushing the genre forward, but I think a lot of it failed. For one, the “concept” of the album is extremely tenuous at best. I feel like everyone saying this is some kind of punk opera about a kid leaving home but things get worse and then it turns out it was a dream are taking crazy pills. Not only is there very little to connect one song to the next, there are some songs that very blatantly don’t take place in that narrative like “Newest Industry” and “Turn on the News”. But that’s whatever, most concept albums are a little stupid. It’s the stuff like the momentum-killing “Hare Krisna”, or the uneven interludes, or the aimless fourteen minute closer that do nothing but take away from the punchiness of this album’s best songs. I think this album is good, but it could have been so much more.

I’m really happy with what I wrote above, so I’m going to try to speed through song-specific thoughts. “Something I Learned Today” is a fabulous opener that proves what hardcore can be, and “Broken Home, Broken Heart” keeps the heat coming. I respect that they went acoustic on “Never Talking to You Again”, but I consistently found the vocal melody to be unpleasant and annoying. “Chartered Trips”, on the other hand, is the album’s second-best song and the basis for all alternative rock to come. Crazy this song came out in 1984. “Hare Krsna” is awful and way too long. “I’ll Never Forget You” is one of the better pure hardcore songs. “The Biggest Lie” is like “Chartered Trips”;lite (complimentary). “What’s Going On” is like “Turn on the News”-lite (derogatory, we’ll get to that later). “Standing by the Sea” has a really pretty vocal melody and a cool cyclical bass line, great track. Side 3 is by far the strongest of the album; “Somewhere” and “Whatever” are both very strong, and “Newest Industry” is alright. “Pink Turns to Blue” is, of course, the best on the album. The guitar gives me the same looming, suspenseful feeling as a clock tower ringing on a foggy night as my time is up.  I do not care for “Turn on the News” and do not understand why it’s so loved. It’s not awful, but everything in the song builds to a climactic chorus, and I think the chorus is really underwhelming. The closer sounds fine but I don’t see a reason for its existence at 14 minutes.

Some very good songs, but a mess of an album. *Double Nickels* is a mess too, but in a way that makes me want to get lost in the chaos. This makes me want to add 4-5 songs to some playlists where I can consult them in peace.

Favorite tracks: Something I Learned Today, Broken Home, Broken Heart, Chartered Trips, I’ll Never Forget You, The Biggest Lie, Standing by the Sea, Somewhere, Pink Turns to Blue, Whatever

Least favorite track: Hare Krsna
