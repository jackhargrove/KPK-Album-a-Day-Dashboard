# Write-up

J Dilla (real name James Dewitt Yancey) was a hip hop producer from Detroit born in 1974. He was one of the most important producers of the underground hip hop scene in the late 90s and early 2000s before he died of a rare blood clotting disease in 2006 at age 32. His parents were both musicians, and their training of him from a young age resulted in Dilla having perfect pitch. He became interested in hip hop in high school and learned to use music workstations at age 18. In 1994, at age 20, he became friends with Q-Tip of A Tribe Called Quest, and in 1995 he signed to a major label as part of a duo with MC Phat Kat called 1st Down. The label dropped them after one single, and Dilla returned to the underground. He formed the group Slum Village, who released its first album in 1997 and became well-known locally in Detroit. Although the style was similar to A Tribe Called Quest, Dilla resented the comparison; while most underground hip hop (then called “backpack rap”) was associated with positivity, Dilla’s music was about sex, violence, and other traditional hip hop topics. He began producing songs for a variety of artists, including Michael Jackson, A Tribe Called Quest, De La Soul, Janet Jackson, and The Pharcyde, with almost half the songs on the latter’s second album *Labcabincalifornia* being produced by Dilla (including the incredible “Runnin”, one of my all-time favorite hip hop songs). Slum Village signed to a major label in 2000, and around thh same time Dilla became a founding member of the Soulquarians with Questlove (of The Roots) and D’Angelo. The Soulquarians would go on to become a major collective of black musicians, including Eryka Badu, Roy Hargrove (hey!), Common, Mos Def, Q-Tip (of A Tribe Called Quest), Bilal, and others. Dilla released his debut solo album, *Welcome 2 Detroit*, in 2001. Important note: up until that point, Dilla had used the name Jay Dee; however, he changed it to J Dilla in 2001 to avoid confusion with another artist named JD. He began to record a second album, which was to include guest production from artists like Madlib and Kanye West, but disputes with the labels led to it going unfinished and unreleased. He released a collab album with Madlib in 2003 under the name Jaylib called *Champion Sound*.

After 2003, Dilla’s output slowed significantly. He also lost an extreme amount of weight, and was very rarely seen. He revealed to the press that he had both thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura and lupus and was frequently hospitalized. While in the hospital, he began making his final album as quickly as he could before he died. His mother brought all the equipment to Cedars Sinai hospital in LA, and Dilla produced the beats obsessively as his health deteriorated. His mother would massage his fingers between sessions as the clots made them painful to use. The album was called *Donuts*, as the last track loops into the first one, and it was released in February 2006. Dilla died three days later. Or so the story goes. I was heartbroken to learn while writing this that there was a book released in 2022 that disputes this story. They instead say that *Donuts* was based on a 30 minute existing tape that producer Jeff Janks formed into an album from the studio with Dilla’s approval every step of the way. Either way, the album was Dilla’s last statement to the world. The tracks are all short and instrumental because Dilla was too weak to rap. Like his other music, the beats are not quantized, meaning they have a human feel with drums that aren’t exactly on beat. Many rappers have rapped over the beats from the album in tribute to Dilla after his death, including Drake, Nas, and Ghostface Killa. Regardless of how true the traditional story of the album’s production is, its music is one of the great triumphs of instrumental hip hop, and alongside *Madvillainy* as one of the defining pieces of the 2000s underground scene.

# Hargrove's review

I have probably listened to this album and *Skylarking* a combined 200 times, so it’s funny to me to have them back-to-back. I can’t read or write when listening to music with lyrics, so this was my go-to study album (alongside Steve Reich’s *Music for 18 Musicians*). Despite this, I still felt the need to do a full note-taking listen so that I could cite specific tracks. Everything in this album just flows into the next. It might as well be one long, extremely dynamic song. You get little flitters of ideas, some experiments on said idea, and then it’s on to the next. This almost entirely a positive quality, however. It’s a complete and total vibe. You never know what you’re gonna get, even when you’ve heard it a thousand times. A smooth as hell soul track, a classic hip hop banger, better guitar work than you’ve heard on any rock album, an interesting soundscape, actual circus music: this album truly has it all. And it blends it all together seamlessly. It’s often said that Dilla intended for others to rap over these beats after he was gone, and many rappers have. Even with this knowledge, though, I honestly find that hard to believe. It doesn’t sound like anything is missing. These songs sound like they were meant to be heard instrumentally. Despite this, they don’t sound complete. They’re unfinished, much like Dilla’s life. If you cracked open Hip Hop’s heart, this album is the fluid that would pour out.

There are too many tracks to go over them each individually, but these are the ones that stand out to me, and that I consistently return to. “Workinonit” is such a banger. It’s the only song over 2 minutes, and it deserves every second of its run time. We, of course, love a Beastie Boys sample, but beyond that I love how the guitars sound like a race car shifting gears. And the second part provides more of a downer foil to the first. I’m a big fan of the nonsense vocal samples on “Waves” and how they cascade through the song. The slide guitar in “Stop” is a favorite of mine, as is the shift in the vocals at the chorus. The horns in “The Diff’rence” are a very welcome texture, and man is the track smooth. The piano in “Mash” is so nocturnal and pretty, it carries the song. “Time: The Donut of the Heart” has such bouncy guitar work, it’s so uplifting and fun. “Lightworks” is weird in that I never know if I actually love it, or if it’s just very memorable. Nevertheless it sounds super cool and unique; the computer-y ascending bits are awesome, the vocal sample is great, and the bass sounds inquisitive. “Two Can Win” starts an incredible three track sequence, the highlight of the album. I’ve always found the title of this song extremely optimistic and reassuring, contradicting the zero-sum message of the “only one can win” in the track itself. The drums are also phenomenal. Once the chopped vocals really get going in “Don’t Cry”, oh man, what a great beat. This is one I can most see people rapping over. And finally, “Anti-American Graffiti” is my favorite track. That guitar is more wistful than most vocalists could ever dream of being, and I love the deranged rambling of the sample. “Geek Down” has great drums, psychedelic guitar, and I can’t complain. The album drops in quality a bit after this point, but “Dilla Says Go” has a wonderful soul sound and a deeply hopeful feel. The last great track is fittingly “Last Donut of the Night” with its chilling strings and strong pulse.

Almost every track is good in some way, even the oddball “The Twister (Huh, What)” and “The Factory”, which add diversity and texture. The above are just the ones I can pick out of a lineup. Even if my understanding of this record’s creation was shattered earlier today, this is an amazing record. Some of the best that instrumental hip hop has to offer.

Favorite tracks: Workinonit, Waves, Stop, The Diff’rence, Mash, Time: The Donut of the Heart, Lightworks, Two Can Win, Don’t Cry, Anti-American Graffiti, Geek Down, Dilla Says Go, Last Donut of the Night

Least favorite track: People

# Aidan's review

Cool, super unique record. We did Ariel Pink last week, and while I liked that album, I didn't get the "hypnagogic" feel from it that the dictionary definition. Donuts does that. The variety is insane, and the jittery feel of the sampling combine to make it feel like you're drifting off as the grooviest TV channel in the world switches channels. Pretty much every track feels like it's building to some sick verse that never comes, which is a super unique feeling. You'll stop paying attention and notice that you somehow went a whole 5 tracks, but each track is wholly unique. The only track I find mildly annoying is The Factory, but with 31 tracks, it's a hiccup in an otherwise incredible experience. One of those albums I think everyone should listen to just because there's very few things out there like it. Beyond the obvious hip hop ties, the two things that came to mind when listening to this were vaporwave (specifically the "It's Your Move" sample off Floral Shoppe) and the Avalanches. Great album that just breezes by, an easy and enjoyable listen.
