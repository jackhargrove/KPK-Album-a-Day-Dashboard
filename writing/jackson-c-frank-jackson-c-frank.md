# Write-up


# Hargrove's review

I was not super impressed by this album. The best thing I can say about it is that predicted the style of 70s folk artists like Nick Drake (and, by extension, 90s folk artists like Elliott Smith) back in 1965. It definitely feels forward looking for a time when American folk was still synonymous with the proto-hippie Greenwich Village scene, and forward looking in a different way than Dylan was forward looking. But the issue is just that the songs aren’t all that great. They’re not terrible by any means, just bland and not all that moving to me. I absolutely adore Elliott Smith and Nick Drake and Phoebe Bridgers, so the formula of sad guy/girl + sad guitar can do wonders for me. What makes those artists so good, though, is that they’re all brilliant songwriters. They effectively convey their sadness through concise lyrics, catchy vocal melodies, and sparse yet spacious guitar atmospheres. I often find it much easier to memorize the lyrics of the best folk singer/songwriters than I do any other genre because of the killer execution on just a few necessary elements. Good songwriting ties it all together. With Jackson C. Frank, his songwriting just isn’t there for the most part for me, especially on the first half of the album. The only song with a vocal line that gets stuck in my head is “My Name Is Carnival”. That song, along with a couple others, is among the few that create an effective guitar atmosphere as well. Reading about Jackson C. Frank’s life story tells me that he was certainly filling to the brim with sadness and dysfunction, but only “Milk and Honey” stirs a real sadness in me. Phoebe Bridgers seems like she’s lived a pretty damn good life, and yet she has over a dozen songs that break me down more than anything on this album. I don’t know. Just not for me.

“Blues Run the Game” is far from the worst track on this album, but I really have trouble understanding the hype. He uses “the blues” cleverly as a literary device, but I found the lyrics too nonspecific to be truly moving. He gets the blues from booze, gambling, and women? Wouldn’t have guessed. I also find the way he says “mama” grating. “Don’t Look Back” is my least favorite track because of how out of place it sounds. It plays to his strengths less than any other track. It’s upbeat, loud, and randomly political. It just does not work. By far the best track on side 1 is “Kimbie”. He kills the vocal delivery here, putting so much sauce on “Baby, where you ben so long” at the beginning of verse 3. “Yellow Walls” has some good guitar flourishes and bass notes but is otherwise forgettable. “Here Come the Blues” sounds like a standard acoustic blues, and I’d rather just listen to Robert Johnson in that case. Side 2 is much stronger than side 1, kicking off with the quite good “Milk and Honey”. Frank finally creates a genuinely despondent atmosphere with just his vocals and guitar. The use of “gold and silver” in the first and final verses is quite clever as well. “My Name Is Carnival” is the best track on the album. I’ve had the refrain stuck in my head for a few days, though I have no clue what it means. The guitar line is nice too. “I Want to Be Alone (Dialogue)” keeps up the grim atmosphere to round out a nice trilogy. “Just Like Anything” breaks that atmosphere, but it’s still good because finally there’s some decent songwriting. The “Just like anything, to sing, to sing, to sing” flows so well. I’m surprised this isn’t one of the ones that has become a semi-standard. “You Never Wanted Me” returns to the weakness of forgettable first side songs.

It’s hard to see me ever coming back to any of these songs when later artists just did everything better in every aspect. Props for getting there in 1965, but I don’t think it’s for me. I feel really similarly about this album as I do De La Soul’s debut.

Favorite tracks: Kimbie, Milk and Honey, My Name Is Carnival, I Want to Be Alone (Dialogue), Just Like Anything

Least favorite track: Don’t Look Back
