# Write-up

Jethro Tull are a folk rock and progressive rock band from Blackpool, England whose lineup at the time of this album consisted of Ian Anderson (frontman, vocalist, acoustic guitarist, flautist, violinist, trumpeter, saxophonist, accordionist, songwriter), Martin Barre (electric guitarist, lutist, flautist), John Evan (pianist, organist, harpsichordist), Jeffrey Hammond (bassist), and Barriemore Barlow (drummer). They were all school friends who were inspired to form a band because of The Beatles in the early 60s. They went through some lineup changes over the years, including a brief stint with guitarist Tony Iommi of Earth in 1968. Iommi would return to Earth after only a couple weeks, and not long after, Earth changed their name to Black Sabbath and put out a few records of note. Jethro Tull put out 3 albums of hard rock with a strong folk influence in the late 60s. Their second went number 1 in the UK. Their third, *Aqualung*, received significant critical attention. They were notable for Anderson’s use of the flute, an instrument he picked up because he thought his competitors were better guitarists, and for the unified themes across their albums. *Aqualung* in particular was considered a concept album by critics, something that was still notable and new in the early 70s. Anderson vehemently disagreed with this assessment, thinking it was just a collection of songs, so he decided to make the mother of all concept albums to make fun of the critics: one record, one song. Similarly, Anderson wanted to make fun of the now-massive progressive rock movement, which was a critical darling. So Jethro Tull wrote 13 songs in the progressive rock style, stitched them together with extended musical passages, added a shitload of random instruments like the lute, and called it *Thick as a Brick*. The record packaging was unique, with a twelve page foldout cover that mimicked a newspaper. One of the pages includes an epic poem written by a fictional 8 year old named Gerald Bostock, which is actually the lyrics for the album. All of the lyrics deal with the theme of maturing and childhood. The album received lukewarm reviews at the time, but is now considered one of the pinnacles of prog rock. Jethro Tull continued until 2012, and then reformed in 2017 and is still playing today, albeit with Anderson as the sole founding member remaining.

# Hargrove's review

I was really dreading listening to this because I already don’t love prog, and on top of that Jethro Tull isn’t at least like Pink Floyd or King Crimson which at least make me feel like I’m listening to something important. While this hasn’t won me over for the prog menace or anything, I would definitely say I enjoyed this. I’m ranking it one step below *Wish You Were Here*, which has been my favorite prog album so far. I like the folk elements that you get throughout, not only with like the flute and the fiddle but even something about some of the melodies feels British folksy. There were some individual stanzas I enjoyed lyrically, particularly the opening stanza and the entire section about comic book heroes. Overall, though, I didn’t really think it worked as the “ultimate concept album” because I had trouble following it all the way through. Like I get that childhood and maturity are consistent themes for some parts of it, but it didn’t really outdo something like *Songs for the Deaf* for me in that regard. I did like the mishmash of instruments, it sounded unlike anything I had heard before and you’re constantly like “huh, that’s new”. I do think some of the extended instrumentals connecting the movements, the connective tissue as it were, goes on a little long and feels like filler at times. Aidan is correct about it succumbing to the worst instincts of the genre about a quarter of the way through side 2. That being said, the first like minute and a half of side 2 sounds pretty epic. Also, despite being able to tell that it was constantly sneering and making fun of something, it was never actually funny for me. I was somewhat torn on the vocals. I thought they fit the album well, but they got a little grating at points. Overall, I definitely think listening to this was a positive experience. It’s never gonna be one of my favorite albums or anything, but it’s unique sounding enough to stimulate my desire for the strange, and I definitely see myself coming back to it at some point in my life.

# Aidan's review

My favorite prog album so far. The first half avoids all the normal pitfalls of prog, keeping momentum and avoiding noodlely sections with the help of the marching drums and flute. The faux-epic vocals are infectiously fun, and the lyrics are just concrete enough to draw you in. The second half starts out with similar energy, but doesn’t fully match up to the first and starts to return to the natural place of prog, up its own ass, but still, the noodling feels hard won after how breakneck the first 25 minutes were. When the original themes start to reestablish themselves, it makes for a fitting ending. It may be a parody of the genre, but it manages to avoid the worst tropes and that results in a great product.
