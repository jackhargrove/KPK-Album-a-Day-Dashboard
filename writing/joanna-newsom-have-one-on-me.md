# Write-up

**Joanna Newsom - *Have One on Me***

After releasing her second album *Ys* in 2006, Joanna Newsom was more successful than anyone ever thought she would be; at age 24, she had turned her idiosyncratic folk music into an album that charted at #137 on the Billboard 200 and eventually sold a quarter of a million copies. She embarked on a world tour, often playing *Ys* in its entirety backed by entire orchestras. SNL cast members Andy Samberg and Fred Armisen attended one of her concerts in 2008, and after being invited backstage, Newsom and Samberg began dating. They are now married with two children. Also at a concert in 2008, Newsom surprised the audience by playing multiple new songs. Further, these songs were played on piano, which she had not used since her 2004 debut, instead of the harp. Later that year, she went to the studio to record a new album. However, there was one little problem: she couldn't sing. Not that she was bad at it (and fuck anyone who says she is), but she literally could not produce sounds. As it turns out, she had vocal cord nodules ("nodes"), a relatively common affliction for singers caused by vocal overuse and lack of vocal warm-ups. Nodes are typically minor and manageable, but severe cases like Newsom's have a risk of permanently altering a voice, especially if surgery is required. Newsom's doctors recommended surgery but she refused, instead choosing two months of complete vocal rest with no talking whatsoever. When she recovered, she found that her voice had changed. However, while nodes usually change a singer's voice for the worse, in this case it actually deepened and smoothed out her naturally high-pitched and wild voice. The ordeal also inspired her to seek out vocal training for the first time. As a result, Newsom's voice on *Have One on Me* and her fourth album *Divers* is notably less challenging than on her first two records.

After the nodes delay ended, Newsom returned to work on her new album throughout 2009. On *Ys*, she had perfected her orchestral, progressive, and surreal style. Instead of trying to recreate it, she tried something new. The new album was more intimate, both lyrically and musically; she abandoned the orchestras of *Ys* in favor of a stronger focus on her primary instrument and voice, and more songs had lyrics grounded in the present day. She also played piano instead of harp on 5 of the 18 tracks. Instead of the European feel of *Ys*, she found inspiration in Americana and folk artists from the Laurel Canyon scene like Graham Nash and especially Joni Mitchell. Lyrically, it's a loose concept album that tells the story of the end of a relationship from first warning signs to breakup; some songs further the narrative directly, while others do so through allegory. It is commonly speculated that the subject of this album is folk singer and Newsom's ex-boyfriend Bill Callahan. Finally, the album is over two hours long. The album's eighteen tracks are designed to be split up into discs of six tracks each that fit onto a single vinyl LP and stand alone as individual records. The album, *Have One on Me* was announced in January 2010, just a month ahead of its release. When critics learned it was a triple album, they were concerned; in Pitchfork's (positive) review, it says "...when I heard that Newsom would be following [*Ys*] with a 3xLP set...I had troubling visions of 25-minute songs with lyrics that stretched to 5,000 words." And while some reviewers did think it was too long, *HOoM* received similarly raving reviews to *Ys*. It also hit #75 on the Billboard 200.

Since then, Newsom has released one album, 2015's *Divers*. It's her shortest and most accessible album to date, not to mention a beautiful meditation on time, birth, and death. It stands up to any in her discography and I would recommend it to anyone. Unfortunately for me, she has laid low and started a family in the decade since instead of releasing music. Hopefully she returns soon.

**Have One on Me - Narrative Breakdown**

I did one of these for *good kid, m.A.A.d. city* so I feel justified in doing explainers for concept albums I'm particularly fond of. And, like with that one, I will be injecting my personal interpretations as fact alongside generally accepted material.

Disc 1 introduces the album's central relationship and lets the listener in on why it's not working. In the opener "Easy", our female narrator insists she is low-maintenance and comfortable with her boyfriend's level of commitment, though it quickly becomes apparent that she is not. We go from "Easy, easy, my man and me/We could rest and remain here, easily" to "Who asked you, asked you/If you want to be loved by me?/Who died and made you in charge of who loves who?/All the livelong day, if I have my way, I will love you". She is, of course, dissatisfied with his commitment, saying "But One can't carry the weight/Or change the fate of Two". Then, the title track "Have One on Me" takes us into our first allegorical interlude. It gives a full biography of Lola Montez, a 19th century dancer who had an intense affair with King Ludwig I in Bavaria until a revolution against him forced him to abdicate the throne in 1848. As Newsom sings of a woman in the entertainment industry whose controlling but sympathetic behavior in a relationship caused the downfall of both parties, it's not hard to see how it fits into the overarching story. It also introduces one of the album's strongest recurring motifs: alcohol. Lola speaks of a "bottle of white, bottle of red" and beseeches King Ludwig ("Louis") "Won't you have one on me?" Track 3's title, "'81" evokes both the year Newsom was conceived and is also a homophone for A.D. 1. Newsom narrates a story of the Garden of Eden in which she reckons with sin and accepts that bad comes along with good. Following on this theme comes "Good Intentions Paving Co.", a play on the phrase "the road to hell is paved with good intentions". In one of the most direct songs lyrically, Newsom sings of a heated argument with her lover while on a long drive: "And I did not mean to shout, just drive/Just get us out, dead or alive/The road's too long to mention — Lord, it's something to see!/Laid down by the Good Intentions Paving Company". Even though both are good people, sometimes bad comes with the good and they can't help but fight. The coda features the first ideas of separation, but the song ends on the line "When I only want for you to pull over and hold me/'Til I can't remember my own name". In "No Provenance" we find a reprieve, one where our narrator forgets her problems while in the arms of her lover. However, the uneasy feeling doesn't fade away. "Baby Birch" is the final and least ambiguous exploration of a theme prevalent throughout Newsom's first two albums: abortion. Though no one knows for sure, it is heavily speculated that Newsom had an abortion in her early adulthood and took years to come to terms with it as evidenced by songs like "The Sprout and the Bean" and "Emily". Whatever the case, "Baby Birch" is undeniably a song about terminating a pregnancy, opening with "This is the song for Baby Birch/I will never know you". After the narrator spends most of the song imagining what her child would have been like, the outro sticks in the knife: "And there is a barber who's cutting and cutting away/At my only joy/I saw a rabbit as slick as a knife/And as pale as a candlestick/And I had thought it'd be harder to do/But I caught her, and skinned quick/Held her there, kicking and mewling/Upended, unspooling, unsung and blue/Told her 'wherever you go, little runaway bunny, I will find you'/And then she ran, as they're liable to do/Be at peace baby, and be gone". Though the abortion may not have happened in this relationship, the end of the song makes one thing clear: our narrator wants a family. The song ends on one of the only extended instrumental sections in Newsom's discography.

Disc 2 begins with our relationship in a place that's fundamentally flawed but passionate all the way to a place that's broken beyond repair. In the wake of disc 1's long and heartbreaking conclusion, "On a Good Day", Newsom's shortest song, condenses all of those ideas into a minute-and-a-half conclusion. Our narrator can now clearly see the relationship's end in the distance: "Hey, hey, hey, the end is near/On a good day, you can see the end from here". She reflects on her pain at not having a child and knows their love is now "flat and still". And yet, she cannot bring herself to leave just yet: "So 'cross the years and miles, and through/On a good day, you can feel my love for you/Will you leave me be so that we can stay true/To the path that you have chosen?" Much like "Have One on Me", "You and Me Bess" takes a story of a real person from history to describe the state of the relationship. In this case, our narrator takes the form of Dick Turpin, an 18th century highway robber from England with a horse named Black Bess. Our narrator heavily personifies Bess, treating her as a full partner in crime. After years of taking care of each other through a hard life beyond the law in the English countryside, a particularly brutal winter causes Bess to accidentally get caught stealing. However, the narrator refuses to give up Bess, swearing that he alone committed their crimes. As he's hauled away and awaiting execution, he expects Bess to rescue him but, being a horse, she never comes. At the gallows, he sees Bess in the crowd and becomes indignant: "Who do you think that you are, arching your hooves like a crane/In the shallow gutter that lines the boulevards?" But at the moment of his death he forgives her, saying "Kindness comes over me, what was your name? It makes no difference, I'm glad that you came/Forever I'll listen to your glad neighing". Our narrator gave far more to Bess than he received in return, but ultimately, how can you blame her? "In California" is the album's centerpiece and marks the point of no return. Back in reality in the present day, our narrator has turned to heavy drinking to relieve her sorrows and retreats to her hometown in rural Northern California. Here in her sanctuary, what she describes as "my land", she begs her lover not to visit her. And yet, as she has made the decision to separate, she still pines: "While I wait all night for you in California". She wants to leave, but she doesn't want to be single: "I don't belong to anyone/My heart is as heavy as an oil drum/And I don't want to be alone/My heart is yellow as an ear of corn". In the outro, our narrator reflects that her easy life growing up in California made her weak. That leads to "Jackrabbits", in which she sheepishly returns to her lover. She convinces herself: "Telling you I can/I can love you again". "Go Long" retreats once more to allegory, this time through the folktale Bluebeard. In this classic story, the nobleman Bluebeard takes and kills six wives one after another after they fail a test of obedience. Before he can kill his seventh wife, she outsmarts him and inherits his castle. Although she speaks directly to our lover of his philandering and harmful behavior, the song takes aim at the entire male gender ("go long" is a masculine thing to say). Notably, the final verse references several albums by Will Oldham a.k.a. Bonnie "Prince" Billy. Disc 2 closes on "Occident", the term for the West that is equivalent to what "Orient" is for the East. Newsom feels smothered by the ways of her native culture and weary with life.

Disc 3 tells the story of the final disintegration of our central relationship. "Soft as Chalk", one of Newsom's most piercing songs, paints a hellish portrait of their current life together. "I roam around the tidy grounds/Of my dappled sanatorium" she sings, feeling completely trapped. "I sleep like a soldier without rest/But there is no treason where there is only lawlessness" is, to me, one of the most powerful lines on the album. He can't even betray her because there's no trust to begin with. "You give love a little shove and it becomes terror" indeed. In the outro, she fantasizes about leaving. This track is the most severe condemnation of her lover, but we're only at the beginning of disc 3; right now she's angry, but she'll have to reckon with her fault in all of it soon. In "Esme", our narrator meets the young daughter of a good friend and feels that urge to have children once again. For the first time in a while, Esme reminds our narrator of how beautiful life can be: "And this is a world of terrible hardship everywhere/And I search for words to set you at ease/But there in the looking-glass, a kite is soaring/Stilling my warring heart and my trembling knees". On "Autumn", our narrator is once again in her hometown as referenced on "In California". However, summer has now ended and the cold has come at last. She feels old and altered by the end of her relationship: "Would I could tie your lying tongue/Who says that leavin keeps you young". She decides to wait through the winter until she receives a sign that it's time to leave her relationship. In "Ribbon Bows", our lonely narrator decides to purchase a dog for comfort. She goes to the pound and chooses the saddest looking dog she can find. In crisis, she begs the dog for answers: "Black nose of the dog as cold as a rifle/Indicating, with a nudge, God, no God, God, no God/Sweet appraising eye of the dog/Blink once if God, twice if no God". In the outro, she returns to heavy drinking once more, a habit she just cannot kick. The penultimate track "Kingfisher" is the final allegorical epic, mirroring the second track "Have One on Me". It is also, by far, the track that most sounds like it could have been on *Ys*. A kingfisher is a kind of small, colorful bird that often symbolizes calm or peace. Newsom uses birds extensively in her music for symbolism, most famously in the opening lines of "Emily". Our narrator wonders who she will be with when she's old and dying before presenting apocalyptic imagery. She has reached her limit: "I can bear a lot, but not that pall". Of her lover, she says: "He was a kind, unhurried man/With a heavy lip and a steady hand/But he loved me just like a little child/Like a little child loves a little lamb". In the climax, she has a dream of her lover murdering her, and then states what she has finally learned from this whole ordeal: "It is too short, the day we are born/We commence with our dying/Trying to serve with the heart of a child/Kingfisher, lie with the lion". The closer, "Does Not Suffice", is a reprise of the melody from "In California". Finally, our narrator leaves this relationship for good. She's no longer angry, no longer scared, no longer torn, just sad and ready to go. In a reference to the album's opener, she takes some share of the blame for how things turned out: "And everything that could remind you/Of how easy I was not". Still, she reminds her lover of the ways he failed in his responsibilities: "It does not suffice for you to say I am a sweet girl/Or to say you hate to see me sad because of you/It does not suffice to merely lie beside each other/As those who love each other do". Her final thoughts are of what her lover will do when she's gone, the tragic realization that he'll move on despite all of her efforts: "And everywhere I tried to love you is yours again/And only yours". So ends this ill-fated relationship.

# Hargrove's review

TL;DR: One of the best albums of all time. Certainly the best concept album of all time. God, I love this album.

Though *Ys* was my first introduction to Joanna Newsom, *Have One on Me* was the album that made her my favorite artist of all time rather than another passing fascination to cross off my list. I first listened to *Ys* in the Fall of 2017 and was enchanted. It was such an odd little album, unlike anything I had ever heard before, but man was it beautiful and so full of things to dig into. I had my fun with it, but I didn’t really know where to go next with Joanna Newsom. I had seen that *Have One on Me* got similarly rave reviews, but 2 hours is a lot. I had never listened to an album so long! I put off checking it out for a long time, I think into 2018, but I had a long flight coming up (a rarity for me at that time) and figured it was the perfect opportunity. I listened to it once and I fell in love. It’s so different from *Ys*! Immediately you can tell it’s a different collection of timbres and ideas. And yet, it’s so immediately gripping. “Easy” is enrapturing as an opener, beckoning you into an unsettling world. “Have One on Me” is an immediately satisfying compositional masterpiece. “Good Intentions Paving Co.” is a fucking pop song! “Baby Birch” cleaves your heart in two. “On a Good Day” proves she can distill everything beautiful she’s ever made into a minute and a half. “In California” is the most thoughtful reflection on an area I’ve spent much of my life that I’ve ever heard. “Soft as Chalk” brims with more spite, sulfur, and bile than any metal song. “Kingfisher” does *Ys* almost as good as *Ys*. And these are just the songs that stand out on a first listen. There is so goddamn much to dig into on this album. After the listen on the flight, I was sold on Joanna Newsom being my favorite artist. No one had ever made one album that spoke to me that way, let alone two. I quickly devoured *The Milk-Eyed Mender* and *Divers* (both great!) before digging into EPs. I wanted to hear every song she ever put out. I would listen to her pretty consistently over the next few years, but sometime in 2021 I got really into *Have One on Me* again specifically. I listened to it 50-100 times over the course of two months or so. It was this obsessive period that let me really dig into it like I have no other album. Some of the deep cuts on this thing are better than any song anyone else has ever released. “You and Me, Bess” is my personal favorite of the deeper cuts and my candidate for most underrated Joanna Newsom song. “Ribbon Bows” also grew on me quite a lot. “Jackrabbits” is simple but so so crushing. “Go Long” is to my knowledge the only Newsom song to use more than one harp simultaneously, with 3 (!) cascading one over the other for 8 minutes. “’81” is short but carries such a fragile beauty. From a narrative perspective, “Does Not Suffice” might be the greatest album closer I’ve ever heard. Which brings me to another element of this album’s greatness: as a concept album, it is *flawless*. I can be pretty harsh on concepts, given that so many are half-assed or only cover 75% of the album. Not the case here. “Easy” establishes a doomed relationship and “Does Not Suffice” describes the final separation. In between, every last track tells you something new about what how and why it fails. Whether through direct and literal narrative beats as on “Good Intentions Paving Co.” and “Soft as Chalk”, or through insightful allegories as on “Have One on Me” or “You and Me, Bess”, *Have One on Me* is relentless and inventive in telling its story. And that story is just so goddamn human. Newsom elevates a tale as tried and true as a romantic breakup into a Greek tragedy, complete with questioning of God(s) near the end. If art is humanity's pursuit of the divine, *Have One on Me* feeds you nectar and ambrosia easy as falling asleep. And that right there is the course I keep.

Though I go back and forth, disc 1 has to be my favorite of the three. It has four tracks that are in the top half for the album. That’s just difficult to beat. “Easy” is such an unbelievable opener. I love the focus on JN’s voice in the beginning and the haunting strings. It’s a deeply unsettling song to be honest. She sounds obsessive, like some kind of malevolent spirit that will collect your soul the second you let your guard down. The lyrics get creepier as the song progresses, from “I’m your little life giver/I will give my life” to “C’mon you little life giver/Give your life”. I mentioned in my *Ys* review how random little lyrics and melodies will get stuck in my head at all times. That’s back on this album and starts right away. “But One can’t carry the weight/Or change the fate of Two” feels like a mission statement for the album. The rustic instrumentation and relatively conventional song structure also establish this album’s identity right away. It’s genuinely hard to think of how this album could have a better start. “Have One on Me” is disc 1’s *Ys*-ian track, a parallel to disc 2’s centerpiece “In California” and disc 3’s climax “Kingfisher”. Though I say *Ys*-ian, it’s still unlike any other song I’ve ever heard. It’s composed almost like a classical song with American folk instruments, but it doesn’t build like any classical music I’ve ever heard. I love the use of piano on “Easy” (and the rest of the album), but man does it feel good to hear some harp again. This track is full of lines that get stuck in my head: “Here’s Lola — ta da! — to do her famous spider dance for you”, “But there is nothing I adore, apart/From that whore’s black heart”, “Bottle of white, bottle of red”, “As if God himself spat like a cornered rat”, “And I will drink toy your excellent health/And your cruelty, will you have one on me?”. Newsom uses words to construct strings of sound that are as catchy as any melody, even absent meaning. Then, on top of that, she adds infinitely interesting meanings, and infinitely catchy melodies. I just don’t know how she does it. The story of Lola Montez is super interesting and just a perfect parallel to draw in the album this early. This is a masterful, masterful song. It sounds like if European classical music developed in 19th century America. “’81” is a nice, sweet track. I’ve historically been critical of short and simple songs coming after fiery epics on albums, but wow does this one actually perfectly establish some needed calm. As an individual song, I think it’s a tiny bit on the weaker side, but in terms of positioning it’s necessary. I really love the harp part, particularly the line in the right hand (high notes). “Good Intentions Paving Co.” is the closest JN has ever come to a pop song, and man does she kill it. This song just sticks in your head like gorilla glue. It’s the only one of her songs where I’ve ever memorized every last lyric. I can also play the left hand part on piano. How is it so catchy! How are the lyrics so good! “And it’s my heart, not me, who cannot drive/At which conclusion you arrived”, “Like I’m in a fistfight with the fog, baby”, “How I said to you, ‘Honey, just open your heart’/When I got trouble even opening a honey jar” are all such BARS. “And I’ve been fessin’ double fast/Addressing questions nobody asked/I’ll get this joy off of my chest at last/And I will love you ’til the noise has long since passed/And I did not mean to shout, just drive/Just get us out, dead or alive/The road’s too long to mention — Lord, it’s something to see/Laid down by the Good Intentions Paving Company” might be my favorite song verse of all time. “And am in love with the hook upon which everyone hangs” is such a beautiful description of life. “But it can make you feel over and old/(Lord, you know it’s a shame)/When I only want for you to pull over and hold me/’Til I can’t remember my own name” fucks me up every last time and is such a perfect ending. I know that’s just about every word in the song, but it’s just so full of gems. I also love how folksy the lyrics have gotten, full of “Lord” and “honey” and “darling”. The trombone is great and the non-lyrical outro, a rarity before this album, is pure sex. Each disc has one song that I’m not crazy about, and for disc 1 it’s “No Provenance”. It’s also my least favorite on the album. It has a lot in common with “’81” vibes-wise, but I think the big difference is that “No Provenance” drags on for over 6 minutes. It’s obviously very pretty but it feels like it doesn’t move the album forward as much as the other songs and “in your arms” is not that strong of a chorus. It’s a bit unfortunate that it’s sandwiched between two of the best songs of the album, but I think shortening it by about half would have been smart. “Baby Birch” is more of an experience than it is a song. It is heart-wrenching every single time I listen to it. I can’t hear it without feeling all of it, every emotion that explodes in the climax. It feels insane listening to how soft and slow the opening is knowing where it will end up. I’m a big fan of the false start build where the harp picks up but then the song returns to softness because it adds so much uncertainty and conflict. “Your eyes are green, your hair is gold/Your hair is black, your eyes are blue” is such a well-constructed line. It feels almost futile to write about the final build; it’s something that needs to be experienced. Those dissonant guitar chords are unlike anything she’s ever used before, it sounds like The Velvet Underground back there! Once you get to “There is a blacksmith…” all bets are off for the next 30 seconds or so. Holy FUCK. “Held her there, kicking and mewling/Upending, unspooling, unsung and blue” is so violent I don’t know how anyone could bear to write it. Then there’s another rare instrumental outro that just lets you stew. It’s one of the only moments of the album where it feels like JN has lost control. Just a ridiculous piece of music.

Disc 2 is my second favorite disc. It’s an unbroken line of great songs until the closer “Occident”. However, it’s more consistent and doesn’t reach it’s highest highs as often as disc 1. It feels like it should be impossible to follow up “Baby Birch”, but “On a Good Day” knocks it out of the park. “So ‘cross the years and miles, and through/On a good day, you can feel my love for you/Will you leave me be so that we can stay true/To the path that you have chosen?” is so perfectly written. It’s one of my favorite JN songs and it’s only a minute-and-a-half! “You and Me, Bess” is a sleeper favorite for me. I never hear people talk about it, and yet it’s o damn good! First off, I love how it uses the trumpet. The diversity of instruments is such a great part of this album. I find the story to be super engaging, particularly the way the narrator treats Bess like a human, which I find unsettling. The verse that starts “It is the day…” is one of my favorite climaxes on the album. The horns that play against “Who do you think you are, arching your hooves like a crane” are like orgasming. “No one told us about the laws of the land” and “You and me, Bess, we was as thick as thieves” both get stuck in my head, and “Kindness comes over me, what was your name?/It males no difference, I’m glad that you came/Forever listening to your glad neighing” kills me. “In California” is perhaps the greatest song in JN’s entire repertoire, up there with “Emily” from *Ys* and “Time, as a Symptom” from *Divers*. I will admit that I am biased, as using inland Northern California as an extended metaphor is always going to be a key to my heart. But she just does it so well!The chords in the intro are so uncertain and foreboding. “My heart became a drunken runt/On the day I sunk in this shunt to tap me clean/Of all the wonder and the sorrow I have seen/Since I left my home” is relatable as FUCK. “Where the darkness does fall so fast it feels like some kind of mistake” is beautiful imagery. “Well I have sown untidy furrows across my soul, but I am still a coward/Content to see my garden grow so sweet and full of someone else’s flowers” is the most piercing line I’ve ever heard an artist direct at herself. The song’s central melody is so pretty and so catchy that it carries not one but two tracks on this album. “Advance the tallow-colored walleyed deer/Quiet as gondoliers” is so fun to say. “As I wait all night for you in California/Watching the fox pick off my goldfish from their sorry, golden state” is a BANGER pun. “And I am no longer afraid of anything/Save the life that, here, awaits” is a terrifying line when you really think about it. “I don’t belong to anyone/My heart is heavy as an oil drum/And I don’t want to be alone/My heart is yellow as an ear of corn” is maybe the snappiest and folksiest way she could possibly present her dilemma. The outro has the same melody but with more of an edge in the surrounding instrumentation and you can hear that paralleled in the lyrics. “For it has half-ruined me to be hanging around/Here among the daphne blooming out of the big brown/I am native to it, but I’m overgrown/I have choked my roots on the earth as rich as roe/Here, down in California” is such a sad conclusion to come to. This is one of the best songs I’ve ever heard about homesickness I’ve ever heard, and the fact that I also grew up near there is a cherry on top. “Jackrabbits” is relatively lowkey for disc 2 but, honestly, it does exactly what it needs to. “I was tired of being drunk/My face cracked like a joke/So I swung through here like a brace of jackrabbits/With their necks all broke” is quite the opening line, as she immediately succumbs to her worries from “In California”. “Love you again” is a bit of a weak chorus but otherwise I do rather like this song. “And I scrabbled at your chest, like a mute/With my fists of ham” and “And it can change in shape or form/But never change in size/Well, the water, it ran deep, my darling/Where it don’t run wide” are both banger bars. “Go Long” is a song that I still love, but not quite so much as the first time I heard it. It was my original deep cut favorite when I first listened, but it ended up getting supplanted by “You and Me, Bess” and “Ribbon Bows”. “We both want the very same thing/We are praying I am the one to save you” cuts through the narrative like a hot knife. The three harp arrangement is positively gorgeous, cascading like Niagara Falls. The story of Bluebeard is such a good pull for this point in the album, and “Go long, go long, right over the edge of the earth” cleverly pokes holes through masculinity. “We danced in the lodge like two panting monkeys” does make me uncomfortable though, like many JN descriptions of sex. “Occident” unfortunately closes disc 2 on a weak note. It’s comparable to “No Provenance” Though she uses it well in small doses elsewhere on the album, I don’t fully understand the Occident/Orient to men/women metaphor she uses throughout. It constantly hints at building into something explosive but it just never does. However, it’s still nice to listen to and chorus is not too shabby.

Disc 3 is probably my least favorite thanks to a bit of a lull between “Soft as Chalk” and “Ribbon Bows”, but it’s also the disc that has most grown on me over time. I found it really benefitted from individual listens, as I would not be fatigued from the first hour and 20 minutes and could give the songs their due. “Soft as Chalk”, though, is an amazing, amazing, AMAZING song. It’s the point where the relationship finally disintegrates beyond any hope of repair and JN finally gets angry. And man, is she angry. Every word is dripping with bile. It slowly builds into one of the most spiteful and hellish pieces of music I’ve ever listened to. The chorus is goddamn catchy and the piano line is gorgeous. The drums hover somewhere between jazzy and militaristic. The wordplay in the lyrics is just insane. The point in the middle where these tribal drums pick up is a highlight of the whole album, it sounds like everything is tumbling at breakneck speeds. “I watch you sleep, repeating my prayers/Give love a little shove and it becomes terror” and “I sleep like a soldier, without rest/But there is no treason where there is only lawlessness/Lawlessness” are sharp as knives. “Esme” is the only song on the album that, after all this time, I still have not made up my mind about. Each disc has one track that’s an unambiguous least favorite for me, but this is the only one that’s “middling” (in a relative sense, of course). It’s very sweet, the harp part is simple but pretty, and it’s nice to hear JN hopeful after the last song. It’s jarring to have something so delicate after something so harrowing, giving it a wistful quality. The character Esme also plays foil to Baby Birch, a precious baby that was actually born, and as such is important narratively. However, it’s just too slow, soft, and static for me sometimes. I really don’t know. I think a big part of the issue is that it combines with the next track to create an extended lull near the end of the album. “Autumn” has similar issues to “No Provenance” and “Occident”: slow, never reaches climax, and way too long. It’s 8 minutes! It returns to the hometown idea of “In California” but doesn’t explore it in a way that I find as satisfying. I do like the melodrama inherent in the song, both from the lyrics and from the strings, but it just doesn’t go anywhere. I just do not care for the “climax”. It’s scattershot and almost deconstructed. To its credit, this song is rather Autumnal. It sounds like dead leaves falling off a tree. I used to place “Ribbon Bows” in a similar category to “Autumn” and “Esme”, but it has grown on me a lot. I really, really like it now. The story of the dog is one of my very favorites. It’s a completely believable story of a mental break, and who can blame JN at this point. She sounds fucking insane when she’s asking the dog about the existence of God. The instrumentation is among the most rustic on the album and it sounds great to me. It has a natural build that sounds great at each stage. “Black nose of the dog, as cold as a rifle” is great sensory work. And then at a certain point the strings just explode. “God, no God, God, no God/Sweet, appraising eye do the dog/Blink once if God, twice if no God” is as stellar a climax as any. I also appreciate how this is the first track to really deal with higher-level questions beyond the breakup. Her horrible experience has her questioning life and death. I love how she can turn the mundane into the divine so easily. “Kingfisher” is the perfect mirror to “Have One on Me” and the most *Ys*-ian track on the album. It brings back that quasi-Medieval sound! It also deals with higher level topics of life and death like the last track. The imagery is just terrifying: “when the sky had set to boiling”, “And for the sake of that pit of snakes”, plus the Pompeii stuff later. The instrumentation is pure controlled chaos, much like “Have One on Me”. “I can bear a lot but not that pall” is the moment that she’s finally fucking done after putting up with everything. I love the bait-and-switch of “He loved me just like a little child/A little child loves a little lamb”, as well as the assonance of “obstinate as obsidian”. The song’s bridge is a build-within-a-build that provides such immaculate release. “And with your knife, you evicted my life/From its little lighthouse on the seashore” is just so violent. “It is too short, the day we are born/We commence with our dying” is a personal favorite line for me, and “Kingfisher lie with the lion” ends the track with such aura. It has so many quotables, such a great build, sounds so alien and Medieval, it’s just a masterpiece of a song. It’s crazy that we’re in so deep and it’s still giving us all-time greats. Where “Kingfisher” is the album’s emotional climax, “Does Not Suffice” is its wistful denouement. She calls back all the way to the beginning with “And all the things that could remind you/Of how easy I was not”, a nice little bow tied around the album. Her vivid descriptions of fabric remind me of Edith Wharton’s way of describing the interior decoration of rooms. The “In California” melody is one of my favorites, so I’m happy hearing it here again. I love hearing JN finally stand up for herself with “It does not suffice for you to say I am a sweet girl/Or to say you hate to see me sad because of you/It does not suffice to merely lie beside each other/As those who love each other do”. And man, the parting lines: “I picture you rising up in the morning/Stretching out on your boundless bed/Beating a clear path to the shower/Scouring yourself red/The tab of hangers swaying in the closet/Unburdened hooks and empty drawers/And everywhere I tried to love you/Is yours again and only yours”. There’s so much regret and implication of what could have been. It’s also crushing to not only hear how much this hurts her, but how unbothered she thinks he is. She thinks he’s better off without her, something that must be so painful to admit.

I think I’ve said enough. I love, love, love, love, love this album. As I’ve said, I have some minor gripes with a couple songs, but I mean for a 2+ hour album this has remarkably little fat to cut. I think I’ll always like *Ys* just that much more because of how singular and bizarre a record it is, but *Have One on Me* is right next to it in my heart. An absolute masterpiece.

Favorite tracks: Easy, Have One on Me, ’81, Good Intentions Paving Co., Baby Birch, On a Good Day, You and Me, Bess, In California, Jackrabbits, Go Long, Soft as Chalk, Ribbon Bows, Kingfisher, Does Not Suffice

Least favorite track: No Provenance

# Aidan's review

Yet another Hargrove certified artist who has probably taken a shit in Sacramento or something. I knew Good Intentions Paving Company just off the name because Hargrove would play it whenever I too drunk to complain about the music on the kitchen TV. I remain convinced that Newsom's music doesn't do well in single songs, but as a whole album, her lyrical ability is fantastic, and she actually manages to do interesting things with an admittedly bloated runtime of over two hours. Every single song is loaded with clever wordplay, references, and it all connects to form a coherent and incredibly mature narrative considering she hadn't even turned 28 before finishing the album. While Ys was quite good, I felt that her voice was often more interesting than the other aspects of the album. That is absolutely not the case here. While the piano is more pedestrian than the harp, it adds a steady backing that works well with the exotic melodies Newsom can twist her voice into. I've spent the past week fawning over Jim O'Rourke's music so I would be remiss if I didn't mention his excellent mixing, although I lack the knowledge to really say anything of meaning. It's just a more polished effort, and every time I listened to it I found myself discovering new things. "Does Not Suffice" is my pick for the best song, forming a perfect closing to the entire album, but there's so many highlights here, and no songs are particularly weak. It's just a great album and an extremely impressive work of art despite how daunting it is to approach.
