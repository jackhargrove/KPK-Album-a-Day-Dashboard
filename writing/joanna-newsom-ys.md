# Write-up

I had never even seen a shooting star before. 24 years of rotations, passes through comets' paths, and travel, and to my memory I had never witnessed burning debris scratch across the night sky. Joanna Newsom was hunched over her instrument. She slowly strummed on a pedal harp, singing, eyes closed, into her microphone like she was trying to kiss around a big nose. Van Dyke Parks conducted patiently a large orchestra, waiting for his cue. White pearls of arena light swam over their faces. A lazy disco light spilled artificial constellations inside the aluminum cove of the makeshift stage. The metal skeleton of the stage ate one end of Florence's Piazza Santa Croce, on the steps of the Santa Croce Cathedral. Michelangelo's bones and cobblestone laid beneath. I stared entranced, soaking in Joanna Newsom’s new material, chiseling each sound into the best functioning parts of my brain which would be the only sound system for the material for months.

Joanna Newsom is a singer-songwriter and, if you’re not already aware, my favorite musical artist. Though she also plays the piano frequently in her music, she is most commonly associated with her primary instrument, the harp. She grew up in Nevada City, California, near Lake Tahoe, and is the second cousin twice removed of California Governor Gavin Newsom. She was raised by bohemian artistic parents in the woods and attended a Waldorf school. At age 5, she informed her parents that she wanted to play the harp; given that a typical harp is 6 feet tall, they decided to have her learn the piano instead. She remained insistent and so, after learning piano and growing more, she began to play the Celtic harp (kind of a miniature harp). She finally moved up to a full-size pedal harp as a teenager. She moved to Oakland to attend Mills College and recorded an album with the group Nervous Cop in 2003, an experimental rock project led by Zach Hill, the drummer who would go on to found Death Grips. She soon dropped out of college and moved back to Nevada City, where she made two EPs of songs featuring just her harp and her challenging voice. These EPs found their way to Will Oldham (the man who took the cover photograph for *Spiderland*), who got her signed to the record label Drag City.

She released her first album *The Milk-Eyed Mender*, a beautiful but raw collection of 12 tracks with relatively conventional structure, in 2004 to good reviews. The album was taken as an entry in the freak folk movement led by Devendra Banhart and including Animal Collective and Xiu Xiu, although it was clear that she was unique even within a scene of weirdos. *The Milk-Eyed Mender* would sell 200,000 copies and spawn the modestly successful single “The Sprout and the Bean”. Newsom took some time to tour after this album, where she began a romantic relationship with folk musician Bill Callahan, also known as Smog. He introduced her to composer Van Dyke Parks, with whom she would make the chamber folk album *Ys* in 2006. *Ys* was a critical and (relative) commercial success that established Newsom as a singular artist. Over the next few years she toured, starred in the music video for “Kids” by MGMT, and met comedian Andy Samberg, who she would marry. In 2010, she released the triple album *Have One on Me* which moved away from her earlier psychedelic folk sound to a more Joni Mitchell-inspired blues-y folk, and which appears on this list. In 2015, she released her fourth album *Divers*, a meditation on time that was her shortest work yet. In the 10 years since she hasn’t released any music (I’m going to kms), but she has had 2 kids with Andy Samberg. They split their time between LA and the West Village.

Joanna Newsom fans can never agree whether *Ys* or *Have One on Me* is her best album. While I love both, I very firmly belong to the *Ys* side of the argument. After the stark and stripped-back sound of her debut record, Newsom wanted to make a record with a grander and fuller sound. Bill Callahan introduced her to Van Dyke Parks, a classical composer and arranger who had been Brian Wilson’s creative partner during the writing of *Smile*. Parks conducted and recorded orchestral arrangements for every song but “Sawdust and Diamonds”. Steve Albini, legendary Chicago post-hardcore artist and producer, recorded and engineered Newsom’s harp and vocals. Jim O’Rourke, who makes random appearances across some albums on this list, was the mixing engineer. There are two guest  backing vocalists on the album: Newsom’s sister Emily on the opener “Emily”, and Newsom’s then-boyfriend Bill Callahan on “Only Skin”. The album gets its name from the mythical Celtic city of Ys, similar to Atlantis, that is traditionally place in the Brittany region of France. *Ys* only has 5 songs, but they are long, sprawling compositions that range from 7 to 17 minutes.

# Hargrove's review

This is a top 3 album of all time for me, and Joanna Newsom a top 1 artist. I still vividly remember the first time I ever heard this: for some reason I had a 3 month free trial on Apple Music, I saw someone in late 2017 online mention that Joanna Newsom had just put her albums on Apple Music, and I threw *Ys* on not really knowing what to expect. I was hooked immediately by “Emily” with a feeling that I had been transported to a Medieval world unlike anything else I had ever heard. Her voice was certainly off-putting at first, but I never hated it as some do, and very quickly it began to sound normal (but gorgeous) to me. It sounds like I’m a child in 1300s England and the village’s elderly woman has called us to her house to tell us stories. Except the elder storyteller has just dropped acid and is a huge fan of the band Yes! The orchestra is beautiful, and it tailors itself to every song perfectly; it builds with “Emily”, it’s playful with “Monkey & Bear”, its absence gives space in “Sawdust & Diamonds”, it’s relentlessly linear in “Only Skin”, and it’s loud and chaotic in “Cosmia”. It always sounds kind of far away, though, allowing for the proximity and warmth of Newsom’s voice and harp to always be the main focus. Newsom yaps her way through all 55 minutes with personal stories of relationships, loss, and womanhood, never holding on to a single vocal melody for more than a stanza. Her harp adds a sharp texture to the songs that contrasts with the blunt and continuous orchestra. The line “Landlocked in bodies that don’t keep/Dumbstruck with the sweetness of being/Until we don’t be” from “Emily” replaces “Can’t believe how strange it is to be anything at all” from “In the Aeroplane Over the Sea” as my favorite lyric so far. This is such a bizarre, singular, and unbelievable beautiful record that I’ve listened to over 200 times. It’s never lost any of its beauty for me, and I get random lines with her inflection stuck in my head all the time. “Downright sickening”, “hopeless endeavor”, “hydrocephalitic listlessness”, “the water from the water”, “your feast is to the East, which lies a little past the pasture”, “I wasn’t born of a whistle or milked from a thistle”, “And in a moment of almost unbearable vision”, “I’m the happiest woman among all women”, “Scrap of sassafras, eh, Sisyphus?”, “and miss and miss” all stuck in my head until the end of time. And I’m grateful for it. I have never loved anything this much.

Favorite tracks: Emily, Sawdust & Diamonds, Only Skin, Cosmia

Least favorite track: Monkey & Bear (if I absolutely, absolutely, gun to my head, had to choose)

Long review:

“Emily” was the first ever song I heard by Joanna Newsom, and I was hooked immediately. This song is utterly stunning in its beauty, its scope, its emotion. But where I think it really excels is in pacing and structure. Holy FUCK is this song built well. It begins with just voice and harp, two elements that will be constant for the next 55 minutes. Things begin with simple melodies, simple instrumentation, and a slow pace as Newsom describes the natural surroundings in what we later realize is a flashback or a dream. All of the musical elements slowly form as this memory comes together from the vivid recollections, but the song hits its first inflection point at the first mention of its subject: “And, Emily, I saw you last night by the river” Joanna chokes out as the orchestra swells. This section introduces my favorite vocal melody of all time, one that I cannot get out of my head ever, used for the first time as she says “Frowning at the angle where they were lost and slipped under forever/In a mud-cloud, mica-spangled, like the sky’d been breathing on a mirror”. As if the melody alone wasn’t firing on all cylinders, that imagery is transcendent. The artsy Joanna makes an earnest attempt to connect with her analytical sister, but even when she tries her best she fails (the meteoroid causes the light and the meteorite is the stone that’s devoid of the fire that propelled it to thee, you dolt). Alright, this song has gone on for a little while, and it’s had a powerful tension build and release, surely it’s almost - wait there’s 8 more minutes? The instrumental relinquishes all the tension it has built (or so you think) and starts over; Joanna is seemingly unwell, and Emily (or “you”) has done something to make it worse. The instrumental builds with this frantic harp line as Joanna gets deals with whatever betrayal. Everything slows again though as Joanna puts the betrayal aside and longs for Emily’s support, with the strings in the background quietly freak out, foreshadowing more build. We get a mini-release in the instrumental at “And say, say, say, in the lee of the bay” but it doesn’t last; this section of reckoning with her conflicted feelings goes up and down and up and down. Then suddenly the song returns to the sparse and slow state of its beginning, as Joanna describes nature once again. This time, however, there’s a sense of longing instead of nostalgia. Things then build once more, now for the last time, as Joanna finally finds some kind of understanding for Emily as she gazes at the star with their father. Right near the end, we get probably my favorite lyric of all-time: “Landlocked in bodies that don’t keep/Dumbstruck with the sweetness of being/Until we don’t be”. As we enter the repetition of the chorus, there’s an eruption, not just releasing the tension of the most recent build but of the tempest of emotions built in the last 11 minutes. Joanna repeats the stanza she wrote in her attempt to connect with Emily as the conflicting feelings finally coexist, and we get one of the only instrumental sections of the album for the 30 seconds of smoldering harp in the wake of that explosion. Joanna Newsom’s greatest song, and the greatest song we’ve listened to so far.

“Monkey & Bear” is the weakest track, but that’s really not saying much at all. It’s a complete fairy tale, complete with talking animals and villagers and everything, that feels totally authentic while also feeling totally new. In contrast to “Emily”, where the orchestra was grand and primarily helped with the build and release of tension, the orchestra in “Monkey & Bear” is playful, lilting up and down and in and out to establish this magical world. The focus on flutes in the foreground is instrumental in building this feel. The overdubbed vocals in the beginning also add to that nursery rhyme feel, and the melodies in the verse but especially in the chorus have a Mother Goose quality without sounding like any specific nursery rhyme. It’s really impressive, and I don’t quite know how she did it. While the harp is nothing special in the beginning of the song, it becomes really sharp and cold in the verse beginning “Deep in the night”. In terms of story, lovers Monkey (male) and Bear (female) escape from their farm to seek freedom. Monkey is in charge, but he keeps pushing Bear to travel farther and sell herself as entertainment for money (perhaps a parallel to selling something else for money) temporarily as they make their own way. As the song progresses, Monkey becomes more manipulative and abusive, and Bear takes to going deep into a cave at night just to have control over something, to have a place to herself. Monkey’s reaction to this? “But Monkey just laughed, and he muttered/‘When she comes back, Ursula will be bursting with pride/’Til I jump up/Saying ‘You’ve ben rolling in muck’/Saying ‘You smell of garbage and grime’’”. Just chilling cruelty that I find very affecting. While in the cave, however, Bear sheds her costume and finds peace as she gets deeper and deeper; there’s not really any resolution, but I take it as a victory that in that moment she feels so free. The constant bear puns are super clever, I don’t know how Newsom came up with so many. The music tracks the story perfectly, with the more magical parts of the opening soundtracked by whimsical orchestration, while the darker cave parts at the end have complete chaos in the orchestra and sharp textures from the harp.

“Sawdust & Diamonds” is a tough song to crack, with the music stripped down to just Newsom’s voice and harp and the lyrics positively impenetrable. Newsom does mention a wide, white staircase in the beginning, evoking imagery of heaven that the harp part emulates, playing angelic and beautiful as I would expect at the pearly gates. However, one must first die to enter heaven, and there are plenty of references to death, not least of which is the taxidermied dove stuffed with the titular sawdust and diamonds. The harp polyrhythms are insane here, really stretching my credulity with what can be done simultaneously by on person with two hands. Without the orchestra to create dynamic contrast, Newsom instead alters the style of harp playing and the register. The typical build in this song is alternation between quiet slow sections with only harp flourishes, standard verse sections awash with frantic plucking in the high register, and then the introduction of strings in the bass clef to give heft to the climactic sections. Newsom’s decision to use the bass tones sparingly in this song is genius, picking things up any time a low sound is heard. I think the specific way she plays the harp in the “long face” section is captivatingly beautiful, and in the final third she introduces a new pulse to the harp playing that greatly enhances the rhythm. Her vocal work is also extraordinary, reaching a peak twice in the song as she frantically spits out the lyrics. And as for those lyrics, well: I’ve read a lot of supposed analyses of these lyrics over the years, but I’ve never really bought any of them. I really don’t know what this song is about outside of the heaven motif. There are lines I really like though, particularly “I wasn’t born of a whistle or milked from a thistle at twilight/No; I was all horns and thorns, sprung out fully formed, knock-kneed and upright”. When I’m feeling contrarian, I say this is my favorite song on the album instead of “Emily”.

“Only Skin” is a sprawling epic that’s really difficult to look at from a bird’s-eye view like this. I struggle to contextualize the ebbs and flows of this track like I have with the last few. What I can say is that it is relentlessly linear, dropping you off at the end far away from where it picked you up. There are recurring melodies, but they’re unpredictable, and often bring entirely new lyrics and circumstances when they do recur. I’d like to pick out specific example of my favorite vocal melodies, but they’re all so beautiful, and there are just so goddamn many of them. If I had to pick out just one vocal melody, it would be the one on the section with “I have washed a thousand spiders down the drain.” The orchestra is back on this one, and while I do love “Sawdust & Diamonds” it’s a welcome addition. However, despite the orchestra, the harp still steals the show, tracking the changing vocals perfectly and adding much needed texture. I’m also partial to the little sections where the harp just plays a scale, usually signaling that a section has come to a head. The orchestra brings in some instruments we haven’t heard before, most notably an accordion that gives a stretchy and melancholic texture to the atmosphere. We also get a horn section in parts, and an electric guitar. Bill Callahan guests near the end of the song, and his voice is jarring (in a good way) after 45 minutes of Joanna. His deep, velvety voice combines with Newsom’s high-pitched and raw wail. Lyrically, this song is super hard to follow, but there are some moments that stand on their own very well. I’m partial to the dropping of the title in “Scrape your knee, it’s only skin/Makes the sound of violins” with impeccable vocal delivery. “Sky was a bread roll, soaking in a milk bowl” is beautiful imagery, and “Scrap of sassafras, eh, Sisyphus” is masterful use of word-sounds (like the texture more than the meaning). I’m haunted to this day by “Stay with me for a while/That’s an awfully real gun” which is visceral in its sudden violence. Similarly, hearing Joanna talk about her vagina as “to where I’m unfresh” is a real trip too. This song is relentless, but man, if it isn’t jaw-droppingly gorgeous. And ends on the ambiguous “Then all I can do/Is do, my darling, right by you”. One of the most ambitious and stunning songs I’ve ever heard.

“Cosmia” is a frantic, manic, ominous mess that’s a bold choice to end the album on. The harp melody in the beginning is more pointed than anything we’ve heard yet. I feel like it could cut me if I approach it from the wrong direction. The strings are similarly loud and jagged, reaching sharp peaks. Newsom stretches her voice on this song more than any other, starting with “And I miss your precious heart”. There’s also an electric guitar on parts of this song which gives a kind of warm fuzz I enjoy. There are polyrhythms on every song on this album, but never do they feel as disconcerting as they do here. In particular, there’s a section where the orchestra is performing a grand waltz in 3 while the harp is spitting out an almost atonal cycle in its tightest strings in 4. It’s genuinely discombobulating, and I really enjoy it. It’s immediately followed by a section with galloping from a wood block. This song is positively schizophrenic, constantly switching from section to section in a way that never allows you to get comfortable. All of this mirrors the emotional turmoil of the lyrics, where Newsom has to cope with the death of a close friend. I’m a big fan of “In the cornfield/When she called me/Moths surround me/Thought they’d drown me”. Newsom’s vocals only become truly abrasive near the end, as she repeats “and miss and miss” over and over again with a completely cracked voice. This is such a weird little short track that stands out in the track listing, and I’m glad it’s included. In the other songs, Newsom expresses her emotions slowly and subtly, letting them build and seethe. Here, she lets everything out, and I am here for it.

# Aidan's review

Unfortunately Hargrove is right and this is a great album. I haven’t heard anything else that sounds like it, Newsom’s voice is unbelievable and the orchestra is incredible, especially on the later tracks. Emily, the opener is the strongest song on the album, and Newsom’s voice is able to contort itself into melodies I didn’t know human voices could make. Monkey and Bear is probably the most accessible track, but it lacks the lyrical complexity that define the rest of the album. The harp on Sawdust on Diamonds is fantastic, and Only Skin is probably the most lyrically deep song on the album. The Bill Callahan duet is also super neat, although I’m biased as a fan of his other stuff. Cosmia has some of the coolest arrangements on the album and is probably the song that is most possible for another human being to sing. I was pissy about the lyrical simplicity of our previous album and this is just night and day, it’s festooned with gorgeous allusions and metaphors. One of those albums that you can listen to time after time and still find something new each time. Would not characterize this as accessible, but very glad that I took the time to listen to it
