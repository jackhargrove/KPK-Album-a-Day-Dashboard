# Write-up

The genesis of A Love Supreme came in 1957, when John Coltrane was playing saxophone with fellow jazz legend, Miles Davis in "The First Great Quintet". At the same time, he was also using heroin frequently, not an uncommon thing in the jazz scene. However, when it began to affect his playing, Davis, who had suffered from his own addiction 4 years prior, fired him from the band, spurring Coltrane to find religion and quit his habit. Coltrane writes in the album's linear notes, "In the year of 1957, I experienced, by the grace of God, a spiritual awakening, which was to lead me to a richer, fuller, more productive life." This album was his expression of this spiritual awakening, an expression so profound that he would be worshipped as a god and later a saint after his death at an African Orthodox Church in San Francisco.

The album was recorded in a single session in Englewood Cliffs, New Jersey, across the river from Manhattan. The album was heavily improvisational, and was intended to replicate the live performances of the recording quartet, even turning down the lights in the studio to replicate the feeling of a jazz club. The first track is the most famous, opening on a four note bass melody which Coltrane eventually repeats in each of the 12 possible keys before the chant of the album cover, the first of its kind in jazz. The album features Elvin Jones on drums, who was also on Speak No Evil, as well as Jimmy Garrison on double bass and McCoy Tyner on piano. The album would be recognized as a masterpiece in modal jazz as well as the founding work in the new genre of spiritual jazz.

# Hargrove's review

I’ve been sitting in my bed listening to this over and over and procrastinating this review because I don’t really know what to say about it. It’s a combination of me wanting to continue listening to it and me not really knowing what I can add to it with my writing. It’s really good. It’s not enough my style to become one of my favorite albums of all time or anything, but I really respect it and have thoroughly enjoyed listening to it. I imagine it’s similar to how Aidan felt after listening to *Illmatic*. It’s kind of like the platonic ideal of a jazz record: it’s tight, it’s short, it’s passionate, it’s emotional, the solos are good, the melodies are good, the rhythm is good, there’s a saxophone, and you just feel extremely cool while you listen to it. At the same time, there are many aspects that make it unlike any other jazz record I’ve heard in the bebop lineage. For one, it doesn’t follow the typical cyclical structure at all. Instead of head solo head, you get a completely linear album without so much as a motif outside of the first movement. This thing dries forward relentlessly, picking you up Boston and dropping you off in Hanover. Or perhaps picking you up in Lisbon and dropping you off in Beijing. At the same time, there is very much structure; there’s no free jazz here. Every part flows nicely into the next, with a clear overarching plan in mind. It’s for this reason I found this more challenging to get into at first compared to other jazz records, but ultimately more rewarding after numerous listens. With a more traditional structure (the Wayne Shorter album we listened to is a great example of extremely conventional bebop structure), even if you find the solos and improvsations difficult to parse, you can always latch on to the pretty melodies at the beginning and end of each song. Here, you’re in for a ride that you cannot exit for 33 minutes, absorbing it all at once. Thankfully, once you’ve got your sea legs, it’s actually really accessible. I think anyone could get into this, even with a baseline dislike of jazz. It’s a great work that I both enjoy and admire.

The album opens with a gong and some short chaos before the bass restarts everything on a smoother note. The rhythm section jumps out at me immediately as great, both in the background as structure and in the foreground as texture. Trane sounds cool and in control on his sax, easing you into his sound. All of a sudden almost 5 minutes in, we get a repeated motif on the sax (which will be the only one we get on the album). These 4 notes, played again and again and again, sound almost lyrical, like the sax is trying to tell us something. Out of nowhere, vocals kick in, singing the album’s title to the tune of that melody. It fits like a glove, and demonstrates how Trane can communicate just as well with his instrument as with his words. Overall, Pt. I isn’t my favorite movement, but it’s a great introduction to the album. Pt. II, on the other hand, is an absolute banger. After some bass noodling, the sax, drums, and piano all kick in at once like a shot of adrenaline straight to the heart. It’s probably my favorite moment on the album. The sax plays around with a really nice melodic idea, presenting variations on it before going into a solo. Later in this movement, the piano gets its time to shine. I’m a big fan of this one. Pt. III keeps the momentum going, starting with a drum solo and ending with a bass solo. I love it when the rhythm section gets its turn in the sun, so I’m a fan of how this movement is structured. The sax is much more frantic here, combining with the movement’s title (“Pursuance”) to indicate a less meditative and more active aspect of Trane’s relationship with the Almighty. Pt. IV has the most expressive sax playing of the bunch, sounding almost as wistful as it is reflective. Trane stretches his playing to an emotional peak as the drums create a halo around it through manipulation of the cymbals. The piano’s suddenness also helps instill that grandness. Fantastic finish.

This might sound like an S tier review, but I’m placing it in A. I really like and really respect this album, but I didn’t connect with it in the way I did with the S tier albums, and I don’t see myself returning to it as much. But when I do return to it, what an occasion that will be. I can’t wait.

Favorite tracks: A Love Supreme, Pt. II - Resolution, A Love Supreme, Pt. III - Pursuance, A Love Supreme, Pt. IV - Psalm

Least favorite track: A Love Supreme, Pt. I - Acknowledgement

# Aidan's review

The most accessible jazz album we’ve done so far, and my favorite. We’ve only done albums lead by the sax, so I’m curious to see how other will compare, but Hargrove is right in calling this a platonic ideal of jazz. The cymbals wash over you while the sax takes an undeniably vocal-esque role. Coltrane is amazing, there’s moments where the sax starts to strain that are as emotional as anything I’ve heard from a lone instrument. The first track was my favorite and probably the easiest to understand, with a simple 4 note melody that Coltrane takes everywhere. I didn’t love the (intentionally) harsh sax on Shape of Jazz to come, but Coltrane’s sax is as smooth as butter. Great album, wish we did it to start jazz off.
