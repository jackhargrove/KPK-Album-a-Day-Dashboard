# Write-up


# Hargrove's review

I’ve really been putting off doing a final listen and review for this album, which is unfortunate in a week with *69 Love Songs*. Part of it is that this album is quite long. As an archival piece that makes sense, you’re just putting out the rediscovered live performances as they come and grouped by general chronology. As an album I’m trying to enjoy, though, I think it suffers. But more importantly, I’ve been putting off this review because I didn’t know what I was going to say. There are so many cool things about this record and Fahey’s music writ large. That classic, pre-recording industry Americana sound is so cool and can create an entrancing atmosphere. Fahey’s deep academic interest in the scene is palpable. That his life roughly mirrored the blues greats he was obsessed with also moves me. And most of all, none of his music sounds bad in any way. It’s always pleasant to have on in the background. Despite all of this, I’ve struggled to connect with this album at all. It’s nice as it plays, but the second it ends or I try to think about how it made me feel, I draw blanks. I ask myself how it’s even possible to play his music while it’s on, but never have any guitar bits stuck in my head afterward. I don’t know exactly what causes this disconnect for me, but I think the two major culprits are lack of defined song structures and lack of vocals. I think what makes really old-timey music stick with me is the simplicity of songs (they didn’t have to do much to distinguish themselves from what came before, after all) and the often difficult circumstances of life obvious from the weary voice singing the AAB blues. The little interludes here do help with that; “Fahey Blows His Nose” is, unironically, one of my favorite tracks on this album, as well as one of my favorite bits of banter from any live album. But most of the music doesn’t actually make me feel how I want to feel. And yet, all of it is pretty decent. It’s a conundrum to review.

“When the Springtime Comes Again” starts slow, but it uses its ten minute run time to build really effectively. It introduces the style well, plays with dynamics in an impressive way, and truly evokes the feeling of Spring. It’s certainly one of the better tracks. “Joe Kirby Blues” has a nice downtempo, dark vibe to it but it’s very short. “Requiem for Mississippi John Hurt” is a bit bluegrass for my taste but it’s fine. “When the Catfish Is in Bloom” is good, very lively and upbeat. I like “Lion” quite a bit, I might even say it’s my favorite on the album. Part of that is the lead-in giving it more personality than any other track. The first 45 seconds or so introduce my favorite guitar line on the album, then things slow to a crawl and slowly build back into that line. This is what the songs on this album should sound like. Unfortunately, things take a turn for the dull. “Dance of the Inhabitants of the Palace of King Philip XIV of Spain” would be better if it were more Spanish or more danceable. “View East from the B&O Railroad Viaduct and the Riggs Road Intersection” and “On the Sunny Side of the Ocean” are also not that exciting. “The Great Santa Barbara Oil Slick” brings things back a bit. It’s quite complex and I love the chords. It’s dark in a uniquely Americana way. “In Christ There Is No East or West” is short and sweet, I’m a fan. The album finishes with three tracks from a different performance, and Fahey must have taken some benzos before this one. The three tracks are so slow and static. “The Death of the Clayton Peacock” has a bit of a twang to it that I like at least, but “The Revolt of the Dyke Brigade” has to be my least favorite track. “Magruder Park” eventually builds into something good in the final two minutes, but it’s tough to sit through the first seven after getting over an hour into the album.

So many things about this album are cool, but it never comes together in a way I’d like it to. “Cohesion” was a track that I really liked from *Double Nickels on the Dime*, and it’s clearly a send-up to Fahey. But it gets more emotion out of me in two minutes than this album does in 76. I don’t know. I’m glad I have this in my mental jukebox now for background music at the right event.

Favorite tracks: When the Springtime Comes Again, When the Catfish Is in Bloom, Fahey Blows His Nose, Lion, The Great Santa Barbara Oil Slick, In Christ There Is No East or West

Least favorite track: The Revolt of the Dyke Brigade
