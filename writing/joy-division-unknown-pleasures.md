# Write-up

“No future, no future for you/No future, no future for me” - God Save the Queen by the Sex Pistols

Punk's first wave in Britain lasted all of two years before the music of no future found itself wondering where to go. One band that answered the call was Joy Division. Acolytes of the first wave, Joy Division was founded by Bernard Summer, Terry Mason and Peter Hook after meeting a Sex Pistols concert. They brought their friend Ian Curtis on as vocalist, and the band began under the name "Warsaw", after the song "Warsawza" from Bowie's Low. Due to another band having a similar name, they quickly pivoted to Joy Division, which came from a Holocaust's survivors term for the sexual slavery camps of the Nazis. This flirtation with the horrors of Nazism was not the first or the last in punk, with swastika armbands being a popular fashion statement among the SEX crowd. The early music of Joy Division hewed closely to the Sex Pistols who had inspired them, and gathered enough buzz to sign with the newly formed Factory Records, which was the imprint of a local punk club. Unknown Pleasures was their first full record, and was produced by Martin Hannett. Although Hannett was only 31 at the time, he was far older than the 23 year olds in Joy Division and had his own ideas about what their sound should be like. In order to coax that sound out of them, he resorted to unorthodox techniques, turning the temperature down in the studio, forcing Mason on drums to record each single drum individually, and recording vocal on a telephone line,  turning what was a compact punchy punk sound into the echoey drones you hear on the album. Hannett also incorporated several outside sounds, including smashing bottles, someone eating chips, a guitar tape played backwards and a toilet flushing. Lyrically, Ian Curtis drew heavily from authors like William R Burroughs (Interzone borrows its title from one of his books), JG Ballard and Kafka. His live performances were also notorious for his dancing, in which he would seemingly imitate a seizure live on stage. Later on, he would be diagnosed with epilepsy. The musical inspirations of Joy Division included Iggy Pop, Bowie, Kraftwerk and the Velvet Underground. The famous cover of Unknown Pleasures was an image of radio waves to a pulsar and now can be seen gracing many a 50 something's T-shirt.

Unknown Pleasures was a moderate success on release, making 50,000 pounds in profit, and allowing the band to tour with the Buzzcocks. The band followed up the album in the next year with Closer. However, before the album was released, with his wife divorcing him and epilepsy worsening, Ian Curtis put on a Walter Herzog movie, listens to Iggy Pop and tied a rope around his neck. He was 23 years old. The band achieved its greatest success posthumously with the release of the already recorded "Love will Tear Us Apart", whose title graced Curtis's tombstone. In the wake of the tragedy, the rest of the band found itself at a crossroads, with its lead singer and lyricist gone in a suicide that had given new emphasis to their dreary lyrics. They decided to form a new project, New Order, with the remaining members, which would play a more dance heavy form of electronic rock. Joy Division is now recognized as being among the progenitors of both post-punk and gothic rock, with peers including U2 and The Cure drawing heavily from their style.

Picking up where we left off yesterday: Ian Curtis killed himself in May 1980, shortly before the release of Joy Division’s second (and now final) album, Closer. The remaining members of the band (guitarist and keyboardist Bernard Sumner, bassist Peter Hook, and drummer Stephen Morris) decided to continue making music together, but no longer wished to continue using the Joy Division name out of respect for Curtis. Instead, the reformed band chose the name New Order. Sumner assumed singing duties, but the group still felt like something was missing. In late 1980, they added Morris’ girlfriend, guitarist and keyboardist Gillian Gilbert, and their original lineup was set. New Order released their debut single, “Ceremony”/“In a Lonely Place” (both intended to be Joy Division songs), and their debut album, Movement, in 1981. These continued the dark and guitar-driven post-punk sound of Joy Division, but used synthesizers and electronic drums to a surprising degree. Later that year, New Order visited New York City, where they all got really into Italian disco and other forms of electronic and dance music. They released the single “Everything’s Gone Green”/“Temptation” in late 1981, beginning a major shift away from rock music to music that was electronic and designed for dancing (man I wish there was a name for that). In 1983, New Order released their second album Power, Corruption and Lies which is on this list. This album made heavy use of synthesizers, and helped define the signature synthpop sound of the 80s. Immediately after, they released the single “Blue Monday”, a major dance hit that would become the best-selling 12” single of all time. New Order would continue to release club banger after club banger (most notably “Bizzare Love Triangle”) for the rest of the 80s, helping teach the punk kids it was okay to like dance pop. New Order is still together today, albeit without bassist Peter Hook, and they’re doing a world tour this year.

Substance is a compilation released in 1987 that consists of all of New Order’s singles released up to that point. Legend has it that the owner of their record label, Tony Wilson, had just bought a car with a CD player and decided he wanted to have all of their hits on CD. It was released in 3 versions: a 12 track double LP with (almost) all of their A-sides, a 24 track double CD with all of their A sides followed by all of their B sides, and a 29 track double cassette with all of the above and more. For whatever reason, the songs “Temptation” and “Confusion” were re-recorded for the compilation instead of using the original single versions.

New Order has proved extremely influential on the development of dance music as a genre. They popularized a signature mechanical drum sound that they took from the German krautrock bands of the early 70s and particularly from German early synthpop group Kraftwerk. New Order was highly influential on the synthpop sound of 80s popular music. They also were a major influence on house music, which developed in Chicago in the mid-to-late 80s and was the start of EDM. Finally, New Order inspired many future underground and punk-descended groups to put down the guitars and pick up synthesizers.

New Order's second studio album (and the first one that anyone cares about), Power, Corruption & Lies was released before Substance, the singles compilation that we listened to earlier on this list. The release of Power, Corruption & Lies was the first album from New Order that showcases their transition to the electronic synth pop that they would become known for. This transition was probably helped along by the departure of their groundbreaking product Martin Hammett, whose work on Unknown Pleasures had helped catapult the band to stardom. Hammett walked out on the band permanently during a difficult spell recording their debut album after showing them how to use a mixing board themselves, peeved that the band was showing more interest in Italo disco than his preferred gloomy post punk sound. Age of Consent is the clearest example of this, a huge dance hit, but Blue Monday, their single biggest single was also recorded during the Power Corruption & Lies sessions, although it was not included in most versions of the album, including those on streaming services.

The cover of this album is regarded as among the best of all time. Designed by Peter Saville (no relation to the pedo), the roses are a painting by Henri Fantin-Latour, to which Saville added the color code in the top right. Album booklets contained a decoder which translated the colors to "NEW ORDER". The intent was to contrast the romantic and the modern, the beauty of the roses and the artificiality of the color code, a fitting symbol for the sentimentality which New Order packed into sleek synth pop.

# Hargrove's review

I’ve always felt this album is a little overrated, and my listening over the past few days hasn’t really shaken that view. While I think “New Dawn Fades” is a stunning song and an all-time great, and there are a few other tracks on here that I enjoy but not quite to that same level, most of the tracks don’t get more than a “this is decent” out of me. I think Ian Curtis was a phenomenal vocalist, and he’s consistently a highlight through nearly every song. He’s so distinctive with that deep monotone, and the production makes him sound so far away. I also respect its commitment to a monolithically dark atmosphere, although now that I’ve heard *Pornography* by The Cure I think that they did it better. Peter Hook’s bass lines are also typically interesting, a sign of his dance music future. Beyond that, though,  most of the songs just leave me wanting. A song almost never ends leaving me satisfied, I usually want something more dynamic, or with more moving parts. None of this is to say I hate this album by any means, in fact I respect it and think it’s good; it just never really stands out to me as a record I want to listen to again and again.

“New Dawn Fades” is a bonkers track, and if every song was like this it would be an all-time great record. That guitar part is fantastic, and it rumbles in so forcefully. Both the bass and the drums are simple but seething, bubbling under the surface of the booming guitar and the tension building in the vocals. The vocals completely explode in the second verse, giving the song a healthy climax in the middle. And then the song Fades into the distance, like a New Dawn (I guess). Really great track with great instrumental parts and great build. I also rather like the opener, “Disorder”. It’s one of the danciest tracks between the driving bass line, the angular guitar line, and that electric drum sound. It also has a stellar vocal melody. I could definitely hear this one as a New Order song. “Day of the Lords” didn’t stand out to me at first but it ended up growing on me. I really enjoy the ominous droning around the chorus, as well as the rapid release in the vocal melody. The last song that really stands out to me above the rest is “Shadowplay” if only because of the amazing guitar part between the vocals.

Other than the songs in the paragraph above, the rest kind of blend together for me. “Candidate” is dreary and forgettable outside of Curtis’ vocals. “Insight” doesn’t even have the notable vocal performance, although I did enjoy the breakdown at the end. “She’s Lost Control” is a tough one for me because people loooooooove this song, but I just really don’t get it. The lyrics are genuinely quite affecting, but I don’t think this song is well developed. The vocals are catchy I guess, but I don’t get the dance vibe that so many seem to. “Wilderness” has a kickass bass line but not much else. “Interzone” is just way too upbeat for this album, and the guitar lick sounds like they’re trying to sell out an arena. I respect “I Remember Nothing” for its bleakness, but it’s a pretty static track.

All in all, I want to like this record much more than I do. I got 4 tracks out of it that I think are really great, and I suppose that’s enough, but I never think it stands up as a whole. I admit there is some element of this album’s legendary stature working against it for me, but it just never sounds like I hope it will. Ian Curtis was truly gifted though, RIP.

Favorite tracks: Disorder, Day of the Lords, New Dawn Fades, Shadowplay

Least favorite track: Interzone

# Aidan's review

The magic of this album starts with the production, which at points is incredible. At points the lead guitar sounds spotless, and the little details really add a lot to the empty feeling of the album. Curtis’s voice is incredible and when the entire atmosphere comes together, it’s an incredible effect. That isn’t always the case, with the final 3 tracks standing out as falling back into the punk cliche, as the guitars trade reverb for feedback and lose the magic. She’s Lost Control is one of my favorite tracks, but its also probably the one where the production is the worst, with the echoing of Curtis’s voice in the first half feeling very out of place. The best tracks are the one that build to an emotional climax, Hargrove’s favorite, New Dawn Fades included. Lyrics are evocative, but they’re carried through by Curtis incredible voice more than being interesting on their own. It’s easy to see why this album was so important, but it’s not able to hold it together for the full running length. Still, the great parts are on par with some of the all time great albums and I thought well over half of the album fell into that category.
