# Write-up

Kate Bush is a half-English half-Irish musical artist born in 1958 and raised in London. She is one of the weirdest musicians to ever become extremely popular. Bush was musically precocious, teaching herself to play piano and writing her first songs at age 11. As a 16-year-old in 1974, she recorded a demo that found its way to Pink Floyd guitarist David Gilmour through a family friend. Impressed, Gilmour had legendary producer Geoff Emerick, who recorded *Abbey Road* only half a decade earlier, work with her to create another demo tape. She was put on a two-year retainer by EMI, who wanted to sign her but were worried that she was too young to handle releasing music professionally. Bush used the money and free time to study interpretive dance, an artform she would become widely known for. She released her debut album *The Kick Inside* in 1978. Despite her label’s protests, she chose “Wuthering Heights” as her debut single, a deeply bizarre art pop song inspired by the Emily Brontë novel of the same name. Both the album and the single were smash hits in the UK, with the former selling over a million copies and the latter going #1 on the UK single charts (making her the first woman to do so as a solo artist). She found some commercial success in the US, but much less. “Wuthering Heights” was accompanied by two very famous music videos showcasing Bush’s avant-garde dancing. Later in 1978, Bush released her second album, the label-mandated *Lionheart*. She embarked on her first tour in 1979, an extravagant and exhausting production; upon its conclusion, she decided to never tour again. Her third album, 1980’s *Never for Ever*, topped the UK album charts and experimented further. Bush’s experimentation peaked with her fourth album *The Dreaming* in 1982, which provoked mild backlash for its challenging nature at the time but is considered a classic by many today.

Hoping to balance her avant-garde inclinations with her pop sensibility, Bush began recording her fifth album at the end of 1983. She split the record into two sides; side 1 (“Hounds of Love”) contained all four singles, while side 2 (“The Ninth Wave”) was a suite of seven songs telling a loose story of a woman who has been shipwrecked and is drifting through water. Musically, the album continued Bush’s progressive take on art pop but merged it with the synthpop sound that had dominated the early 80s. Released in 1985, *Hounds of Love* quickly became Bush’s best-selling album of all time. The lead single, “Running Up that Hill (A Deal With God)”, became her first top 40 hit in the US. The song’s lyrics express a desire for God to swap men and women’s places so that each would understand the other better. To this day, it is considered her signature song, and *Hounds of Love* her masterpiece.

Bush released *The Sensual World* in 1989 (cited as a favorite by Charli XCX and the Cure’s Robert Smith) and *The Red Shoes* in 1993. She then took a twelve-year hiatus from music and the public eye to raise her child. She returned with *Aerial* in 2005, and then both *Director’s Cut* and *50 Words for Snow* in 2011. In 2014, she embarked on her first tour since 1979 to rave reviews. Though she hasn’t released any music since 2011, the fourth season of *Stranger Things* in 2022 included “Running Up That Hill”, triggering a genuinely unbelievable resurgence of her popularity. With the help of TikTok, a song that had been released nearly 40 years prior shot up to #3 on the US Billboard Hot 100. Now 67, Bush lives a relatively quiet life in Britain. A pioneer for weird women in pop music, just about every female artist with an unusual voice and experimental sensibility can expect to be compared to her, from Björk to Joanna Newsom to Fiona Apple to Grimes to FKA Twigs to Chappell Roan. But even heavily masculine artists like Metallica and Johnny Rotten of the Sex Pistols have proclaimed their admiration. Kate Bush is an artist whose career I truly have difficulty squaring in my mind.

# Hargrove's review

When I was about 17 and started to firm up what kinds of music I was most into, there was a common theme; Joanna Newsom, Fiona Apple, St. Vincent, Charli XCX, all theatrical female solo artists with distinctive voices and an inclination for the avant-garde. Every recommendation algorithm, every reddit thread, every adult I talked to was recommending the same artist, one from whom every musician I listened to allegedly descended from: Kate Bush. I eventually acquiesced and listened to *Hounds of Love*, what seemed to be the consensus favorite. I immediately fell in love with “Running Up That Hill (A Deal with God)”. I think it’s easily one of the 25 best songs ever recorded. I was so happy when it randomly got huge again like 5 years later. Just an unbelievably good song. Otherwise, though, I had a bit of trouble getting into the rest of the album. I still liked it, but I would only revisit it periodically, unlike my frequent listens to “Running Up That Hill”. I don’t know why I found it challenging exactly. She was just so wildly free. It felt like she had no antecedents whatsoever. Regardless, my love of the opener was enough for me to call myself a Kate Bush fan and get the album on vinyl for Christmas.

Coming back to the full album now, though, man was I blown away. It’s still true that there’s no song on it as good as “Running Up That Hill”, but every last track is, at minimum, good. Now that I’ve had 8 more years to mature and train my ear, everything that I previously found impenetrable now excites me. Despite the 35 people listed in the personnel credits, this is as singular a piece as any work of art can be. These songs could only have come out of the brain of Kate Bush and, on her fifth album as an extremely successful artist, no one on Earth could make her substantially alter them. And yet, she has such a natural pop sensibility. Every song is of reasonable length, digestible structure, and has a catchy melody. It’s a pop album with no compromises, something the nature of the music industry makes rare by design. Bush’s expressive vocal delivery is unbelievable; she gives her all on every track, conveying each and every emotion she feels as she feels it. Her commitment adds such a sincere, grandiose quality that I seek out in art. And if the songwriting and performance weren’t enough, *Hounds of Love* is a revelation in album sequencing. Exceedingly few pop albums can be entirely composed of single-worthy bangers, leaving two options for the more low-key tracks. One approach is to mix them in between the singles, which often results in listeners accusing them of being filler. The other is to place all the singles first, letting a typical listener tune out once they’re done and throwing the other tracks under the bus. While Bush’s approach here resembles the latter, she creates a strong incentive for the listener to remain after the singles through the perfectly executed *The Ninth Wave* concept. By keeping her focus on atmosphere and storytelling, she elevates tracks that might have otherwise gotten lost among “Running Up That Hill” and “Cloudbusting”. I had a ton of fun this week giving the second half of the album the attention I never had before. It’s much better than most full concept albums. Anyway, Kate Bush kicks ass, *Hounds of Love* kicks ass, “Running Up That Hill (A Deal With God)” kicks ass, so what are you waiting for?

I’ve already said a lot about “Running Up That Hill (A Deal with God)”, but I’d just like to reiterate that it is, in fact, very very good. It’s so catchy, the chords build tension to an insane degree, the tribal drums thump along swimmingly, and the lyrics are both fun and profound. It’s pretty much a perfect song. “Hounds of Love” somehow follows it up admirably, though. One thing I love about this album is the juxtaposition of stereotypically 80s sounds with music that doesn’t sound like any decade on Earth. In the title track, the drums provide the 80s sound, but they crash all over the place in a way I’ve never heard elsewhere. Bush’s vocal performance forms the heart of the track, particularly her singing on the pre-chorus. “The Big Sky” is my least favorite on side 1 but, seeing as how it’s one of the best sides of music ever, it’s still one of my favorite tracks. I appreciate the dreaminess of the lyrics, the driving tempo, and the bounciness of the synth. I’m not crazy about the vocals in the outro, but it’s fine. This track reminds me of “Alarm Call” by Björk for some reason. “Mother Stands for Comfort” comes at the perfect time for the pacing as we were due for a bit of a slowdown. There’s this clanging, industrial percussion and high-pitched synth that combine to create an unmatched eerie atmosphere that perfectly complements the grim lyrics of a mother still loving her murderer child. “Cloudbusting” ends the side on a very high note. The strings are the highlight of this song, particularly those that come in on “On top of the world, looking over the edge” in the second verse. I love how it all builds, especially when the militaristic drum enters with about a quarter of the song left. Its greatest asset is just how gorgeous it is.

*The Ninth Wave* pulls off the whole concept thing in a way that few dedicated concept albums have. I found it a genuinely creepy and off-putting experience in the best way. “And Dream of Sheep” avoids having to spell out the shift in the lyrics because its instrumental sounds so clearly detached from what we heard on the first side. The piano and bouzouki are nice new textures, and I have the delivery of “Wish I had my radio” stuck in my head. It’s a beautifully tender ballad. “Under Ice” turns the ominous dial up a notch, and things won’t let up until the final track. While I find the “it’s meeeeeee” near the end cathartic, overall the theatricality of Bush’s vocals worked to this song’s detriment. I’m not entirely sure how I feel about the spoken montage at the beginning of “Waking the Witch” but, once the music kicks in after a little over a minute, this track BANGS. It’s so, so cool. The demonic voice is actually scary, as are Bush’s glitched out vocals. And I just love the way the music opens up during the choruses. “Watching You Without Me” is alright, but unfortunately my least favorite track. Why is she mumble slurring through the verses? Is she Michael Stipe? The track’s best quality is its lyrics, which remind me of the 2017 film *A Ghost Story*. “Jig of Life” is the best track on side 2 and honestly gives most tracks on side 1 a run for their money. Bush’s galloping delivery of “‘Now is the place where the crossroads meet/Will you look into the future?/Never, never say goodbye’” blows my mind every time. The Irish instrumentation is bouncy, fun, and unlike anything else on our list. The part where the original melody kicks in again after the softer bridge section is pure sex. The lyrics are some of the best too, with a woman pleading with her younger self to stay alive. “Hello Earth” is a very cool but also flawed track. The chorus melody is fantastic, as are the more theatrical elements. I don’t know what to make of its structure, however. Punctuating segments with extended chanting is certainly unique, but it unfortunately drags on a little too long. “The Morning Fog” closes both *The Ninth Wave* and the overall album brilliantly. It’s such a nice reprieve after the terror I felt throughout the preceding 23 minutes. I adore the bounciness of the bass. This song is the perfect mixture of pretty, upbeat, and melancholy.

Kate Bush, you are an icon. I know it’s weird, but I think anyone who lets themselves leave their reservations at the door and float in her world can fall in love with this album. And, if nothing else, listen to “Running Up That Hill (A Deal with God)”. It’s indescribably good.

Favorite tracks: Running Up That Hill (A Deal with God), Hounds of Love, The Big Sky Mother Stands for Comfort, Cloudbusting, And Dream of Sheep, Waking the Witch, Jig of Life, The Morning Fog

Least favorite track: Watching You Without Me
