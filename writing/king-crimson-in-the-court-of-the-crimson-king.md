# Write-up

Story/Fun Facts: Although King Crimson’s lineup has shifted drastically over their 50 year career, this debut album was made with their original five man lineup: guitarist Robert Fripp, bassist and lead vocalist Greg Lake, drummer Michael Giles, woodwind multi-instrumentalist and primary composer Ian McDonald, and lyricist Peter Sinfield. The earliest iteration of the band formed in the mid 1960s when Peter Giles and his brother Michael Giles met Robert Fripp in their hometown of Dorset, England. They formed a short-lived jazz-rock group aptly named Giles, Giles, and Fripp. Hoping to grow the band, they recruited Ian McDonald and Peter Sinfield, and Fripp ended up forcing Peter Giles out of the band and replacing him with Greg Lake because Peter Giles was not on board with the complex direction the band’s sound was taking. And thus, King Crimson was born.

Musically, King Crimson rejected the blues sound and structure of American rock music and instead sought to emulate the sounds of European classical music. Aesthetically, King Crimson reveled in the dark and the medieval, with their name evoking the Devil. Interestingly, lyricist Peter Sinfield played no instrument nor was he a musician in any real sense; he was essentially a poet they kept on staff exclusively to write the lyrics, which allowed the complexity of the lyrics to match that of the music. *In the Court of the Crimson King* became one of the original and defining documents of the progressive rock music that would become dominant in the 1970s. The iconic cover, meant to represent the 21st Century Schizoid Man, was a self-portrait drawn by friend of the band Barry Godber. Almost immediately after the release of the album, Godber dropped dead of a heart attack at age 24. C’est la vie.

Although King Crimson released 13 studio albums between 1969 and 2003, and toured for even longer, this original lineup didn’t even make it to the second album. Guitarist Robert Fripp has remained the one constant throughout King Crimson’s history, and, through his friendship with Brian Eno, has been a collaborator with David Bowie and Talking Heads (he played guitar on *Fear of Music*, the album immediately before *Remain in Light*). Bassist Greg Lake went on to form the progressive rock supergroup Emerson, Lake, and Palmer. Composer and woodwind player Ian McDonald went on to form the immensely popular arena rock group Foreigner in the late 1970s. Lyricist Peter Sinfield went on to become an extremely successful pop song writer in the 1980s, writing hit songs for artists like Celine Dion and Cher. Drummer Michael Giles didn’t really do much of note after leaving King Crimson, but he and Robert Fripp are the only ones still alive, so.

# Hargrove's review

As I said during my review for *Wish You Were Here*, prog really isn’t my cup of tea, and it remains pretty true for this record. I do respect how King Crimson establishes and maintains their own little universe and sound that is fairly unique even within prog, and I enjoyed the lyrics even though I didn’t fully understand them. And the final track, “The Court of the Crimson King”, really impressed me and is probably my new favorite prog song, which I’ll expand on later. I also enjoyed the beginning and end of the opener, “21st Century Schizoid Man”, but overall this album really just isn’t for me.

One thing I enjoyed was the world they built in this album, both through the lyrics and the music. Tracks 2-5 do a great job of building this medieval vibe. The lyrics on “I Talk to the Wind” read like they could be a rhetorical poem written by a monk, and something about the melody and chords also feels ancient. “Epitaph” feels very foreboding, and I particularly liked “Will no one lay the laurel wreath/When silence drowns the screams?” The crescendo in the middle is also very well done. And then the first two minutes of “Moonchild” feels like its crafting an impressionist painting through words, very beautiful but not fully formed. “21st Century Schizoid Man” obviously has a more contemporary feel to it, but I did really enjoy the sections with the primary riff and melody, the vocals have an interesting inflection and the guitar has a nice metal feel to it. Additionally, the drumming throughout the album is phenomenal, and the variety of woodwind instruments is endearing, especially the flute which enhances the medieval feel.

But all of these tracks also have significant downsides. To get the obvious out of the way, “Moonchild” has 10 minutes of quiet improvisation. Nearly a quarter of the album is more or less dead air. It’s kind of interesting intellectually one time, but it destroys re-listenability for me without a skip (and I hate having to skip). I’m pretty nonplussed by the instrumental breakdown in the middle of “21st Century Schizoid Man”, it sounds kind of like a James Bond spy movie theme and it subverts the atmosphere of the rest of the album for me. “I Talk to the Wind” is a little too pastoral and simple compared to the rest of the album, and is probably the least interesting track. “Epitaph” typifies my issue with progressive rock, the instrumental is overwrought and the vocals are whiny, overly serious, and make my eyes roll.

The one track, however, that I will unabashedly and unreservedly celebrate is the closer, “The Court of the Crimson King”. This is the platonic ideal of what a prog rock song should be. It builds its own world, the different movements feel connected, the melody is transcendent, and the vibe of the music perfectly matches the world of the lyrics. The choral melody is just insane, I can’t get it out of my head. The Crimson King is built up as a terrifying, looming figure; the music just absolutely explodes every time we get to the chorus and his name is mentioned. The destructive power of the Fire Witch has been instilled into my heart. The flute solo is sick, and I love the stripped back echoing of the melody at the end. “The yellow jester does not play/But gently pulls the strings/And smiles as the puppets dance/In the court of the Crimson King” is just such an insidious closing message. Love, love, love this track, shows I can appreciate serious prog when it’s done right.

Not my favorite prog rock album, not my least favorite prog rock album. I would say that I like it more than I like Pink Floyd’s *The Dark Side of the Moon*, but less than I like Pink Floyd’s *Wish You Were Here*. I will absolutely be spinning that closer when I’m in the mood for high fantasy though.

Favorite tracks: 21st Century Schizoid Man, The Court of the Crimson King

Least favorite track: Moonchild

# Aidan's review

21st century schizoid man is great, I honestly think the James Bond breakdown is fun, although the ending breakdown is not. The rest of the album never really finds its footing in the same way. There are parts on Epitaph and the title track that are great, but the songs are just too long with not enough compelling parts. I Talk to The Wind and Moonchild both sound like Beatles knockoffs to me. The guitar riffs sometimes hit the spot, and I understand the influence of this album, I just don’t like it.
