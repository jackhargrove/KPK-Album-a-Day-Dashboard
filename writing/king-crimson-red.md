# Write-up

We left off with King Crimson in the wake of their debut album, which pioneered the genre of prog rock. The band had 5 members: Micheal Giles on drums, Greg Lack on bass and vocals, Ian MacDonald as a multi-instrumentalist, Peter Sinfield writing lyrics and Robert Fripp on guitar. MacDonald was the primary composer of the music itself, but Fripp was the spokesperson for the band and as they toured became more interested in a heavier, more intense sound, the exact opposite of the interests of Giles and MacDonald. Fripp offered to go his own way, but MacDonald thought that Fripp was more essential to the band than him, so he and Giles were the ones to leave, leaving King Crimson as a trio.

Lake had one foot out the door while recording their second album, In The Wake of Poseidon, and the label even tried to get Elton John to sing vocals, but in the end Lake took most of the vocal duties while Fripp added keyboards to his role. Micheal Giles and his brother Peter (who had also been a member early on) played some instrumental parts as well. After the release of the album, Lake left to form the supergroup Emerson, Lake and Palmer, leaving Fripp and Sinfield in full control of the band. Their third album, Lizard was completely written by the duo, and was a disaster. The new musicians they had brought on either hated their parts or the vocals, causing two of them to quit, and Fripp lost interest in the project altogether, leaving saxophonist Mel Collins in charge of the band. He brought in a new drummer, which in turn brought back Fripp's interest, and they managed to fill out a lineup. Fripp was still not fully bought into the band, especially because the rather strait laced Fripp did not agree with his bandmate's heavy drug use. This new iteration lasted for one album, Islands, and ended with Fripp refusing to play one part written for him, the band hating Sinfield's lyrics and Fripp kicking Sinfield out, along with the rest of the band. Everyone but Sinfield came back to tour due to contractual obligations, only to leave again.

The next iteration of King Crimson featured percussionist James Muir, drummer Bill Bruford, bassist and vocalist John Wetton, multi-instrumentalist David Cross and Richard Palmer-James writing lyrics by mail as an unofficial member. Muir drew attention for his willingness to use basically any percussion instrument and bizarre on stage behavior, in which he would wear weird clothing, move around randomly and occasionally even employ fake blood to himself. This lasted for one album until Muir decided that he was not cut out to be a musician and joined a Scottish Buddhist monastery. The label told the rest of the band and the public that a gong had fallen on his foot. The next album, Starless and Bible Black (for which Starless was originally written) saw the band continue to disintegrate, with Cross complaining of his acoustic violin being drowned out by the frantic playing of Bruford and Wetton. He was voted out of the band.

Down to three official members, the band started recording Red. Cross was told he was kicked out of the band the day before the recording sessions started. Fripp was withdrawn during the recording, playing by himself in the drummer's booth, while Bruford would bike home from the studio the moment his parts were done. The album was not named after King Crimson, but rather the loud volume during recording, which pushed all the needles into the "Red". The album was a critical success and loved by fans, but only sold modestly. Two weeks before the album was released, Fripp, entranced by a local mystic and believing the world would end in 1981, disbanded the band. They would stay split up for 6 years. Fripp would work with Brian Eno in that period, releasing two groundbreaking albums as Fripp+Eno and feature on Bowie's Heroes and Eno's Here Comes the Warm Jets. We will return to King Crimson one more time with their first album after reuniting, Discipline.

# Hargrove's review

Aidan’s algorithm’s birthday gift to me was…an album in one of my least favorite genres (1) by a band whose most famous album doesn’t resonate with me much at all (2) and with a song whose position atop the RYM all-time songs chart has bugged me for months (3). Thanks a ton, Aidan. Except when I actually started to give it a listen, the most annoying thing possible happened: I kinda liked it? And from my very first listen to. I want to go through the three complaints I had from before listening to find out what went wrong.

For (1): is this even true anymore? I don’t really know. Early on in this project, as we were listening to our third album *Wish You Were Here*, I announced myself a prog rock hater. I didn’t like the overwrought and self-aggrandizing complexity, the focus on technical skill, or the fantastical lyrical themes that made it difficult for me to find emotional catharsis. My complaints were similar to those early punk rockers at CBGB who wanted to turn the page on the prog rock era. But I was undermined from the start by the fact that I liked *Wish You Were Here* a decent bit, and I’ve returned to it frequently since. And sure, since then I’ve been lukewarm to negative on *In the Court of the Crimson King*, Pink Floyd’s *Meddle*, and that Magma album. I found myself surprised that I didn’t hate Jethro Tull, however, and I was really floored by that super weird Henry Cow album. The hardest to stomach for me, though, was Yes’ *Close to the Edge*. It is perhaps the most stereotypical prog album in existence, designed in a lab to annoy me…and I’ve listened to its 18-minute title track all the way through ten times since I finished my review. That’s 4/7 for liking prog albums (not counting *Red*). I don’t know! Maybe I was being unfair to prog. Maybe I had just heard bad prog. Still, complaint (2): one of the three prog albums I didn’t like was King Crimson’s debut (I like the closing track a lot, but the other four are not for me). What about *Red* fixed the problems I had with their first album? For one, I still have my issues with Wetton’s vocals, but he’s miles less annoying than Greg Lake. Similarly, Richard Palmer-James’ lyrics are far more human than Peter Sinfield’s medieval epics. I didn’t have any problems with Michael Giles’ drums on the first album, but Bill Bruford’s here are a total standout, really really good. The jazz elements that bugged me on their debut have been streamlined and toned down into something that feels much less out-of-place. The main thing, though, has to be Fripp’s guitar. I like his choices better here. He dominates this album with his hulking riffs and arpeggios, picking up where John Lennon left off (literally) on “I Want You (She’s So Heavy)”. Though both *Red* and *In the Court of the Crimson King* are total downers, the latter is pessimistic on a civilizational level, while the former feels much more personally distraught. The primacy and immediacy of Fripp’s guitar work here makes me feel claustrophobic but emotionally invested. Finally, for (3): I let my feelings of annoyance cloud my first couple listens of “Starless”, but Wetton’s bass work eventually wormed its way into my brain and made me get over it. It’s a very good song. It wouldn’t end up in my top 100, but I see its value now. But I’ll talk about that more later. On the whole, I was impressed with this album and thoroughly enjoyed listening to it. It’s not perfect, and it’s still limited in that it doesn’t conform to my preferred aesthetic, but it’s easily my second favorite prog album after Henry Cow.

In contrast to the 30 seconds of near-silence that open *In the Court of the Crimson King*, “Red” starts things off loud with an immediate Fripp riff. I *really* like that riff. Which is good, because this is the simplest song across both KC albums I’ve heard. Fripp alternates between the same two riffs over and over, but it’s good enough for to work, particularly the twang in the “bridge” sections. Broof is so nice with it on the drums, I love the way he hits what sounds like metal sheets or some other metallic non-drum percussion throughout the album. It reminds me of Swans’ *Filth* and just sounds so sick. The most progressive element of this track is the cello section, but that honestly rules too. My only complaint is that this track is about one minute too long. “Fallen Angels” is a frustrating track for me to assess because it has the best instrumental sections on the album, but I do not care for the vocal line at all. Wetton sounds like a prog parody on this song and this song only. Otherwise, this song FUCKS. Fripp’s guitar arpeggios terrify me as they hulk forward and instill a sense of impending doom. They pull off the jazz breakdown perfectly, especially the frantic cornet. Even when we get to the second vocal section, there’s at least a nice oboe to focus on. And then the last minute is orgasmic. “One More Red Nightmare” has the opposite problem: I really like the vocal line (the “One more red nightmare” line has been stuck in my head for days) but I am not crazy about the instrumental. Fripp’s main guitar riff sounds like a prog take on Deep Purple’s “Smoke on the Water” and it just doesn’t work. The different sections connect so choppily, killing any flow. The jazz sections are much looser and less focused than the previous track. It’s still pretty good overall, but it’s got a lot of issues. At least Broof still sounds fantastic on the drums. I also do like the lyrics quite a bit, as they vividly and strikingly describe a real nightmare I’ve had numerous times. Because the instrumentals are much more important to this album than the vocals, “Fallen Angel” is one of my favorite tracks and “One More Red Nightmare” is not.

“Providence” plays the same role here that “Moonchild” did on their debut, a free improvisation in the penultimate slot before the epic closer. This was a terrible choice on both albums. It’s at least better here because “Providence” is not as sparse as “Moonchild”; it sounds like a real song, albeit one played very poorly. I wish they gave these 8 minutes to a real song. “Starless” begins with an eerie, unsettling wall of mellotron that I absolutely love. The opening melody is also quite pretty. The vocals are more like “Fallen Angel” than “One More Red Nightmare” but I don’t mind them as much here because the cryptic lyrics fit the style better. That being said, I’m glad they get all three verses out of the way in the first third. The bass riff creeps in a little early, heralding what is definitely the better part of this track. That 13/8 riff is just so good and so powerful. It forms the required backbone “Starless” needs to build around. And build it does, spending the next four minutes adding textures and timbres while the bass drives things and Fripp’s guitar whines off to the side. It reminds me a little of “Helpless Child” and makes me wonder whether KC were particularly influential on post-rock. My biggest issue with “Starless” is structural and happens between the first bass riff section and the jazz explosion. The transitional section goes on for way too long and telegraphs that things are about to explode. A transitional section is needed, but I think this track would pack a much greater punch if it were like one-third as long. Still, the jazz explosion is super cool, and Wetton’s bass goes on an absolute tear. He’s my MVP for “Starless”. The bass riff returns at twice the speed and with more complexity, which is the element that initially won me over. In the final minute, the beautiful melody from the beginning recapitulates; more importantly, however, is the return of the wall of mellotron. It’s a good track and a good closer.

Fripp, you bastard, you’ve won me over. Now I don’t have to pretend it’s someone else playing guitar when I listen to Bowie’s “‘Heroes’”. I’m pretty excited for our third KC album, *Discipline*. From what I’ve read, it sounds like if *Remain in Light* were composed by Steve Reich.

Favorite tracks: Red, Fallen Angel, Starless

Least favorite track: Providence

# Aidan's review

I had 0 faith going into this album. Court of the Crimson King was among my least favorite of the first 18 albums, and this one was supposed to take their style to something approaching heavy metal, which has probably been my least favorite genre so far. Court of the Crimson King started off with a banger and then just got worse, so when listening to this, I keep waiting for the other shoe to drop and it just never did. I wouldn’t call it my favorite prog album, but it’s still pretty cool, the guitar interplay is quite nice, the prog noodling is limited to Providence (still the worst track but not that bad) and it saves the best for last with Starless. It’s not near the best song of all time but it’s damn good. I also found the lyrics on this album to fit really well with the overall style, nothing exceptional, and the delivery is perfectly suited. I wasn’t blown away listening to this album, but it’s definitely a good prog album and was enjoyable enough to listen for an album I dreaded.
