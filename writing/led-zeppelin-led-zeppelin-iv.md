# Write-up

Led Zeppelin was a British hard rock band formed in London in 1968 that consisted of guitarist Jimmy Page, vocalist Robert Plant, bassist and keyboardist John Paul Jones, and drummer John “Bonzo” Bonham. Led Zeppelin’s influence on rock in the 70s was comparable to Elvis Presley in the 50s or The Beatles in the 60s; they inherited a genre dominated by hippies that was growing more conscious of its artistic merit and turned it into a loud, arena-filling, hyper-masculine spectacle. Jimmy Page was a session guitarist who joined blues rock group The Yardbirds in 1966 after Jeff Beck replaced Eric Clapton as lead guitarist. The Yardbirds collapsed in 1968, and Page and Beck tried to form a supergroup with Keith Moon and John Entwistle of The Who. They declined, saying the group would sink like a “lead balloon”. The Yardbirds were still scheduled to perform some tour dates, so Page was tasked with putting together a group to fulfill those obligations. After his first choice declined, Page recruited Robert Plant as a vocalist. Plant suggested his former bandmate John Bonham as drummer. Session musician John Paul Jones applied to be bassist and was given the position based on previous work he had done with Page. The quartet assumed the name The New Yardbirds and, after finding they had chemistry, decided to record an album. The old Yardbirds issued a cease and desist on the name being used for the album, and the new one was based on “lead balloon”: Led Zeppelin.

Led Zeppelin released their self-titled debut album in early 1969. It established their loud, bluesy style and was a surprise top 10 hit in the US. Critics, however, absolutely despised it, declaring the intensity a gimmick, Plant’s voice annoying, and the style derivative. Nevertheless, their second album *Led Zeppelin II* was released later in 1969 to even better sales and even worse reviews. The single “Whole Lotta Love” reached top 5 on the US charts. In 1970, they released*Led Zeppelin III*, featuring all-time hit “Immigrant Song”, influence from English and Celtic folk music, and a side 2 that was fully acoustic. Despite the experimentation, critics other than esteemed Dartmouth alum Robert Christgau still hated it. This didn’t stop them from becoming the biggest band in the world. They earned a reputation for debauchery, originating the stereotype of rock stars trashing hotel rooms. Bonham’s drinking had also become legendary and certainly wouldn’t result in his early death. Led Zeppelin released their fourth album in 1971. They chose not to put their name on it or give it a title to prove that people would still listen without the hype; all that appeared to identify it were four symbols, one for each member of the band. This album, commonly called *Led Zeppelin IV*, sold 37 million copies, finally impressed some critics, and contains “Stairway to Heaven”, which has attained legendary status.

Led Zeppelin released its fifth album *Houses of the Holy* in 1973 to further success and with some early synthesizer experimentation. Their American tour later that year broke records for attendance. They released *Physical Graffiti* in 1975, their greatest critical triumph at the time of release. By 1976, they were starting to get oversaturated; their seventh album *Presence* was their first critical disappointment since their early days, and their concert film *The Song Remains the Same* was their first commercial failure. After Plant’s son suddenly died in 1977, they cancelled all tour dates and retreated from the public eye. Although they didn’t know it at the time, their 1978 album *In Through the Out Door* would be their last. In 1980, drummer John Bonham choked on his vomit and died after a day of heavy drinking. The remaining members broke up afterward, and they’ve only reunited for four live performances since. In their 12 years of existence, Led Zeppelin sold nearly 300 million records and became one of the most important artists of the 20th century.

# Hargrove's review

I have always considered myself a Led Zeppelin hater, but to be honest I’ve never like reaaaaallllllllly listened to them. I would’ve been able to name like 5 Zeppelin songs and recognize at least 10 more before listening to this album, but I had never listened to an album, or even given a serious listen to a song. They represent a certain classic rock, in your face, technically gifted, cock rock vibe that does not align with who I am, who I want people to think I am, or what I like. In the spirit of living up to my lofty ideals of wanting to like everything and giving everything a chance, though, I gave this album a serious shot! And…it was fine? I’m not sold on the vibe, I think there’s some filler in what is already a pretty short album, and I think “Stairway to Heaven”, while a good song and definitely not a skip, Dad, is seriously overrated. At the same time, “When the Levee Breaks” is a genuinely top tier song that I see myself listening to regularly for the rest of my life, “Going to California” is a really beautiful ballad and has been added to my “California” playlist, and I just had a lot of fun listening to this album, even when I was nonplussed. There’s a certain humanity, a certain jocularity, a certain realization of just how fucking young they were when they made this record that shines through the Rock God™️ image they project. The mistakes on “Black Dog” and “Misty Mountain Hop” are endearing. The Tolkein nerdiness on “The Battle of Evermore” and “Stairway to Heaven” is revealing. The yearning on “Going to California” is grounding. I was surprised, pleasantly, by these tender moments. The two best tracks come at the end of the record. The closer, “When the Levee Breaks”, has some of the best drumming I’ve ever heard. Bonzo really nailed it, especially with the cymbal crashes when the guitar phrasing changes. And the weird echo effect? Genius. One of the best uses of studio-as-instrument I’ve ever heard. The penultimate track, “Going to California”, is honestly really pretty. Great range from Plant, even when he goes back to yelling it fits. A tier below those two, you have “Black Dog”. As much as I want to hate this song for its over-reliance on that riff, I think it has other elements that complement it well. Plant’s vocals are good, the drumming is tight. It’s fine. Good even. Wouldn’t go out of my way to listen to it. “Rock and Roll” also falls in this tier. It’s pretty basic, but man is that vocal line fun. It’s a tiny bit infectious. Then there’s “Stairway to Heaven”. It’s a good song! I especially like the middle section, after the drums come in but before the guitar solo. Those recorders at the beginning are iconic! The lyrics are mostly gibberish. It never reaches a moment of transcendence for me that other people seem to get, and that makes me sad. I’ll have to keep trying, but as it is, consider me nonplussed. In the bottom tier, you have “The Battle of Evermore”, “Misty Mountain Hop”, and “Four Sticks”. Snoozers! “The Battle of Evermore” has some interesting Celtic folk flare that makes me like it the best of these three, but it has no drums! On an album with John “Bonzo” Bonham providing the drums! For free! What are we doing here! “Four Sticks” is mostly just forgettable, and the drumstick gimmick doesn’t add much. I actively dislike “Misty Mountain Hop” as a song; I cannot stand the vocal melody and rhythm, and it’s a terrible opener to side 2. Did this album change my opinion on Led Zeppelin? On the whole, no. Did it give me more to appreciate? For sure. Was it worth my time? Absolutely! Hard for me to rate it highly though considering only two songs were enjoyable enough to me to add to my liked songs playlist. Still, productive listen!

Favorite tracks: Going to California, When the Levee Breaks

Least favorite track: Misty Mountain Hop
