# Write-up

Lee Morgan was a jazz trumpeter born in 1938 in Philadelphia. He was a talented musician from a young age, taking lessons from and modeling his playing after Clifford Brown, a trumpeter who played with Art Blakey and Max Roach. At age 18, Morgan was playing with Dizzy Gillespie and recording for Blue Note records. At 20, he joined Art Blakey in the Jazz Messengers and played on Moanin'. It was Blakey that introduced Morgan to heroin, an addiction that would stick with him for the rest of his life. Morgan was instrumental in Wayne Shorter joining the Jazz Messengers, kick starting Shorter's career, but would himself be forced to leave after 3 years due to his worsening drug use. The Sidewinder was recorded when Morgan was 25. Unusually for jazz records, we have only listened to one album with an overlapping musician outside of Morgan, with his drummer, Billy Higgins, having played on The Shape of Jazz to Come by Ornette Coleman. The Sidewinder was a surprise success for Blue Note records, becoming their best selling album ever and saving the finances of the label. The album reached 25 on the charts and the title track was used in ads and TV shows. Morgan did not share in this success. It was the standard at the time for musicians to completely sign over the rights to the album for a flat fee. And while he should have been paid for its use in a Chrysler commercial, he was not and had to sue to get his fair share.

Blue Note's treatment of Morgan has been a long standing stain on their history. Due to his heroin addiction, he would often be in dire need of cash, and the label would pay him a flat fee for a session, then release full albums from his performances in a trickle, continuing long after his death. Partially because of this, Morgan was a prolific artist, with 31 albums as a bandleader, 27 with the Jazz Messengers and 55 with other groups. At age 33, he was playing in Slugs Saloon, a New York City jazz club that had been the main venue for Sun Ra and his Arkesetra a few years prior. He got into an altercation with his girlfriend, Helen Moore, over his philandering and drug use. Moore shot him and due to snow on the roads, he bled out on a long ride to the hospital.

# Hargrove's review

This is a nice but ultimately completely forgettable jazz album. I have now listened to it 14 (!) times over the last two weeks, and I can still barely keep anything from any of the tracks in my head. Fundamentally, the issue is that the lead instruments just aren’t all that great or memorable. Trumpet plus tenor sax is about as generic a jazz solo instrument pairing as you can get, and neither Morgan nor Henderson do anything special with the instruments to make anything stick. It’s a shame too because the rhythm section on this record is phenomenal. Higgins shifts seamlessly between half time and double time on the drums frequently, Cranshaw has some genuinely inventive bass lines, and Harris steals the show in solo sections on his piano. But the lead instruments just can’t pull their weight in the solos, nor can Morgan’s compositions stand out. It’s really just an in one ear, out the other album for me. And I gave it so many listens!

I’m so surprised that “The Sidewinder” was a massive crossover hit. It barely leaves any impression on me at all. I like the bounciness of the rhythm section, but it lacks the kind of melody it needs to be truly great. Then, once you get into the solos, nothing outshines the piano. The bass solo is also decent, but I feel like it just highlights the nothingness of everything else. Look, it’s a nice jazz track. I wouldn’t hate hearing it playing in the background. But it’s really nothing special. I like “Totem Pole” way more. It’s the only track with a head melody that stays with you once you enter the solo section. I really enjoy the alternation between slow sections and fast sections, something that extends past the head and into the solos. I also love the piano vamp in the solo section, something I never thought I would specifically point out about a jazz track. The solos are also better on this one than the opener. “Gary’s Notebook” is pretty decent too and also better than the title track. This one has a cool quarter note groove that sounds super interesting in swung music like this (without eighth notes, the quarter notes are just played straight, which gives it the jarring rhythmic feel). “Boy, What a Night” and “Hocus-Pocus” are both forgettable snoozers. I like the latter better than the former because of a standout piano solo.

This is the shortest review I’ve written in a while, but I really don’t have a ton to say. It’s not terrible, but it’s not special either. It’s something I’d want to hear playing at a club, not something I would put on of my own volition, and definitely not something I would put on an essentials list.

Favorite tracks: Totem Pole, Gary’s Notebook

Least favorite track: Boy, What a Night

# Aidan's review

A tremendously enjoyable jazz album, this feels like another 6 tracks of Moanin'. "Totem Pole" specially has a section towards the end that is nearly identical to the call and response melody of the titular Art Blakey track. I really liked Moanin', and I like this album. There's very few standout parts, but it flows along nicely, and was exceptionally pleasant to listen to while reading, on the bus, or basically wherever I listened to it. Probably the most "what I thought jazz sounded like" album we've done so far. It's listenable first and foremost, and I can see why it managed to save Blue Note, it's something I'd be happy to buy and play from time to time. The flip side of that is there's no real moments of brilliance, the melodies are far less emphatic than Moanin', and it never matches the great personality of its predecessor.
