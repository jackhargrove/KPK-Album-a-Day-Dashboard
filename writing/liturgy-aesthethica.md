# Write-up

Liturgy is an avant-garde black metal group which began in Brooklyn in 2005 as the solo project of Haela Hunt-Hendrix (HHH). HHH was born in 1985 and raised in NYC as a member of the oil billionaire Hunt family. Her uncle Lamar Hunt founded the Kansas City Chiefs, and her cousin Clark Hunt currently owns them. At age 20 and while going to Columbia University, HHH started Liturgy as a solo project. Her primary influences were the post-no wave totalism of Glenn Branca and Swans and the minimalism of La Monte Young. Liturgy became a band in 2008 with guitarist Bernard Gann, bassist Tyler Dusenbury, and drummer Greg Fox, while HHH sang lead vocals and played guitar. Liturgy released its debut album *Renihilation* in 2009 to decent reviews from indie-focused outlets like Pitchfork. Now with some buzz, HHH quickly made enemies with much of the black metal community thanks to her strong, unorthodox opinions.

Liturgy released its second album *Aesthethica* in 2011 to critical acclaim…again from non-metal publications. A manifesto written by HHH was published by a black metal magazine around the same time that outlined her theory of “Transcendental Black Metal” and how it differed from traditional black metal. This ignited backlash against Liturgy from the metal community, who saw HHH as a hipster encroaching on their culture. Since then, Liturgy has released 4 more albums, with the most recent in 2023. HHH also released a solo album under the name Kel Valhaal in 2016. These albums have pushed the boundaries of black metal even further, incorporating influences from EDM, hip hop, and classical. They continue to get good reviews while slowly gaining more acceptance from the metal community.

Black metal emerged from the increasingly extreme underground metal of the mid-80s. The “first wave” consisted of European thrash metal artists like Bathory and Celtic Frost who took the speed, rawness, and evil lyrics of metal to the extreme. This inspired the formation of a metal scene in Norway, in the late 80s and early 90s centered around the band Mayhem. The story of Norwegian black metal is unbelievably fascinating, but I already covered it in my Burzum write-up. Long story short, a series of church burnings, suicides, and murders in the Norwegian scene established black metal as the most extreme and exciting style in metal. Burzum, a former member of Mayhem, released *Filosofem* in 1996 while in prison for murder, creating the more ambient style of atmospheric black metal. His style spread throughout Europe and arrived in the US in 2000 when the SF-based fathers of American black metal Weakling released their only album *Dead as Dreams*. Their slow, atmospheric style inspired many, including the Cascadian black metal of Portland-based Agalloch and Olympia-based Wolves in the Throne Room, who combined black metal with post-metal and folk. They also helped birth the blackgaze of Alcest and Deafheaven, a style that combined black metal with shoegaze. All of this caused black metal to break through to non-metal indie music fans (“hipsters”). “True” black metal “purists” are insular and resistant to change, with serious backlash to these new bands starting ~2010. This is the environment into which HHH’s manifesto was published in 2011. She outlined her philosophy of “Transcendental Black Metal” aka “American Black Metal”, as opposed to the “Hyperborean Black Metal” that started with the Norwegian scene. Philosophically, she championed affirmation over nihilism, the pursuit of the penultimate over the ultimate, and evolution. Musically, she advocates the replacement of the blast beat (super fast kick drum technique) with the burst beat, which is more dynamic. She also outlines her view of “aesthethics”, a new type of art she made up that contrasts with comedy and tragedy. The word is a portmanteau of “aesthetics”, “ascetic”, and “ethical”. In the years since, the hipsters have largely won, and bands like Deafheaven remain popular.

# Hargrove's review

This was a pretty nice listen! It’s pretty similar to yesterday in that this was a firmly above average album from a genre I didn’t have a ton of background with before we started this project. There are multiple metal albums that have come out of nowhere and rock my world (Agalloch, Kyuss, Sleep), but kicking back and just enjoying something from the genre really makes me feel like I’m making progress and settling into the style. *Aesthethica* is a fun, energizing experience from beginning to end. It’s loud, it’s melodic, it’s rhythmically complex, it’s diverse, it’s got a formula that works and it rides it hard. The first thing that stands out to me is the drums. I don’t hate blast beats in the same way that Aidan does, but certainly after 6 (!) black metal albums I was ready for something different. Well, the drums on *Aesthethica* shake up the formula while preserving the oppressive, overbearing, all-consuming power of the blast beat. Fox drums so fluidly, I don’t know how he does it. The pulse on this album feels loose, human, alive. It reminds me of the drum sampling on a J Dilla album. And yet, it still blankets 80%+ of the album. He also does some really novel, crisp things with his cymbals that kind of take over the regimenting role from the bass drum. The guitar work on this album, generally speaking, is quite good. On the best tracks, the guitars flood the zone from top to bottom with these wistful, uncommon chord progressions. When it works, it works. Unfortunately, a few tracks overextend themselves on the math rock-y guitar riffs, but those are less common. The vocals are mixed so low and fuzzy as to be nearly inaudible, but honestly I thought that worked well too. It added a certain faceless anguish that I enjoyed. All of that being said, I do have a few issues with the album. While I do appreciate the sheer uniqueness of the extended interludes, from video game music to polyphonic chanting to whatever the hell that noise is on the first minute and a half of the album, they do get a little grating on repeated listens. Another issue is the lyrics, which, frankly, are just too impenetrable to leave much of an impression on me at all. Additionally, this album depends so heavily on the perfect synchronization of elements to pelt you with the maximum amount of sound, which means that when anything is even slightly off, the entire track falls apart (looking at you, “Sun Light”). And finally, while I think the super clean production does complement the mood created by the chord progressions in the guitar, as well as the fluidity of the drums, I can’t help but miss ultra lo-fi rawness of other black metal records. That was certainly something that gave the black metal genre just that much more bite and accentuated those frigid, Norwegian atmospheres. But overall, this is a good record, especially for people coming into metal from an indie music nerd background.

The album kicks off with a mediocre track, “High Gold”. The minute + of croaking sounds at the beginning were not a good omen. While it does feature many of the elements that I enjoy about the later tracks, it just doesn’t match them in songwriting or memorability. The minute of chanting at the start of “True Will” had me worried that things were about to get really annoying, but then the guitars start and just blow me away. This is a very good track. That guitars envelop me in sheer bliss with their repeating four bar chord pattern. I can’t quite articulate what emotion they conjure up, they just sounds good. I also like the pounding, dissonant section in the back half. In contrast to the standout melodic and harmonic elements of “True Will”, “Returner” primarily uses the guitars as choppy rhythmic jackhammers. Unfortunately, I don’t love it. The guitars sound nice during the bridge section, and I love the way the bass drum accelerates into the ending, but this song is too herky-jerky for its own good. “Generation” continues this style but actually puts it to good use, with the percussiveness of the guitars propelling the song forward nonstop. This track has a real pulsating quality to it between each cymbal smash. It starts to feel like it’s gone on too long around the 5:00 mark, but then the drums breathe new life into the track and everything comes together in the final minute for sheer ecstasy. “Tragic Laurel” is the best track since “True Will”, reprising a similar chord progression in the guitars. This time, however, the melancholy is accompanied by a small twinge of triumph. The atmosphere makes me feel like I’m standing in Heaven as God casts out the angel Lucifer for his mutiny, an unbelievably grand setting marred by unfathomable tragedy. But then, “Sun of Light” comes in, and it totally blows. There’s two minutes of washed out guitars to kick things off, then another five minutes of a guitar riff that just doesn’t work. It’s like the riff goes on one bar too long each time so the song can never settle into a groove. And to add insult to injury, there’s a full minute of silence at the end. This track sucks, easily the worst on the album.

I’m not crazy about “Helix Skull” in and of itself, but I do appreciate how out there it is. Its placement right after the album’s low point is certainly unfortunate though. Thankfully, “Glory Bronze” starts out hot to kick off close to a perfect second half of the album after the hit or miss first half. It starts off as a full aural assault from the drums and guitar before giving way to the guitar chords I like again. Now, the triumphant feeling starts to overcome the melancholy, and all the while the drums go batshit in the best way. This is the track that reminds me most of Deafheaven. “Veins of God” is a weird one but I’ve come to really like it. It stands out so clearly on the album as a slow hard rock song, very clearly not black metal in any way. It kind of sounds like Queens of the Stone Age, albeit with a slightly rougher guitar tone. It’s thick, heavy, and has me headbanging the whole way through. Super fun track. “Red Crown” starts with some strange sounds that I can’t quite describe which accompany the drums for the first minute (they do really love this “open every song with a minute of bullshit thing, huh). But, the guitar line that begins after and defines the rest of the song makes up for it. That short chord that the guitars play between the two long ones each time sounds so clean. I also love the jarring transitions between the sections, but the track does go on a little bit too long. I feel the same way about “Glass Earth” as I do “Helix Skull”. The album finishes strong with “Harmonia”, one of the heaviest and best tracks of them all. It doesn’t let up once in its 5 minute run time, bringing together all of the best parts of the album into an invigorating wall of noise.

Damn solid album. Not nearly as good as Deafheaven, but certainly has super similar vibes. Vis a vis the other black metal albums, I’m putting this below Agalloch (I like that album more and more as time goes on) and Weakling (does everything this album does well but with the rawness I crave), but above Burzum, Bathory, Paysage d’Hiver, and Ulver.

Favorite tracks: True Will, Generation, Tragic Laurel, Glory Bronze, Veins of God, Red Crown, Harmonia

Least favorite track: Sun Light
