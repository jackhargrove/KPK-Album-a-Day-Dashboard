# Write-up

Lou Reed is a rock musician best known for his time as the leader of 60s rock group The Velvet Underground. We already listened to their first two albums (and their third is on the list) so I’ll keep this part brief. Reed grew up in Long Island and moved to NYC in 1959 to attend NYU. He had a depressive episode during his first year and received electroshock therapy (likely for homosexuality) and finished college at Syracuse. He got super into jazz (particularly Ornette Coleman) and moved to NYC again in 1964 after graduation. He had begun songwriting in college and worked as a professional songwriter. He met classical violist John Cale, who at the time was playing for avant-garde minimalist classical composer La Monte Young. They formed The Velvet Underground, who were soon discovered by artist Andy Warhol and became his house band. They released their debut in 1967 to commercial disappointment, and then released the experimental *White Light/White Heat* in 1968. Frustrated with lack of success, Reed wanted the Velvets to make a commercial pop rock album. Cale refused and left the band. The Velvets, now fully controlled by Reed, would then release two pop rock albums in 1969 and 1970, with the former being on our list. Reed left the band in 1970 and it quickly fell apart.

After leaving the Velvets, Reed recorded his self-titled debut solo album. It flopped, and Reed didn’t really know what to do. In 1972, British musician David Bowie was on top of the world, his fifth album *Ziggy Stardust* topping international charts. Bowie was a massive fan of the Velvet Underground in the 60s, so he reached out to Reed to offer to help him get his solo career off the ground. Bowie produced Reed’s second album, *Transformer*, which was in the glam rock style that Bowie had popularized. The singles “Walk on the Wild Side”, “Perfect Day”, and “Satellite of Love” were all very successful, and Reed finally had his commercial breakthrough. Although his music career was in order, Reed had a famously rocky personal life. He had a massive heroin and meth problem, he was leading an “alternative” lifestyle (dating a trans woman), and he was still an obstinate artistic type. Reed released the album *Berlin* in 1973, which was panned by critics at the time but is now considered a classic. However, Reed’s 1974 album *Sally Can’t Dance* was his most commercially successful yet. Despite his personal troubles, Reed is on top of the world, and has one more record on his contract. What does he do next?

In 1975, Lou Reed released his fifth album, *Metal Machine Music*. It is a double album consisting of over an hour of modulated guitar feedback. It is pure, meaningless noise. Reed maintains that it was a legitimate artistic effort, and that if you listen closely you can hear references to famous works of classical music. Critics and audiences did not see it this way, and it broke records for the number of albums returned to stores. Even today, people don’t know what to make of it. Some think it was a way for Reed to get out of his record contract. Some think it was just a drug-addled “fuck you” to the audience. Some believe Reed that it was a serious artistic endeavor. Regardless, Reed’s image as an artist was shattered, and he wouldn’t find major success again until the 80s. But something weird happened: some artists found inspiration in the harsh noises and bold nature of *Metal Machine Music*, and they began to make real music using similar sounds and ideas. This resulted in noise music as a genre, as well as the noise rock that would be the precursor to alternative and indie rock. Whether this was Reed’s intention or not is hotly debated, but it’s increased the relevance of this album significantly. Reed would settle down personally in the 80s and become part of the public consciousness as another 60s and 70s rock icon. He died of natural causes in 2013 immediately before he was supposed to take a flight to record with Lana Del Rey.

# Hargrove's review

What is there to say. I learned of this album when I was a teenager and I always thought it was a really funny story. I never ever ever ever ever ever listened to it though. It was a novelty. One chapter in the fascinating life of Lou Reed. Not to listen to, only to begrudgingly respect. I always believed the story that he did it to get out of his record contract. My recollection is that 10 years ago Wikipedia and all of that largely advanced that as the most likely reason for this record. Reading about it again today, I barely saw that mentioned at all. Perhaps the consensus now is that Lou really thought this was good stuff. Who knows. What I do know is that I actually haven’t minded this as much as I thought I would, although it comes in cycles. In terms of actual sounds, the feedback here feels like baby stuff compared to the industrial and noise music of the last half century. No individual timbre here is as grating as anything in a Merzbow or Death Grips record. As a result, I feel like it’s lost a bit of its shock and awe value compared to 1975. Still, its length and its entirely unchanging, static nature make it a very punishing listen. I’m halfway through my fifth and final listen as I type this, and I go back and forth every 10 minutes between “this really isn’t that bad. it’s kind of pleasant white noise” and “I want to kill Lou Reed with my bare hands”. Before every listen I procrastinate and dread like I have for no other album, but once it’s on it’s honestly fine. It mostly does one sound on the whole, but that sound is kind of cool. It sounds very space age, but also horrible in a cosmic sense. A symphony of robotic screams. I mostly just think to myself how insane it would be to spend real money (and double albums cost more than single albums!) on this at the record store in 1975 and expect more of “Walk on the Wild Side”. I stand by my principle of respecting artists who show contempt for the audience, so I’ll give Lou his props there. But I really don’t think much thought was put into this album. I don’t buy it. This is nonsense. He’s fucking with us. But it’s fine. It’s not terrible to have on while going about my day. There are albums we’ve heard that I’d certainly choose to listen to this over. So we land at the most surprising outcome: meh.

# Aidan's review

“Whatever you now find weird, ugly, uncomfortable and nasty about a new medium will surely become its signature. CD distortion, the jitteriness of digital video, the crap sound of 8-bit - all of these will be cherished and emulated as soon as they can be avoided. It’s the sound of failure: so much modern art is the sound of things going out of control, of a medium pushing to its limits and breaking apart. The distorted guitar sound is the sound of something too loud for the medium supposed to carry it. The blues singer with the cracked voice is the sound of an emotional cry too powerful for the throat that releases it. The excitement of grainy film, of bleached-out black and white, is the excitement of witnessing events too momentous for the medium assigned to record them.” - Brian Eno

Anyone who gets to side four is dumber than I am." - Lou Reed

It's fun to write about Metal Machine Music. Even for the least perceptive of reviewers, it's a chance to channel your inner hater, to string together the most elaborate metaphor to call it unlistenable. Creem Magazine's review consisted of the word "NO" repeated, Lester Bangs, in a pretty positive review, suggested that Idi Amin make it the national theme song of Uganda and that Reed's follow up album be titled "Triumph of the Will". But to simply heap scorn upon the album is to miss the forest for the trees. I doubt Reed's claim that there's references to classical music buried in the hour of feedback, but there is something there. Out of the formless static, like the primordial soup from where life first emerged, a kind of beauty exists in this album. And it really does feel alive, more than many albums with real human voices on them. The guitars squeal under the stress, it morphs and shifts and steadily evolves, not in any set direction, but just enough to slip out from under you when you think you've got a handle on it. It reminds me of something out of William Burroughs's paranoid delusions about machinery morphing into flesh and vice versa. The only real form that the album allows itself to fall into is at the boundaries between tracks, where it cuts out for a second (more if you had the vinyl, which would require you to flip the record over as it fell into a locked groove). Right at the end of track 4, there's finally a glimpse of light at the end of the tunnel, as the most mechanical sound on the entire record, a pulsating long-short-short-short rhythm punctuates the chaos.

The stereotype of this record is that it's unlistenable static, and that's just not true. It’s static, and it’s not mild, but it's never awful unless you really blast it, and it fits in the background as well as anything. It's not white noise either, it lacks the inhumanity required to feel like a true backdrop, it juts in odd spots just enough to make it imperfect. I don't doubt that any idiot with a guitar could make this, but that's not really the point. I could scribble some vague nonsense on a canvas too, but Cy Twombly's scribbles are worth a few million and hanging in the MFA, while I'm #opentowork. It's an artifact above anything else, and its position between two of Reed's many soft rock albums really sets it apart, this was something that someone could chose to make, and if you chose a subset of Reed's comments about the album to believe, one that someone loved.

But is it good? I don't really know. I've listen to it more than most albums on the list, which is something, and I think I've grown to appreciate it. It's a mild headache, it's waiting for a delayed train, it's a hangover. After an hour listening to it, the flimsiest of pop sounds like the voice of God. Yet despite all of that, I keep listening to it. It's not something I'd ever recommend in good conscience, but it evokes emotion more effectively (albeit cheaply) than most other records on the list. It's an album I can find myself listening to on a more regular basis than some other albums I've expressed real admiration for, if only to "blow the gunk out" as Lester Bangs recommends.
