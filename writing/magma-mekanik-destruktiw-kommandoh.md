# Write-up

Magma - Mëkanïk Dëstruktïẁ Kömmandöh

Magma is a French progressive rock band that since its inception has largely served as a vehicle for the artistic pursuits of its drummer and founder, Christian Vander. Vander the adopted child of Maurice Vander, a French jazz pianist of some note and grew up idolizing John Coltrane. When Coltrane died in 1967, Vander felt that the world needed to fill the void left by Coltrane, and founded Magma in 1969. Mëkanïk Dëstruktïẁ Kömmandöh (lots of diacritics) was their third album and the first to feature a new lineup that his future wife, Stella Zelcer (later Stella Vander). The album took two years to create, and featured a shift away from jazz to a more rock-oriented sound. This change was imposed by the label, and the band's earlier attempts at recording this record were later released as a standalone album. Still, the band eventually embraced the new style, and Mëkanïk Dëstruktïẁ Kömmandöh is broadly representative of their work. All of Magma's 15 albums are about the same thing, the mythology of a fictional planet, Kobaïa whose language, Kobaïan all the lyrics are sung in. Per Vander, Kobaïan is a mix between Germanic languages, Slavic languages and "scat-yodeling". Magma has a devoted cult following, included musician Johnny Rotten, magician Penn Jillete (the talkative one), director Alejandro Jodorowsky and snooker player Steve Davis. Davis is responsible for Magam reuniting in 1996 after they broke up in 1984 when he used some of his snooker prize money to fund a series of reunion concerts, which gave Vander the motivation to reunite the band. Vander calls his style of music "Zeuhl" which means "Celestial" in Kobaïan, and has spawned several imitators who work within the same genre, in both France and Japan. Beyond Coltrane, the other major artist to whom Magma is indebted is composer Carl Orff, whose choral arrangements are very similar those employed by Vander. Vander is now 77 and Magma has released new music as recently as 2022. Mëkanïk Dëstruktïẁ Kömmandöh is widely considered their best album, and the band has rerecorded it several times.

# Hargrove's review

I am not crazy about this one. I was put off by my first listen, but I hoped it would grow on me like Henry Cow did. But with each subsequent listen, I just got more and more annoyed. Early on in this group I mentioned that I had a distaste for progressive rock because it was overwrought and self-important, but I’ve actually warmed up to a few prog records here. It brings me no joy to admit that *Wish You Were Here* is a fantastic album, and I’ve also consistently revisited *In Praise of Learning* Henry Cow and *Close to the Edge* by Yes. Hell, I even revisited Jethro Tull! This album, though, just ticks off every box for things that annoy me about prog. It’s certainly out there formally. The voices are weird, the “lyrics” are weird, the rhythms are weird, the instrumentation is weird. I do not deny that it’s a weird, even unique, sounding record. But I have this nagging feeling that I’m supposed to glean some spirit of unbridled creativity, some recognition of ambition, some joy at freedom from convention. I ultimately found a lot of that when listening to Henry Cow. But I found very little of it here. So much just rang hollow. I have difficulty explaining why. I had read one reviewer praise it as sounding like it came from an alien planet, but it just never felt all that alien to me. Maybe I just have too much experience with weird music, and maybe this album doesn’t have too much going for it underneath when you get past the weirdness. Funnily enough, it reminded me a lot of an album I loved as a kid, Mike Oldfield’s *Tubular Bells*. Even funnier, Oldfield was at the studio when this album was recored, and Vander later accused him of plagiarism. That may well be true. But I plan to listen to *Tubular Bells* 100 more times before I listen to Magma again.

“Hortz Fur Dëhn Štekëhn Ẁešt” gets things going on the wrong foot, and an issue with these continuous composition albums is that a bad direction can take a while to recover from. More or less all of side 1 is downstream of this track that I don’t like, so I’m calling it my least favorite. The highlight for me is the rogue, aimless guitar that’s low in the mix but you can hear it so clearly when you focus. This low-in-the-mix guitar is a consistent saving grace for me. Otherwise, this is a very groove-based album, and I cannot get into this groove at all. “Ïma Sürï Dondaï” continues with a premature and underwhelming explosion. “Kobaïa Iss Dëh Hündïn” ends side 1 as unremarkably as it began. Thankfully, and shockingly, “Da Zeuhl Ẁortz Mëkanïk” starts side 2 off really well. It’s by far my favorite track. I would even say I like it in an absolute sense, not just relative to the rest of the album. The up-and-down choral voices become positively hypnotic and I love the soloing from the highest voice. If the rest of the album sounded like this, I could get into it. “Nëbëhr Gudahtt” continues decently, although is nowhere near as good. “Mëkanïk Kömmandöh” is the album’s climactic explosion, but it loses me again here. I do not care for any of the individual motifs that get loudly repeated ad nauseum. And “Kreühn Köhrmahn Ïss Dëh Hündin” ends on a total whimper.

Outside of one track that I quite liked and one that was okay, this was a rough album for me. I’m putting it down by Shabazz Palaces because that was another album that I found similarly difficult to even put on throughout the day.

Favorite tracks: Da Zeuhl Ẁortz Mëkanïk, Nëbëhr Gudahtt

Least favorite track: Hortz Fur Dëhn Štekëhn Ẁešt

# Aidan's review

A space opera written in a made up language about a made up alien planet coming to save us sounds like your most annoying coworker's grandiose art project. Somehow Magma pulls it off in a glorious 43 minutes. It has all of the grandiose choruses of an opera, mixed with an incredible groove that runs through the entire album. There's a little bit of the stereotypical prog improv sections, but it manages to keep the groove going for most of it. It's a very singular album, the core idea of "rock opera in an alien language" doesn't really go anywhere besides a groovy prog track, so if you don't like that, there's no real redeeming qualities. But I found the grand swells incredibly cool and the Germanic sound of Kobaïan are a natural fit for the grand feeling that his album seeks to evoke. Vander's drumming carries this album, the groove is incredibly tight and allows the rest of the album to flow around it. It does stall out a bit on Nebëhr Gudahtt but the beat is just so strong that it carries momentum in some of the sillier parts. I listened to 3 different versions of this album, and the one linked in the chat is by far the best because the drums are put so far forward in the mix that it never loses the beat. This album reminds me a lot of E2-E4, a groove based krautrock improvisational album on an old version of this list that I really enjoyed. Both are extended meanderings around a core beat, and while E2-E4 goes for simple repeated melodies, this goes for huge sweeping choruses. I really liked both directions. Super unique album, if you like grooves or space operas, give it a try.
