# Write-up


# Hargrove's review

I *really* liked this album. This is now a serious competitor for my favorite jazz album. It’s ambitious in concept, effective in execution, and full of deeply personal quirks and ticks. There are moments and melodies of such great beauty, and moments of such harrowing darkness, and moments that combine both of these feelings. It’s really a stunning record. I think it’s really fortuitous that we’re listening to this a week after Max Roach’s *We Insist!*, a record that I really didn’t like. It was an interesting concept, an exploration black history on the doorstep of civil rights, but I thought it was just not done well at all. This record is so similar in many ways (it even has a cover of a song by Oscar Brown, who wrote the lyrics on *We Insist!*), but it more or less solves every problem I had. The songs are much stronger, the singing is prettier, there’s still screaming but it’s done in a much more fitting and palatable way, and it flows better. *Coin Coin Chapter One: Gens de couleur libres* tells its story (a slave girl finds her freedom, gets wealthy, and uses her money to free as many slaves as possible) as a collage of direct narrative tracks (“Pov Piti”, “Kersaia”, “I Am”), theme and setting-related side pieces (“Libation for Mr. Brown: Bid Em In…”, “Lulla / Bye”), instrumental jazz meditations (“Rise”, “Song for Eulalie”) and the touching personal closer “How Much Would You Cost?” that grounds everything back into modern reality. It’s a daunting task to maintain cohesion through such a diverse patchwork of styles, but the pacing and delivery is near perfect. I always feel like I know what’s going on at any given moment and can follow things easily but without getting bored. It really reminds me of Joanna Newsom’s *Have One on Me*, which came out just one year prior, where she tells the story of a breakup with long-winded historical asides that relate to what’s happening in the relationship. It’s a tough framework to get right, but it’s so rewarding when does well, as it was here (and on *Have One on Me*).

Unfortunately, my least favorite track is also the opener. I don’t think “Rise” is terrible for a free jazz song, but it’s not a good choice to begin this album. Sure, it’s a stark and chaotic way to begin a stark and chaotic record, but I really don’t think it reflects the intricacies and the beauty of the sound that is to come. The opening sax solo is not enjoyable to me and the cacophony of the midsection doesn’t come together in my opinion. Still, the chanting of “Jones Jones Jones Jones” is endearing, and I think the atmosphere finds itself right at the very end. The other instrumental jazz meditation, “Song for Eulalie”, is my second-least favorite, although much better than “Rise”. There’s a cool spy vibe in the opening section, and I do like the stop/start and irregular time, but it unravels as the time goes on and gets overly noodley. Still a good track though.

With the bad out of the way, just about everything else in this record is enchanting. “Pov Piti” is the first of the core narrative tracks that all share a related drum rhythm and vocal melody. I think the intro of this track is a much better introduction to the album than “Rise” as the cacophony is much tighter and their weird vocalizations add such a unique touch. The drum rhythm here is pure, unadulterated sex. It’s uncannily similar to a rhythm I’ve tapped with my fingers for years that I thought I came up with. The sax melody is gorgeous. And once the vocals kick in, I’m enraptured. The frantic and inconsistent cadence is so attention-grabbing and Roberts’ voice is unbelievably expressive. The lyrics are so evocative and, again similarly to Joanna Newsom, there are little bits of lyric deliveries that get stuck in my head. On this track, it’s “yellow feeeeeeever”. “Kersaia” is the second core narrative track, and my feelings are largely the same as they are for “Pov Piti” except it’s even better. The lyrics on this one are just brutal. “I have seen so much, but yet have experienced less than most dogs” hits crazy hard, as does “There will never be any pictures of me”. “Hustling to survive so that others may strive to be something more than me” is more or less the mission statement for this entire album. I love when the instrumental comes back in LOUD after the vocals, and the whole track suddenly shifts into an old-timey rag. It’s a scene-setter for roughly the era of America the story is in, it’s fun, it’s raucous, until…the next track reveals the scene being set is a slave auction. But more on that later. The kind of style shifting even within the individual tracks has helped me get a better grasp on the appeal of free jazz as a genre. “I Am” is the final core narrative track, the one with by far the most information, the climax, and also the best of these. There’s screaming on this one, as there was on “Pov Piti”, and I think both have to do with giving birth; the former being Coin Coin’s birth, and the latter being Coin Coin giving birth to her children. Again, I could say more or less the same things for this track as I did the previous two, but it’s once again even better. I really love the theme it solidifies of striving to ensure others become more than you are. I think it’s an aspirational and genuinely important message, as well as the correct worldview.

“Libation for Mr. Brown: Bid Em In…” is, in my opinion, the most impressive song on this album, and one of the most impressive songs we’ve listened to thus far. It is *harrowing* to listen to. It gives me a genuine physical reaction. And yes, I know, Roberts didn’t write (most of) the words. But I listened to the original Oscar Brown version, and Roberts turns it into something else entirely. The melody they add to the chorus is so sweet and tender, a contrast to the lyrics that feels sickening in context. This song conveys how dehumanizing and unthinkable slavery was in such a visceral, uncomfortable manner. “Don’t you mind them tears, that’s one of her tricks” kills me every time. And then when the instrumental finally comes in, I love how Roberts calls in the bass and the slow breakdown into chaos. What. A. Track. “Lulla / Bye” is comparatively low-key to most of the other tracks, but it’s haunting in a way the others aren’t. It combines a certain prettiness with a frightening darkness that’s really unsettling. I can make out some of the French words, but the fact that I mostly don’t know what’s going on adds to that feeling. Really beautiful. “How Much Would You Cost?” is a wonderful, elegant, pretty, and down-to-earth closer that reminds us we’re in the present. Roberts sings a song to their mother of how wonderful life can be despite all of the shit. The chorus has such a hopeful melody, and there’s a combination of talking and singing in the verse delivery that reminds me of Broadway show tunes.

Man this album is good. When I make not one but two comparisons to Joanna Newsom, that’s high praise. This is the most pumped I’ve felt about an album I hadn’t already heard since we did *Pornography* by The Cure. I listened to *Coin Coin Chapter Four: Memphis* a couple times, and it’s similarly good. I’m still processing, but I’m really excited to dig into the rest of the Coin Coiniverse.

Favorite tracks: Pov Piti, Kersaia, Libation for Mr. Brown: Bid Em In…, Lulla / Bye, I Am, How Much Would You Cost?

Least favorite track: Rise
