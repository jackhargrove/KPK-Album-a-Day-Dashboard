# Write-up

Merzbow is the primary musical project of Masami Akita, a harsh noise artist born in Tokyo in 1956. The project takes its name from *Merzbau*, an art piece composed of junk items made by German Dada artist Kurt Schwitters. Although Akita’s early influences were primarily prog rock and free jazz artists, Lou Reed’s *Metal Machine Music* in 1975 set him on the path he would follow the rest of his career. The Merzbow project began in 1979 as a duo consisting of Akita and Kiyoshi Mizutani, a friend he had met in high school. Akita had become disenchanted with the very concept of “music” and instead began making cassette tapes entirely composed of white noise, one of which was titled *Metal Acoustic Music*. He pioneered techniques such as recording quiet sounds up close and then overloading the volume in production, as well as using household objects to make new noises. He began to accrue notoriety in the mid-80s, releasing an album internationally for he first time in 1986 and performing outside Japan for the first time in 1988. Mizutani left Merzbow in 1989. That same year, Akita went on his first tour of Europe. He was unable to take his full setup with him, resulting in stripped back material he used to progress from plain “noise music” to new “harsh noise music”. His production became atively unpleasant, creating sounds difficult for the human ear to withstand. Merzbow has become synonymous with this harsh noise style. As he evolved this style, he specifically took influence from the death metal and grindcore scenes that were blossoming in the early 90s.

Merzbow’s most well-known harsh noise record is his 132nd studio album, *Pulse Demon*, released in 1996. The album takes its name from 1970s band Demon Fuzz and the fact that the album’s sound was made via pulse generation from a fuzz box. The album was completely generated with analogue technology as Akita did not begin using computers until 1999. *Pulse Demon* was the second Merzbow album to be released in the US, and it happened to be the one that earned attention for harsh noise as a genre. Pitchfork specifically helped popularize it, giving its 2003 re-release an 8.7/10 and comparing it to John Cage’s *4’33”*. However, other music reviewers were not fans. Merzbow cited King Crimson as an influence on the record, though I am not entirely certain in what sense.

Merzbow continues to this day as a solo vehicle for Akita. He has released over 500 studio albums under this name, including a collaboration with Sonic Youth’s Thurston Moore in 2015 and another with Fushitsusha’s Keiji Haino in 2016. He has also made seven collaborative albums with Japanese drone metal band Boris since 2002, and formed the duo Maldoror with Mike Patton of Mr. Bungle and Faith No More. Harsh noise has become its own genre with numerous proponents, including an entire Kansai No Wave scene in Osaka, Japan, and a more static style known as harsh noise wall developed in the US. Noise music has become so influential in Japan that journalists coined the term “Japanoise” to describe the scene that ranged from noise pop to harsh noise. However, Merzbow still sits atop the genre.

# Hargrove's review

This reminds me a lot of the lullabies my mother used to sing to me as a child. In all seriousness, I don’t know how the fuck to describe this at all. This was my first time actually listening to Merzbow, and he more than lives up to the reputation around him. I set my volume to half before I hit play for the first time, and I still was startled out of my seat by the immediate barrage. It took a few minutes for my heart rate to go down. The album continues this way for well over 70 minutes. I was a good boy and gave it a full five listens, which, Jesus fucking Christ man. For listens two, three, and four, I was sure to keep the volume low before I started, and I didn’t have that visceral reaction again. When starting my fifth listen an hour ago, however, I forgot that I had my volume on nearly full blast. It was just like the first time all over again, putting me into a fight or flight situation. After a few minutes, I realized I was enjoying the album way more than I had on the prior three listens. Once the adrenaline starts to fade and you’re left with just endorphins, there’s a kind of thrill, an exhilaration you naturally feel as your body calms down. I think that experience is necessary to enjoy this album, and to achieve it, you must ensure you shock your system at the start. Unfortunately, that natural high wears off well before this behemoth album ends. This would’ve been a pretty cool EP; instead it takes up an entire CD. But at the very least, this list still finds a way to shock me, 235 albums in.

I have to give props to “Woodpecker No. 1”. Maybe it’s just because it was my first introduction to the sound, but it generates the unique effect better than any other track. It was just a masterstroke to ensure that the most horrendous sound on this album, and one of the most horrendous I’ve ever heard, occurs at the first millisecond of the first track. It deserves plaudits for that alone. “Woodpecker No. 2” feels insignificant in comparison, although trying to distinguish between the tracks on this album is a fool’s errand. “Spiral Blast” focuses more on higher pitches, and I actually kind of enjoy it. There’s a more active drone that writhes around like an eel in an oil slick, and there are some dynamics in how the track escalates at the end. “My Station Rock” is fine but doesn’t stand out to me at all. “Ultra Marine Blues” is my second favorite track. It’s easily the most percussive on the album, full of jackhammer sounds that almost create a beat in some sections. It gives a skeleton, some bones to the noise. The end is even kind of bouncy! “Tokyo Times Ten” is some pussy shit. It’s the least harsh track, and if I’m listening, I want the harsh. What is this, a Sabrina Carpenter record??? By the time of “Worms Plastic Earthbound”, I’m a little over the album. The first half of this 25-minute monster kind of blows because it’s mostly just white noise. However, the back half returns to the height of intensity and insanity, so it all balances out. Merzbow does not let up on “Yellow Hyper Balls”, swinging for the fences of earrape even on the final track.

I also re-listened to *Metal Machine Music* and I easily preferred this album. Even though it can blend together, I like that *Pulse Demon* keeps evolving, and I also prefer the antagonism created by the harshness. It’s just a more interesting listen.

Favorite tracks: Woodpecker No. 1, Spiral Blast, Ultra Marine Blues

Least favorite track: Tokyo Times Ten
