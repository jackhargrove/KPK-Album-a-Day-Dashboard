# Write-up

As one of the most famous people to ever live, whose peers in terms of global influence include Napoleon and Jesus of Nazareth, I cannot possibly hope to give a comprehensive view of Michael Jackson’s career in the 4,000 character iMessage allows me. So, here’s a quick overview. A failed boxer named Joe Jackson married a failed musician named Katherine Scruse ad the couple had nine children together in beautiful Gary, Indiana. Joe was a psycho who had his five oldest sons form a vocal group and would beat them in rehearsal until they were perfect. Michael, born in 1958 and therefore the youngest member, was made the lead singer of the group they called the Jackson 5. After becoming local celebrities in the late 60s, they were signed by legendary soul music label Motown and moved to LA. Their debut single on Motown, 1969’s “I Want You Back”, topped the Billboard Hot 100 and launched the whole family to stardom, particularly 11-year-old Michael. The Jackson 5 continued as one of the most successful black artists throughout the 1970s with Michael as both lead singer and songwriter, helping bring soul and disco to white America. Michael released four solo albums from 1972-1975, but all are typically viewed as part of the extended Jackson 5 universe rather than as part of his solo career. At age 19 and ready break away from his family, Michael moved to NYC in 1977 to play the Scarecrow in *The Wiz*, an all black musical adaptation of *The Wizard of Oz*. On set, he met former jazz musician and record executive Quincy Jones, who agreed to produce Michael’s next album. The result, 1979’s *Off the Wall*, was a success of unbelievable proportions. It sold 3 million copies by the end of the year, 20 million copies as of 2025, and is remembered as the best disco album of the 70s. Jackson became one of the most famous musicians in America overnight, allowing him to fire his abusive father as his manager. Not satisfied, Jackson and Jones aimed to outdo their success with their next album, 1982’s *Thriller*. Most would agree they succeeded on account of the fact that it is to this day the best-selling album of all-time. With singles like “Billie Jean”, “Beat It”, and “Thriller”, how could it not be? His sound moved beyond the disco of *Off the Wall*, pioneering the genres of dance-pop and contemporary R&B. The music video for “Thriller” revolutionized the medium, and his dance moves like the moonwalk made men and woman around the world cream their jeans.

At the pinnacle of his success, Jackson’s life started to fall apart after *Thriller*. After catching on fire while filming a Pepsi commercial in 1984, he required extensive plastic surgery and prescription painkillers, both of which would become recurring lifelong addictions. He was also diagnosed with vitiligo and lupus, two diseases that made him insecure to the point of psychosis about his appearance. His unprecedented fame also resulted in absurd and slanderous stories from the tabloids. In response to all of this, he sought a darker and edgier aesthetic on his next studio album and third produced by Jones, *Bad*. Sonically, it largely continued in the same direction as *Thriller*, but with  new digital synthesizers and a harder sound. The title track “Bad” was supposed to be a duet with Prince, who turned it down as a result of his mild homophobia. Its music video was directed by Martin Scorsese. “Smooth Criminal”, now one of the most acclaimed songs of his career, describes the murder of a woman in her apartment. Five of the singles released from *Bad* charted at number 1 on the Billboard Hot 100, a record that has since only been matched by Katy Perry’s *Teenage Dream*. While the album was considered a critical and commercial disappointment compared to *Thriller*, it was by any other measure a success, becoming the 12th best-selling album of all time. It earned him his eternal nickname “The King of Pop” in 1989. Its reputation has only increased since, and the three albums Jackson made with Jones are considered his masterpieces.

Jackson remained the most popular music artist in the world through the early 90s. His next album, 1991’s *Dangerous*, was his first without Quincy Jones since the 70s. It was, of course, still a success, but marked the beginning of his artistic decline. In 1993, he became the first pop musician to perform during the halftime of the Super Bowl, inventing the modern spectacle we still have today. Sadly, 1993 was also the year Jackson’s personal life began to spiral beyond the point of repair. He was accused of child molestation for the first time, spawning a media circus of a trial. Though he was eventually exonerated, the ordeal was perhaps too much for him to handle. His 1995 album *HIStory: Past, Present, and Future, Book I* expressed his anger at his treatment by the press and the justice system. His 2001 album *Invincible* would prove to be his last. He was charged with multiple counts of child molestation in 2003, beginning a highly publicized case that would last two years. He was again acquitted, but this time the damage to his reputation was daunting. He became a nomadic recluse and somehow accumulated $500 million of debt. To make money, he planned a comeback tour for 2009, his first since 1997. Shortly before it began, however, he overdosed on a prescription anesthetic and died at age 50. The reaction to the news was seismic, with 2.5 *billion* people watching his funeral on TV. Searches of his name nearly crashed Google. In the 15 years since his death, Jackson has been as popular as ever, topping Forbes’ list of the highest-earning dead celebrities every year but three. He remains controversial, especially thanks to continued debate over the accusations against him. Still, his influence on global culture cannot be overstated; by all metrics, he was the most influential cultural figure of the 20th century. His solo work is the foundation upon which all dance-pop and contemporary R&B is built, and his career progression from the all-black world of Motown to the most popular artist in the world is directly responsible for the dominance of African-American culture worldwide over the last 40 years.

# Hargrove's review

I’ve always kind of thought of Michael Jackson as having the soul of an outsider artist (à la Ariel Pink or Daniel Johnston) attached to the body of someone who was a cog in the machine of the most successful music industry production factory in history before he even hit puberty. His pop sensibility was an ingrained reflex that would always result in him being popular, but his weirdo mind and compositional techniques are what made him the most popular musician in the history of the world. When you read about the way he would write songs by singing bass lines, drum beats, and other component parts into a tape recorder that then other people would translate into musical notation, it really only conjures up stories of like the way Ariel Pink made tapes in his high school garage. Add in his one-of-a-kind high-pitched voice, indecipherable ad-libs, adoption of new electronic technology, exaggerated silent film dance moves, and alien physical appearance, and you get a recipe for a new kind of pop star fitting for a decade as aesthetically idiosyncratic as the 80s. We give the 90s a lot of credit for bringing the underground to the mainstream, but so many of those artists, as much as I love them, were 60s-revivalist poptimists at heart who just wanted to make melodic guitar music. We associate the 80s with plastic commercialism and the mainstream, sure, but what the mainstream produced was often genuinely bizarre.

Like all good Americans, I was familiar with many of MJ’s songs before I was aware of the concept of music. I never actually did a real deep dive as an adult though until the summer of 2024, most of which I spent listening to *Thriller* and the hits from *Off the Wall* and *Bad*. It felt really weird to be a 23-year-old telling people “Did you realize ‘Billie Jean’ is super good???”, but it really is! This was my first time listening to *Bad* all the way through, however; as good as the singles are, the deep cuts on *Thriller* don’t inspire you to listen to other MJ albums in full. Overall, I think the story of this album is that the singles aren’t as good as *Thriller* but that the filler tracks are better (coincidentally, the exact same is also true of *Off the Wall*). “Smooth Criminal” is the only other MJ song in the same tier as “Billie Jean”, an impressive accomplishment that sets *Bad* apart. “Bad” is nowhere near as good as those two, nor is it in the same league as “Beat It”, but I think it’s about as good as “Thriller” or “PYT”. “The Way You Make Me Feel” can sit nicely alongside “Wanna Be Startin’ Somethin’” to round out the comparison of the two album’s highs. Clearly, *Thriller* is the winner, but *Bad* is no slouch. On the other hand, filler like “Speed Demon”, “Another Part of Me”, and “Dirty Diana” (yes, the latter two were released as singles, but pretty much every song on this album was; he was the biggest artist in the history of the world, any song would sell!) clear just about any track on *Thriller* I haven’t mentioned by name. “Liberian Girl” has that adult contemporary atmosphere that isn’t ideal, but it’s just about the best example of that kind of song I can think of. I want to like “Man in the Mirror” for its self-improvement lyrics and catchiness, but it’s just sooooooo cheeeeeeesy. “Just Good Friends” isn’t awful, but it’s a waste of Stevie Wonder. “I Just Can’t Stop Loving You” is the only track I actively dislike, a musical wallpaper ballad that reminds me of my least favorite song from *Wicked*. *Bad* has some stinkers like any pop album does, but overall it’s a much more consistent album. Does that make it better? Probably not. But it was fun to listen to! I really enjoyed the digital synthesizers that they used throughout the album. *Off the Wall* and *Thriller* wouldn’t have worked without the warmth of their instrumentation and analogue synthesizers, but the digital synthesizers on *Bad* give it a cold, harsh, inhuman feel that complements MJ’s mood.

I gave less details than normal on individual songs, but there’s really no value in me saying “I appreciated the vocal performance on X” ten times or explaining why “Smooth Criminal” is so fucking good. This is a work as distinctive and singular as any on this list, but the difference is that your great-aunt has heard all of these songs. God bless the 80s.

Favorite tracks: Bad, The Way You Make Me Feel, Speed Demon, Another Part of Me, Dirty Diana, Smooth Criminal

Least favorite track: I Just Can’t Stop Loving You
