# Write-up

Miles Davis was a jazz trumpeter born in 1926 and raised in St. Louis. He is one of the most influential musicians in history, pioneering cool jazz, hard bop, modal jazz, post-bop, and jazz fusion. He also saw significant commercial success, with 1959’s *Kind of Blue* remaining the best selling jazz album of all time. He began playing trumpet as a teen, and decided to pursue jazz as a career when he played with Art Blakey, Dizzy Gillespie, and Charlie Parker when they visited St. Louis in 1944. He moved to NYC to attend Juilliard, but cut classes to hang out at the clubs where his hero Parker was developing bebop. Davis dropped out of Juilliard in 1945 and spent the late 40s playing in bands with big names. He bcame frustrated with bebop’s focus on technique and virtuosity by 1948, so he formed the Miles Davis Nonet; the slow chill music they recorded in 1949, re-released as the compilation *Birth of the Cool* in 1957, would inspire the cool jazz style of the West Coast. His personal life began to spin out in 1950 as he became increasingly addicted to heroin, causing him to leave NYC in 1953; he successfully kicked his addiction, returning to the city in 1954 a new man. He formed a quintet that played music more aggressive than cool jazz and introduced blues and pop influences, pioneering hard bop.

In 1955, he formed the First Great Quintet, featuring saxophonist John Coltrane, pianist Red Garland, bassist Paul Chambers, and drummer Philly Joe Jones. With the addition of saxophonist Cannonball Adderley, they recorded *Milestones* in 1958, Davis’ first exploration of musical modes. Garland left afterward, prompting the addition of pianist Bill Evans, who also explored modality. With Evans’ influence, Davis recorded *Kind of Blue* in 1959, the album that created modal jazz and became the best selling jazz album of all time. Concurrent to his modal jazz era, Davis dabbled in classical music, with *Sketches of Spain* the best known album that resulted. The First Great Quintet dissolved in 1962, so Davis formed the Second Great Quintet in 1963 with a young Herbie Hancock at piano and saxophonist Wayne Shorter joining in 1964. The group spent the mid-60s incorporating light influences from avant-garde and free jazz into hard bop to make well-known post-bop records.

By the late 60s, jazz had significantly declined in popularity, and it was clear rock was here to stay. Davis responded by incorporating electric guitar and rock rhythms on his 1969 album *In a Silent Way*. Though highly controversial at the time, with both jazz and rock critics dismissing it, it is now remembered as one of his best and most important records. This combination of jazz and rock (and later, jazz and funk) was called jazz fusion, and 1968-1975 became Davis’ “electric period”. He continued down this path with *Bitches Brew* in 1970, his first major commercial success in years. He began to run more in rock circles than jazz circles, sharing stages with Neil Young and Jimi Hendrix. With the explosion in popularity of funk music, Davis’ fusion traded rock for funk starting 1972’s *On the Corner*. The electric period culminated in 1974’s *Get Up With It*, which incorporated krautrock to predict ambient, and a series of highly experimental, grandiose live albums including 1975’s *Agharta*. Davis dropped out of the music scene in 1975 and spent the next five years drinking and fucking in his UWS brownstone. He returned to music in 1981, spending the 80s as a washed-up legend collaborating with music stars and releasing 10 dogshit albums. In 1991, he began playing old music from his earlier career, something he famously refused to do before; he died later that year at age 65.

*Bitches Brew* was recorded in 1969 and released in 1970. It was Davis’ second foray into the fusion of jazz and rock. His band consisted of soprano saxophonist Wayne Shorter, electric pianist Chick Corea, double bassist Dave Holland, and drummer Jack DeJohnette, who along with Davis and his trumpet played on all six tracks (with the exception of “John McLaughlin”, which does not feature Davis or Shorter). However, the album also featured eight guest musicians: bass clarinetist Bennie Maupin, electric pianist Joe Zawinul, electric pianist Larry Young, electric guitarist John McLaughlin, electric bassist Harvey Brooks, drummer Lenny White, drummer and percussionist Don Alias, and percussionist Juma Santos. Brooks had previously played bass guitar on Bob Dylan’s *Highway 61 Revisited* and on some albums by the Doors. Maupin later became a member of Herbie Hancock’s fusion band the Headhunters and played multiple instruments on the album *Head Hunters*. It was produced by Teo Macero, who also produced Dave Brubeck’s *Time Out* and Thelonious Monk’s *Monk’s Dream*.

Davis sketched out the basic chord lines of each track, then told his musicians to play anything they wanted that fit over those chords. They played 15 hours a day for three days, all arranged in a semicircle around Davis and Shorter, who acted as conductors. Then, in post-production, Davis and Macero heavily edited every track (with the exception of “Miles Runs the Voodoo Down”). They put small bits together to form complete compositions and used innovative studio-as-instrument techniques like reverb and delay. *Bitches Brew* was notable for its massive rhythm section, featuring as many as nine rhythm instruments (bass, drums, piano) simultaneously. Davis’ trumpet and Shorter’s soprano sax were the primary solo instruments. Davis’ trumpet solos were notably faster and more aggressive than he was known for previously.

The album peaked at #35 on the Billboard 200, making it Davis’ highest charting album, and was eventually certified gold. It also won a Grammy. Its massive commercial success spurred a 70s resurgence of jazz (particularly fusion) after the genre had been pronounced dead in the 60s. It certainly had its haters, however, with many from the jazz world accusing Davis of selling out. Donald Fagen of Steely Dan also had some not nice things to say about it. The album is still influential today; notably, Thom Yorke cited it as one of Radiohead’s chief influences for *OK Computer*. Herbie Hancock’s seminal 1973 album *Headhunters* was also massively influenced by *Bitches Brew*.

# Hargrove's review

I unfortunately do not have good headphones, just some AirPods Pro, but I can see exactly what McGary means. There is so much going on in this record, so many instruments coming at you from different angles and opposite sides, melding together as one and separating again. I don’t know how you could really catch everything in a standard stereo setup, though I bet this would go HARD at The Sphere with visuals based off the album cover. This cacophony, along with the heavy use of echo and reverb to create space, makes you feel entirely absorbed in the world of *Bitches Brew* (particularly disc 1). Generally in my reviews, I give a lot of weight to memorability, to how well I can play the music in my head after a few listens. Well, I didn’t walk away from this record humming any melodies, but I cannot deny that my final, no distractions, fully immersed listen I did just before writing this was quite the experience. The in-the-moment feeling that listening to this album gives you can really only be compared to watching a film in IMAX.

First and foremost, this thing does not have any want for rhythm. Reading about the (up to) 9 member rhythm section is one thing, but feeling the sheer wall of sound it brings is another thing entirely. I give my highest regards to bass guitarist Harvey Brooks, whose nonstop vamping kept this album together even through the weaker tracks at the end. Whole the drummers were generally good on their own, the double drumset + percussion setup reaps rewards greater than the sum of its parts; I can’t help but be reminded of Swans’ similar setup on *Filth* (and live in concert too). When both sets play cymbals during a loud section, it’s pure sex. Similarly, I found the three piano setup quite cool for the most part. I really like the electric piano as a timbre in the first place, but the rapid, jumbled-up attack of piano keys creates a rhythm unto itself. On the flip side, I could barely hear the double bass outside of the final track, and I was a bit underwhelmed by McLaughlin’s contributions on electric guitar, though I don’t deny that electric guitar is an important texture to this album. As for the solo instruments, Davis’ trumpet was clearly the best. He just plays so sharp here, with each blow into the horn shooting a finely carved spear into my brain. The trumpet interacts with the stereo trickery and intense echo so beautifully, particularly on the title track. Shorter’s sax takes second prize, coming in sparingly to play frantic, scratchy, high-pitched melodies. In dead last is Maupin’s bass clarinet, which isn’t entirely his fault; I just do not like the instrument that much. It’s too breathy, too raspy, too muddled to really make its point. Beyond just its existence, my biggest complaint is the frequency with which it solos, perhaps even eclipsing Shorter. In terms of the tracks themselves, the two on disc 1 are unimpeachable, while disc 2 starts a bit weaker and gets worse with each successive track. This album never gets bad, but I struggle to say it justifies its length. Weirdly, I was both disappointed and satisfied by this record. On the one hand, it has more flaws than I would hope to find in a record of its stature. On the other, though challenging at first, it’s a much more straightforwardly enjoyable experience than I had initially expected. Overall, I enjoyed it more than any of its peer albums (not every jazz album we’ve listened to, just the grand sprawling statements from hard bop icons) except for Herbie Hancock’s *Head Hunters* and Charles Mingus’ *The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady*.

“Pharaoh’s Dance” begins chaotic but quiet, a clear prelude to something big. By the time Davis’ first trumpet solo begins, the rhythm section has already swelled massively and locked into a serious groove. While Maupin’s bass clarinet solo flops just a bit, Shorter’s solo later on is heavenly, and McLaughlin admittedly shreds the guitar when he gets the chance later on. However, Davis’ second solo stands alone as mu favorite. The way it loops and echos in the beginning trips me out more than most psychedelic music. As the song comes to a climax, thoroughly bizarre piano lines melt into the groove as everything swells into a tidal wave beneath Davis’ ascendant trumpet, and then…it breaks as easily as it formed, leaving us to contemplate Davis’ trumpet for the final two minutes. I love the nimbleness of the groove despite its sheer weight. “Bitches Brew” begins with three minutes of the thickest, spaciest atmosphere known to man. Davis’ trumpet drips with echo and reverb, darting from one ear to the other in a straight line. The left channel is delayed ever so slightly, making it feel like the sound is actually moving. Around 3:00, a hefty bass vamp kicks in that won’t stop for more than 10 minutes. Shorter begins by playing his sax around the bass in the most delightful way, but eventually he’s drowned out by the crescendoing drums. This particular extended groove section is one of my favorite stretches in jazz; I love how the trumpet’s intensity pulls the rhythm section up with it as it ascends, I love hearing Davis give instructions to the other musicians, I love Shorter’s bumblebee sax, I love the hectic clanging of cymbals and percussion, but more than anything, I love that bass vamp. Everything cuts out at 14:40 to reprise the atmosphere from the opening before giving way to a second groove section at 17:20. When Davis’ trumpet came in loud and clear at 19:27, it genuinely scared me on this listen. Things pick back up until 24:05, when the atmosphere comes back for a third time to close out the track. While I definitely prefer the groovy sections, I have to admit that the effects in the atmospheric section are just mesmerizing. On its own, disc 1 holds its own with the best LPs we’ve heard so far.

Dis 2 starts with “Spanish Key”, where the almost-vamp of the bass defines the starting section for me. The bass begins with a few bars of “da duh, da duh, da duh duh, da duh”, but then becomes “da duh, da duh duh, da duh duh, da duh”, continuously shifting ever slightly and adding unexpected notes so you can’t settle into a groove. Then, at about 3:30, Corea’s electric piano plays a quick, fantastic line that sweeps everything into shape. This short piano melody serves as a motif in this track, calling back the groove every so often when things get out of hand. While these moments are pure sex, the groove does unfortunately lose me a couple of times, the first time this happens on the album. This track also puts more focus on the electric guitar, which doesn’t quite work for me. Still, on the whole it’s a banger track and nearly on par with the opener. “John McLaughlin” gives even more attention to the electric guitar in its minuscule four minute run time. I do like the way it plays against Corea’s piano, one in each ear, but this track’s runtime means the rhythm section can never swell into a real groove. “Miles Runs the Voodoo Down” is interesting in that it has such a different kind of groove than the other tracks. It’s much funkier and more off-beat, perhaps presaging the jazz-funk direction Davis goes on subsequent albums. It’s a weird adjustment, but I was able to get into it. This track has two major strong suits. The first is the bass guitar vamp, perhaps even better than the one on the title track. The second is its tendency to grow into short but fierce plateaus of noise. Unfortunately, however, outside of those sections, this is a weaker track than the three prior long ones. It just spends too much time in the slow sections. The album ends with “Sanctuary”, starting so quiet you can actually hear the double bass finally. This cracks truly begin to show on this track, with the disjointed chaos of the instruments preventing them from blending together into one big groove. Instead, it’s kind of an overstimulating mess. That being said, I do have to admit the crescendo in the final minute goes crazy, with Davis’ trumpet howling like a wolf. But otherwise, this is clearly the weakest track. That’s okay, though. *Head Hunters* couldn’t stick the landing either.

This album was daunting going into it, but ended up being a lot of fun. It seems so serious, but once you give it a chance it’s all about the hypnotic feel. It makes me want to move. And I respect its ambition quite a bit. In terms of this sound, I do think that on *Head Hunters* Herbie Hancock distills it down to its essence to create a much tighter product. And in terms of sheer ambition and grandeur, Charles Mingus finds meaning in the madness just that much better on *The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady*. But *Bitches Brew* can hold its own against either, and I hope to periodically revisit it for the rest of my life, especially once I get a good pair of headphones, McGary.

Favorite tracks: Pharaoh’s Dance, Bitches Brew, Spanish Key

Least favorite track: Sanctuary
