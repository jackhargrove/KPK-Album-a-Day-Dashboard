# Write-up

Minutemen hail from sunny SoCal's San Pedro, and hailed from the same hardcore scene as Black Flag, whose SST would release their albums. While Black Flag emphasized aggression over everything, Minutemen were far more eclectic, drawing from a range of influences and musical styles. It's a misconception that their name come from their song lengths (which often got them in trouble with crowds who were disappointed at short sets), they played their music fast and short. Mike Watt and D. Boon, the bassist and lead singer/guitarist met as kids growing up in San Pedro and would later join a band called The Reactionaries. While The Reactionaries failed to put any music to tape, they took a liking to the drummer, George Hurley. When the Reactionaries broke up, Hurley was their first choice for a new band, and after trying to make do with Frank Tonche on drums while Hurley was playing in a different band, they got their man and the Minutemen were well underway. The entire scene at the time was dirt cheap, but even among the scene, Minutemen stood out as "econo" to the max. They would record in the order that songs would be on the album, rehearse heavily, and take nighttime studio slots to keep costs down. They looked to make most of their money touring, but still held down day jobs while playing in the band, despite being big enough to tour with R.E.M. and Black Flag.

Double Nickels on the Dime is a sprawling double album that was among the first non Black Flag releases on Greg Ginn's SST records. The direction inspiration for the album was label mate Hüsker Dü's Zen Arcade, which had been recorded a month prior. Stylistically, Pink Flag is more obvious in its influence, both records being punk suites that pushed the boundaries of their scenes. The album's title is one of many inside jokes in the album, being a rebuttal to mainstream rock song "Can't Drive 55" (double nickels being local trucker slang for 55). The band decided that the real punk thing to do was to drive the speed limit and make your own music, and driving became the broader concept of the album. Corona is the most famous song on the album thanks to MTV's Criterion Channel featured Jackass adopting it as their theme song. The first 3 quarters of the album were each allotted to one member who chose their favorite songs, with the 4th side of the double album being allotted to the "Chaff" that was left on the cutting room floor. Boon and Watt did most of the songwriting, although friends Henry Rollins and Chuck Dukowski of Black Flag contributed, as did Hurley. There are also several covers of classic rock songs on the album, although some are cut from the CD version.

While the album wasn't a big hit at the time, it has become revered over time, and along with Zen Arcade and Daydream Nation, seen as a core predecessor album of indie rock. D. Boon was killed in a car crash a year after the album was released, causing the band to disband. Mike Watt has bounced around the music scene for a while, playing with Sonic Youth on the Ciccone project, playing bass for the Stooges and on his own projects, most prominently fIREHOSE. Watt and Hurley still reunite from time to time to play Minutemen's songs, although never under the band name and only as a duo, refusing to replace D. Boon's parts.

# Hargrove's review

I have gotten very behind on this project simply because I’ve had no idea how to review this album. In general, I struggle with albums of super short songs; this was troubling for me with *Pink Flag* as well. As a double album of 45 short tracks, however, *Double Nickels on the Dime* is sprawling and sometimes impossible to parse. Other than a few standouts, the songs endlessly blend together, making it very difficult for me to cite specific songs as examples of my opinions like I typically do. None of this is to say I didn’t like this album - there is a lot to love! “Corona” is a cowpunk banger that feels as if it could be the theme song of Southern California (and, indeed, it was used as the theme of the LA-based *Jackass*. “Viet Nam” comes early on and is one of the first tracks to really leave a lasting impression. “Maybe Partying Will Help” not only shreds in every aspect, but it’s just a really well-written song. “History Lesson Part 2” is sweet without being cloying, and is certainly an emotional high point of the record. “This Ain’t No Picnic”, on the other hand, is damn angry, featuring some of Boon’s most impassioned vocals. “Dr. Wu” is highly catchy, though I do have a long-time soft-spot for Steely Dan. “The Glory of Man” has my favorite of Boon’s guitar licks on the entire album, and Hurley keeps it driving throughout. These are the songs that stand out to me as truly good. The rest of the album is frequently good as well. Mike Watt is the hero overall, with his funky bass setting Minutemen apart from any other band I’ve ever heard. Additionally, his curated side of the record (side 2) is the most banger-ful. Boon’s guitar is often good, but it can get noodly, and his vocals get more and more tiresome over the cours of nearly an hour and a half. George Hurley consistently lays down great guitar beats. They never take themselves too seriously, which is refreshing. But it all just blends together. I would so much rather they made this a single album, or they fleshed out the songs that worked really well. An album with so much to like; in fact, and I mean this seriously and negatively, an album with too much to like.

Favorite tracks: Viet Nam, Maybe Partying Will Help, Corona, The Glory of Man, History Lesson Part 2, This Ain’t No Picnic, Dr. Wu

Least favorite track: You Need the Glory

# Aidan's review

Really wish I liked this album more than I did. If indie rock had saints, D. Boon would be chief among their number, and this album would be seen as a holy work. Mike Watt’s bass is incredible, from when it starts the album and reverberating down every track. There’s masterful tracks throughout this album, Cohesion, Corona, History Lesson Pt. 2, There Ain’t Shit On TV Tonight, June 16th and Dr. Wu show a range that few bands can match, a feat that took the trio a month and eleven hundred dollars to put to tape. But there’s a lot of stinkers on the album too, and D. Boon’s spoken word vocal style can grate when stretched over the 80 minute running time. I’ve remarked before how I think it’s near impossible to make a punk album that’s good the whole way through, and while this is more punk in spirit than music, it still has the same trouble. There’s a not any egregiously bad songs, but the chaff don’t just take up its labeled disc. You have to admire the ambition, but the volume results in a lot of waste, especially with the monotony of the vocals. Still, the guitars sound better than albums with two extra zeros on their budgets, and the gems are all time classics.
