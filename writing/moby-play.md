# Write-up

Moby (real name Richard Melville Hall) is an electronic musician born in Harlem in 1965. His father gave him the nickname “Moby” as a newborn in reference to *Moby-Dick* given Moby’s unusual size and a mistaken belief of descent from Herman Melville. His father died while drunk driving when Moby was two; his mother raised him in poverty in the affluent town of Darien, Connecticut. He began playing music at age nine, receiving training in classical guitar, piano, jazz, and percussion. He put this classical training to good use by playing guitar for local hardcore punk bands as a teenager in the early 80s. Moby began attending UConn in 1983, where he began learning electronic music. College proved unfulfilling, causing him to drop out to work as a DJ in Greenwich, CT. In 1989, aged 24, Moby moved to NYC to break into the big leagues of DJing. By 1990, an independent label signed him. “Go”, his third single, became a surprise hit in the UK and the Netherlands. Moby’s newfound popularity and background in alt rock music led to him becoming a remixer of bands like the Smashing Pumpkins and Soundgarden. His label released his debut album, *Moby*, in 1992. Moby released his second album, *Ambient*, in 1993, the same year he went on tour with the Prodigy. He signed with Elektra Records, who released his first US dance charts #1 single “Move (You Make Me Feel So Good)”. He went on tour with Aphex Twin. Moby released his third album, *Everything Is Wrong*, in 1995. Despite receiving critical acclaim for its diverse blend of electronic styles, it sold poorly. Moby still straddled the alt rock and electronic worlds; his fourth album, 1996’s *Animal Rights*, turned toward guitar-based alt rock and punk that alienated his fanbase. Hitching his wagon to a dying genre in the late 1990s, many claimed Moby’s career peak was behind him, and he left his label in 1998.

In 1999, Moby released his fifth album, *Play*, on independent label V2 Records. The album’s concept revolved around the recordings of ethnomusicologist Alan Lomax, who traveled rural America in the 1930s with his father to preserve hundreds of blues and folk songs. Many of the tracks take Lomax’s recordings of these old blues singers and sample them over Moby’s downtempo electronic beats. When starting the project, Moby intended it to be his final album. Initially, *Play* sold poorly. Only 40 people attended his first NYC show in support of the album. Out of desperation, Moby devised a new commercial strategy: license songs from *Play* to any film, TV, or commercial director he could for cheap. This approach worked; *Play* re-entered the UK charts in January 2000, seven months after release, and remained there for the duration of the year. By October 2000, *Play* had gone platinum in 17 countries, selling two-million copies in the US alone. Over this period, eight singles were released from the album. “South Side” received a remix with vocals from Gwen Stefani which became Moby’s first single on the Billboard Hot 100 (peaking at #14), while “Porcelain” got a music video directed by Jonas Åkerlund, former member of Swedish black metal band Bathory. “God’s Gonna Cut You Down” is a cover of a traditional American folk song that would later be covered by Johnny Cash and Marilyn Manson.

As a result of *Play*’s success, Moby became a celebrity in the early 2000s. However, his decision to license the songs of *Play* to all-comers made him synonymous with “sellout”. His next album, 2002’s *18*, kept the same style as *Play* and was also a huge success. Since then, Moby has released 17 albums under his own name, with the most recent coming in 2026. He has also released numerous side projects and collaborations, often with artists from our list. He remains one of the most well-known figures in popular electronic music. After living in NYC from 1989 to 2010, he moved to LA, where he currently lives. He has famously been a vegan for more than 40 years and is a prominent animal rights activist.

# Hargrove's review

Before listening to this album, the extent of my exposure to Moby consisted of that *How I Met Your Mother* episode that Lefko so kindly ensured we didn’t forget about last night. To be honest, that episode left me with the impression that Moby’s music was probably not up my alley. I mean, all it told me about him was that he was an early 00s electronic music DJ well-known enough to be referenced a network sitcom but anonymous enough for his subplot to involve a case of mistaken identity. Over the years, the fact that I had never heard of him in any other context solidified my mental categorization of Moby as an artist I didn’t need to look into. As a result, I was actually pretty surprised when I learned that *Play* was on this list given the deep overlap between my music exposure and the /mu/ essentials. So I tried to clear my preconceptions and go into this album with a blank slate. Unfortunately, just about everything I suspected about Moby turned out to be true. It really sucks when that happens because I do not want to fall into rigid, insular thinking about music. I want to be surprised, to like albums I never in a million years thought I would. But *Play* is just so…nonexistent. I’ve listened to this album seven times now. That’s nearly seven-and-a-half hours. Had I instead zoned out and stared at the wall in silence during that time, I would have derived the same benefit. It’s not offensive to any of the sense; in fact, I greatly wish it were. It’s just a formless amalgam of familiar sounds designed to wash over me. Thankfully, I was able to locate a few things I enjoyed about this record. For one, it at least does this exact sound strictly better than Röyksopp, which established a floor in my rankings. The compositions are sleeker and tighter than Röyksopp’s in every way. Another thing I enjoyed about it was how the album has a genuine arc in its sound as it progresses. Similarly to *Songs for the Deaf*, it starts with its tightest pop songs before slowly disintegrating into hazy atmospheric pieces. This at least creates a sense of progress not found in any of the terribly static tracks. Finally, there were five tracks that distinguished themselves above the rest: “Porcelain”, “South Side”, “Machete”, “Everloving”, and “Guitar Flute & String”. I don’t like any enough to add to my liked songs playlist, but they at least brightened the listening experience some. Overall though, this just wasn’t for me. I would never seek this out in a million years.

*Play* starts with “Honey”, probably the single best illustration of Moby’s “style” on this album. Now, that style is just not my thing, but I can at least appreciate it as a proof-of concept. Sadly, the constant piano pounding gets seriously grating. Something about the piano also sounds familiar in a bad way, like it was used in a commercial or something. The vocal sample is catchy, but the martial repetition removes the human element in my opinion. The vocal sample in “Find My Baby” drags badly. Worse, I hate the guitar here. It reminds me of the guitar tone in the Home Depot commercials. The rhythm takes a similar shape to a Massive Attack rhythm but lacks any of the hulking menace. “Porcelain” is a real standout from this album. The strings here add direction to the weightless music, making it sound like a soaring eagle instead of an aimless paper bag. The piano may be sentimental, but it works here. I dislike Moby’s vocals on every track where he sings, but these are the least bad thanks to the heavy overdubs. “Why Does My Heart Feel So Bad?” does a lot of the same things that the last track does well. Seriously. Like, in an uncanny way. That makes it sound stale, which, combined with the aimless structure, makes me not like it. “South Side” has a solid bass line and breakbeat that I could actually see being used in a low-mid Massive Attack track this time. I would much rather someone other than Moby sang lead, so I imagine the Gwen Stefani version might be worth checking out. That being said, I like the production on the chorus and it’s certainly attention-grabbing. It’s a well-constructed and catchy piece. “Rushing” brings a huge slowdown that this record lacks the punch or oomph to afford. It’s just flaccid. I despise “Bodyrock”. The Home Depot guitar returns to annoy the shit out of me. The strings just don’t work on such an aggressive track. And could he have chosen a more grating hip hop sample? I hate hate hate this track. I like the catchiness of the vocal sample on “Natural Blues”, but I have trouble getting over the done-to-death house structure in the way it builds. Speaking of, “Machete” brings a pounding four-on-the-floor kick and a squelching synth. Yes, this is certainly acid house. However, even if it’s generic, I appreciate the new sound to break up the dreary sameness of the rest of the album.

The instrumental interlude “7” lasts less than a minute and consists of a vaguely off-beat rhythm and aimless guitar. It feels like an interlude that exists just for the sake of being an interlude. “Run On” has the same annoying eighth note piano as “Honey”. However, I do like the delicate piano flourishes, which remind me of Trent Reznor’s score for *The Social Network*. I also appreciate this approach to sampling where Moby lets the song actually unfold because it feels less mechanical. This would be a favorite track were it not for that piano line. “Down Slow” is another insubstantial interlude without much reason to exist, but this time it’s worse because it doesn’t even depart from the sound of the album. The Home Depot guitar returns a third time in “If Things Were Perfect”. Somehow, though, it’s not the worst part. Moby delivers one of the worst vocal performances I’ve ever heard. He oversells the depressive vibe to a comical degree. The clumsy lyrics don’t help; he says “cold” three lines in a row on verse 1! “Everloving” provides a genuine departure from the sound of the album with its pretty guitar. When the drums and piano enter, both are kept pleasantly light. It’s refreshing to get these new timbres deep into the album. “Inside” is formless, but it almost works because the darker atmosphere provides a taste of the edginess that this album so sorely lacks. It would have been a favorite track were it three minutes instead of five. “Guitar Flute & String” keeps up the same vibe as the previous track but in a much tighter two minutes. Additionally, the sparse arrangement contrasts nicely with the sound I’ve grown so sick of. The flue is my favorite part. “The Sky Is Broken” starts well enough with a simple, relaxed breakbeat, but I absolutely want to kill myself when Moby’s spoken word starts. You can tell that it tries to keep a similar atmosphere to the three prior instrumentals but it fails miserably. Only “Bodyrock” keeps it from my least favorite slot. “My Weakness” actually does recapture the vibe of the instrumental trio, but it doesn’t do much with it at all. It’s a characteristically unmoving way for an unmoving album to conclude.

At the end of the day, I need my music to have at least little bit of edge, and this just had none. At no point did I feel engaged, excited, or moved. Moby’s lucky that we listened to it after Röyksopp so at least I know it can get worse.

Favorite tracks: Porcelain, South Side, Machete, Everloving, Guitar Flute & String

Least favorite track: Bodyrock

# Aidan's review

Moby is not cool. It's entirely plausibly that sometime in his childhood, he found some monkey's paw and wished to be successful, famous and rich, but the fatal drawback is that he would never be cool. As thus, this album is also not cool. The idea of blending electronic music with old field records is very cool. I did not realize that "God's Gonna Cut You Down" was a cover until this album because Cash's version is so good. Moby's version is fine, but it is certainly not cool. Some parts of this album are completely fine. While it starts out with the rather annoying "Honey", it quickly settles into a nice groove with "Porcelain" and "Why Does My Heart Feels So Bad?". "South Side" is completely vacuous and portends the issue with the rest of the album. The songs are just empty. When listening to several songs on this album, I'm haunted by the feeling that there's something interesting just around the corner, because the songs are never brave enough to fully embrace grooves, but simultaneously never do anything unpredictable. "Bodyrock" is probably the worst offender. It seems like the perfect song to cut out during the first chorus into something more interesting, like a mediocre MTV show or a mid aughts sports video game title screen. To his credit, Moby did pair his songs with more interesting things by listening them all to commercials. They're overqualified for that setting, but as actual music, it's not even interesting in the background. Even with the fascinating jazz samples, they're used in such a bland, predictable way that I don't care. It's sort of the opposite of Kanye's usage of "Strange Fruit" in "Blood On The Leaves", instead of being shockingly vulgar in the usage of powerful tracks, it just plops them down on a bog-standard beat and lets them be. I feel bad for Moby, who seems to be a fine dude with a talent for being uncool (although the whole Natalie Portman fake relationship claim is very weird). Album still isn't good. McGary keeps asking us to review a truly mid album and this might be the closest we'll get. It's far more listenable than the albums I'm putting it with, but I don't think there's another album that feels so full of filler tracks.
