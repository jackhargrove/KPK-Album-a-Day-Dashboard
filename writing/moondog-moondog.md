# Write-up

Moondog (real name Louis Hardin) was a self-taught composer born in 1916. He was born in Kansas, raised at a trading post in Wyoming, and later attended high school in Missouri. He was often exposed to Native American culture, which greatly informed his music. In 1934, at age 16, Moondog accidentally stumbled across an explosive in a field. He picked it up, it exploded in his face, and he went permanently blind. As he recovered, his sister began reading books to him, instilling a lifelong passion for philosophy, science, and music. Though he had always only played drums, he was inspired to become a composer. Through both self-teaching and education at schools for the blind, Moondog mastered music theory. In 1943, Moondog moved to NYC at age 27. While there, he became a figure in both the classical and jazz music scenes. He met composer Leonard Bernstein and bebop pioneer Charlie Parker. His music would combine the sounds of both classical and jazz. He adopted the name “Moondog” in 1947 to honor a local street dog that liked to howl at the moon.

Moondog became a famous street musician in NYC, spending his days performing music on the corner of 53rd Street and 6th Avenue. However, he was not homeless, and in fact he owned both an apartment in NYC and a country home upstate. He just enjoyed playing music for free on the street. He became known for his distinctive appearance as well as his music, sporting a long hair and a long beard. This caused some to compare him to a monk. Moondog disliked this, as he rejected Christianity and was obsessed with Norse mythology. As a result, he began wearing a horned Viking helmet in the 60s, a garment with which he became strongly identified. Moondog also commonly invented his own instruments, with the most famous being the trimba. In the 50s, Cleveland radio DJ Alan Freed (the man who popularized the term “rock and roll”) began calling himself “Moondog” and used one of Moondog’s compositions as his calling card. Moondog successfully sued him in a well-known episode in rock music history. Moondog moved to Germany in 1974, where he spent the rest of his life. Moondog died in Münster in 1999 at age 83.

*Moondog*, released in 1969, was Moondog’s seventh full-length album. The album came about thanks to music producer James William Guercio, known for his work with the band Chicago. Guercio had previously met Moondog, and he convinced Columbia Records to allow him to record an album in a major label environment. The album consists of music composed throughout the 50s and 60s, the period during which Moondog slowly transitioned away from jazz and into classical. Moondog conducted an ensemble of over 50 musicians for the album, including double bassist Ron Carter who also played on Freddie Hubbard’s *Red Clay* and Wayne Shorter’s *Speak No Evil*, as well as tuba player Don Butterfield who appeared on Charles Mingus’ *The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady*. “Lament 1 (Bird’s Lament)” was composed in 1955 after the death of jazz saxophonist Charlie Parker, also known as Bird. Thanks to Columbia Records, the album received a high level of publicity, with Moondog even appearing on *The Tonight Show*. It peaked at number six on the Billboard classical chart.

Moondog was notable for being highly influential on a wide variety of musical traditions. His New York playing in the 40s proved wildly influential on classical composers Steve Reich and Philip Glass, who pioneered the minimalism movement that took over the classical establishment in the 60s. Charlie Parker, the father of bebop and thus pretty much all the jazz we’ve listened to on this list, was also a huge fan. Additionally, weirdo artists from the rock tradition often cite Moondog as an influence, particularly Frank Zappa and T. Rex’s Marc Bolan. American Primitivist guitarist John Fahey covered a Moondog composition on an album in 1985. Moondog’s music was included on the soundtracks for both *The Big Lebowski* and *Pineapple Express*.

# Hargrove's review

I had never heard of Moondog before we started working through our list, but I’ve been looking forward to this album for a while. I read about him briefly last year and he seemed really out there. Thankfully, the music itself did not disappoint! This album features some of the most unique music I’ve ever heard without being at all inaccessible. There’s a lot more to it than this, but fundamentally it’s classical music played with a swung rhythm, two things that I never really thought about combining but that apparently go together beautifully. Outside of “Symphonique #3 (Ode to Venus)”, the rhythms on this album drive the music forward relentlessly, always upward and outward. Moondog used a large symphony to record this album, not wasting an ounce of the grandiosity that kind of assemblage can provide. The melodies interlock intricately, the grooves are complex, and the atmosphere is nothing short of epic. So many of these tracks make me feel like an infantryman in pre-gun warfare, ready to march into the oncoming horde. Moondog inserts the jazz elements sparingly and tastefully, like the saxophone solos on “Lament 1, ‘Bird’s Lament’” or the traded melodies in “Symphonique #6 (Good for Goodie). The album’s largest problem is the six-minute anchor that is “Symphonique #3 (Ode to Venus)”, halting the incredible energy generated by the first two tracks. I have various other nitpicks, such as the two couplets of poetry (two is just a weird number here. Either include one with every track, or don’t include them at all) and a bit of a weak closer. Ultimately, though, this record is nothing short of cinematic. You could set any scene to any one of these tracks and instantly make it feel 10x more important. I would feel so lucky to walk down the street and hear some wacko in a viking outfit and a long beard play something this powerful.

“Theme” fucking rules, one of the greatest album openers I’ve ever heard. I’m biased because 5/4 is my favorite time signature, but the rhythm of this track drives me wild. The melody builds tension so effectively while the arrangement does the same by slowly building. Rarely do I hear music with such upward momentum. It gets me hype every time I listen to it. Then, it all dissipates suddenly into the noise of the street. Gotti put it best when he said the drum groove on “Stamping Ground” is “tasty”. That little war drum gives it such a swing! This track keeps up the grandiosity from the opener, but this time the weight is pointed downward. It makes me feel like I’m marching into war in a suffocatingly heavy suit of armor. “Symphonique #3 (Ode to Venus)” is the biggest misstep on the album. I like the cascading woodwinds, but there’s very little momentum. It feels weightless by contrast, stretching on to nearly six minutes. The bass line that opens “Symphonique #6 (Good for Goodie)” effectively restarts the album and takes us back to the great drum-driven stuff. Like the last track, the melodies in the various instruments butt up against each other but the drums here help delineate the melodic lines and prevent them from becoming cacophonous. The result is a busy but fun and well-structured composition. I particularly like the off-beat and shrill horns that come in at the end. Those maracas (or whatever they are) in “Minisym  set such a great foundation for the track. The rhythm drops out in the second section, but the instruments play slower and more together which gives it a nice, light, airy feel. It reminds me of a ballet, delicate and bouncy. Then, in the final section, it returns to a groove with much gusto. As a former tuba player, I love the little solo given to the lower instruments near the end. Fittingly, “Lament 1, ‘Birds Lament’” gives much emphasis to the saxophones, a melancholic yet triumphant trading of melodies back and forth. I love the uneven rhythm laid underneath the soloing. It’s short and sweet, leaving quite the impression despite the runtime. “Witch of Endor” takes its time to build up before frinding to a halt and beginning anew. The straightforward rhythm contrasts with the swing we’ve heard up to this point. Again, I find myself picturing Medieval troops gathering before a battle. “Symphonique #1 (Portrait of a Monarch)” isn’t a bad track, but it’s not the most satisfying closer either. There are some interesting ideas but it fails to culminate into anything beyond what we’ve already heard.

This was a great record. Only 50 albums to go, and still I’m hearing music that sounds totally unique.

Favorite tracks: Theme, Stamping Ground, Symphonique #6 (Good for Goodie), Minisym #1, Lament 1, “Bird’s Lament”, Witch of Endor

Least favorite track: Symphonique #3 (Ode to Venus)
