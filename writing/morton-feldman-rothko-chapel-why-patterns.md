# Write-up

Morton Feldman was an American composer. Born to Russian Jewish emigres, he studied under two other Jewish emigres, Stefan Wolpe and Wallingford Riegger, the latter of whom was among the American pioneers of serialism, an approach to classical music composition based upon repeating patterns. The main figure in serialism was Arnold Schoenberg, whose 12 tone system was enormously influential in modern classical music. While stepping out of a performance of Rachmaninoff due to an unreceptive audience, Feldman met fellow composer John Cage, who would open doors for him in the New York City art scene. Feldman quickly became friends with Cage and moved in to the same building, and via Cage met figures including artists Robert Rauschenberg and Jackson Pollock, and composer Henry Cowell (allegedly NOT the inspiration for Henry Cow). Feldman also was a teacher a University of Buffalo, and titled his professorship after Edgard Varése, the avant-garde composer who Frank Zappa idolized as a teenager. Among his students was Joseph Bird, who would go on to found The United States of America, a pioneering psychedelic rock band. Feldman's earlier work experimented heavily with musical notation, but starting in the 1970s, he started using traditional notation in order to be able to control the rhythms in his work.

Mark Rothko was an American painter. He is best known for his later work, in which he would paint large works of few or single colors, which he called "multiforms". He saw his work as intensely spiritual and recommended that viewers position themselves within reach of the paintings so that they felt enveloped by the totality of the artwork. He was commissioned to create the artworks for the Rothko Chapel in 1964, by John and Dominique de Menil, members of the Schlumberger family whose fortune stems from SLB (formerly named Schlumberger), the largest offshore drilling company in the world. In 1968, Rothko's health began to suffer due to an aneurysm. His marriage dissolved, he was forced to use assistants while painting and he moved into his studio on the Upper East Side. In 1970, he killed himself. The Rothko Chapel was among his last works. He painted 14 paintings, 3 triptychs and 5 individual paintings, all in shades of black. These dark paintings are often associated with Rothko's worsening mental health, although some critics have disputed this association. The Rothko Chapel was opened in 1971 and at its opening, Feldman was commissioned to write the eponymous work to be played in the space. One of the melodies was first written when he was 15.

Rothko Chapel was performed by the UC Berkley Chamber Chorus, which includes William Winant, a percussionist who has worked with Sonic Youth/Thurston Moore, Mr. Bungle, Oingo Boingo, and Fred Frith of Henry Cow. "Why Patterns?" was performed by the California EAR Unit, a Los Angeles based chamber ensemble. One of its members, Arthur Javinen, was a frequent collaborator with Zappa and Beefheart

# Hargrove's review

I’ve been excited for this record because as far as I can tell, it’s the only truly classical music on the list. Yeah, yeah, Glenn Branca is kind of classical, and Popol Vuh if you squint. But Feldman was a contemporary of John Cage. That’s the real deal! Overall, I liked *Rothko Chapel* quite a bit, but I couldn’t get into *Why Patterns?* at all. Really, I’m just a little confused why this of all things was chosen as the token classical album. I know that very few classical composers have their music performed, recorded, and packaged into albums, but Steve Reich’s *Music for 18 Musicians* is right there. That’s an absolute banger! Terry Riley also has some cool stuff, particularly *In C*. But it wouldn’t be fair to blame *Rothko Chapel + Why Patterns?* for this list’s omission of *Music for 18 Musicians*. They’re not actually rivalrous goods. I do think it serves as a good point of comparison though. While Feldman wasn’t actually a minimalist per se, you can clearly see a lot of shared ideas. He makes use of silence quite extensively throughout both pieces, only inserting sound where it absolutely needs to be. I find this approach to minimalism (as a philosophy, not necessarily a genre) less rewarding than Reich’s, who sets glacial changes in dynamics atop walls of droning, unchanging sound. Maybe I just have ADHD, but that works better for me. Still, I found a way into *Rothko Chapel*, even if I couldn’t for *Why Patterns?*.

It took a while to get into, but I enjoyed *Rothko Chapel* eventually. It’s so eerie, just like a horror movie score, with the voices setting the tone, the percussion generating tension, and the viola cutting through like a knife when it wants to. At times, it sounds more like Eno ambient than it does classical music to me (well, the other way around, to be fair). “Rothko Chapel 1” is one of the better parts of the composition. I particularly like the tympani roll into the vocal crescendo near the end of this part. “Rothko Chapel 2” continues a lot of the same ideas, while “Rothko Chapel 3” hits a lull by focusing on only the vocals. The composition gets really good with “Rothko Chapel 4” once the viola takes over. It cuts through the silence with such power. The very best, however, was saved for last with “Rothko Chapel 5”. I liked this final segment so much that I added it to my normal liked songs playlist. It starts with a beautiful celesta that leads into the viola playing the clearest and prettiest melody so far. It’s just gorgeous. It’s almost Medieval sounding, or like high fantasy. It’s a super cool few minutes. Unfortunately, I just cannot crack *Why Patterns?*. It’s austere, cold, and impenetrable. I enjoy the flute, but I cannot for the life of me tell what I’m supposed to get out of the piano and the glockenspiel. I kind of understand what’s happening conceptually, the independent repetitions, but I don’t enjoy it much at all while listening.

I would place *Rothko Chapel?* on its own into low B Tier. *Why Patterns?* drags it into C Tier. Still, this was an awesome change of pace from the other stuff we’ve listened to. I continue to be amazed by the breadth of music on this list.

Favorite tracks: Rothko Chapel 1, Rothko Chapel 4, Rothko Chapel 5

Least favorite track: Why Patterns?

# Aidan's review

I imagine that this album is quite powerful in the right setting, but when I went to my nearest late period Rothko, I was unfortunately surrounded by too many chattering people to properly experience the album. Without the Rothko painting, I found it nicely contemplative, but even the most ambient albums I've heard have been more striking, either by using samples (Biosphere, Natural Snow Buildings), coming to some sort of crescendo(Stars of the Lid) or being more stark in their stylings (Eno, Basinski). It's not technically ambient, but it certainly feels that way with how restrained the entire effort is. This album feels like it's written more as a companion piece, and without that, it never manages to provoke a substantial emotional response from me.
