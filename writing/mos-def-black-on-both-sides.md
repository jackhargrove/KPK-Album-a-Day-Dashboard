# Write-up


# Hargrove's review

I had never listened to this album nor *Mos Def & Talib Kweli Are Black Star*. However, I was familiar with Mos Def because he was part of that conscious backpack rap crew who featured on Kanye West’s debut album *The College Dropout* in 2004. Artists like Mos Def, Talib Kweli, Common, and Consequence, all from New York, all late 90s descendants of the original late 80s/early 90s jazz rap Native Tongues alternative scene, all rapping about life on the streets but with a more aspirational bent than the revelry of the West Coast or the gritty horror of the mainstream East Coast, all building their instrumentals around soul samples reminiscent of the adjacent neo-soul scene, all friends with Dave Chappelle. This was very much its own scene by the late 90s, and pretty directly responsible for producing Kanye (particularly the high-pitched soul samples of his early “Chipmunk soul”). I have never particularly cared for this scene. I have only actively disliked Common, but all of them I have put into the same bucket of rappers that vaguely annoy me. There’s a certain combination of preachiness, woo-woo spirituality, and exaggerated scrappiness that just turns me off. And, look, I like Public Enemy. I do not mind being yelled at about politics, even radical politics I do not support. It’s just the nonchalance of these backpack conscious guys that communicates they see themselves not as activists or revolutionaries but as wizened old souls spitting the truth of the streets. I generally enjoy the soul-drenched instrumentals they choose, but it does get same-y after a while. Ironically, Kanye was the one that “saved” this scene for me by not taking himself too seriously (I know). He has a lot of the same guys back for *Late Registration*, and a few stuck around for *Graduation* (including Mos Def on “Drunk and Hot Girls”, which is…not a song you want to be associated with). Eventually, though, he left them all behind, marking the beginning of his middle period I so enjoy. As for Mos Def specifically, *Black on Both Sides* had a healthy number of moments that made me roll my eyes for many of the same reasons I outlined above. How he thinks the message expressed on “Love” can coexist with the venom he spits on “Rock ’N’ Roll” is beyond me. “New World Water” reminds me that there’s no feeling worse than being talked down to by a conspiracy theorist. And it’s honestly a shame because there are a few really good tracks on this thing. “Ms. Fat Booty” is my favorite new hip hop discovery I’ve made in a while. “UMI Says” sounds so lovely. The drum samples throughout the whole album are consistently so crisp. But there are just so many moments that make me roll my eyes, particularly on the album’s back half. Beyond just the stupid lyrics are the insanely repetitive; how many times does Mos Def just randomly shout out cardinal directions on this record? It feels like at least four. The 11th chopped up soul sample can only do so much.

“Fear not of men because men must die” is a very promising opening sentiment for an album, but unfortunately “Fear Not of Man” leaves me completely unsatisfied. I like the keyboard/organ thing in the instrumental, but I don’t enjoy the rambling flow, nor the stuttery drums, nor the De La Soul sunniness led by a rapper who bugs me. It’s not a strong start. “Hip-Hop” is a significant improvement over the opener. I like the descending bass line of the instrumental, the great flow, the fun vibe, and its tastefully short length. It also has some of the only lines from the album that stood out to me as particularly good, namely “Yo! We went from pickin' cotton to chain gang line choppin’/To Be-Boppin', to hip-hoppin’” and “A long way from the shell tops/And the bells that L rocked (Rock, rock, rock, rock…)”. It’s a decent history lesson that clearly comes from a place of love. Speaking of, “Love” is pretty good too. The hook is great, though, not any better than it was in its original Rakim track. The beat grooves along smoothly and the bridge gets stuck in my head. There are definitely worse tracks. The album’s best track, by far, is “Ms. Fat Booty”. With neither qualifications nor reservations, I will say proudly that this track rules. I like Mos Def better with this low-stakes storytelling style, and Ayatollah masterfully chops up the vocal sample Madlib style. “Speed Law” wraps up this decent run in the first quarter of the album. The beat is great, particularly the exotic guitar sample. The lyrics don’t do a ton for me, but there’s some great flow. It’s another decent track. “Do It Now” has a lot of promise, but it ends up doing too much and turns me off. The beat just has too much going on, Busta Rhymes’ verses are way too aggro, and the line-trading in the hook puts me on edge. I want to like this track, but it’s sensory overload. “Got” has a great instrumental courtesy of Ali Shaheed Muhammad, with a weird repeated synth line over orgasmic smoothness. Unfortunately, Mos Def spoils it with one of the laziest hooks I have ever heard. “Don't get me, don't get me/Don't g-g-g-g-g-get me” Are you fucking kidding me? The verses are whatever, but the hook annoys me so much every time and ruins the song. Thankfully, “UMI Says” follows to soothe my soul. It’s smooth and funky as hell. I love those drums and the psychedelic echoes over everything. It doesn’t sound like something you’d hear on a lot of hip hop albums.

If the album ended with “UMI Says”, I would still have my problems with it, but it would be a solid hip hop record, probably low B tier for me. Unfortunately, it stretches on to over 70 minutes, with some truly frustrating tracks along the way. The first of these is “New World Water”, which literally (*literally*) promotes the fluoride water conspiracy theory in its hook. And just to reiterate, I don’t even mind being lectured by those I disagree with politically in art (there are too many great socialist films for that). It’s being condescended to by a rank conspiracy theorist that drives me up a wall. The drums are stellar, but the rest of the instrumental is meh. What an annoying track. It’s not the worst, though. That award has to go to “Rock ’N’ Roll”. I am so sick of the endless debate over who “owns” rock music. Usually, I’m annoyed by Nazis on twitter who denigrate the black blues musicians that set the foundation for rock. The people on the white side of this argument are usually much crueler and poorly informed. However, Mos Def finally gives them a run for their money with the most mean-spirited, wrongheaded, and downright ignorant possible case one could make for the black side of the argument. Elvis Presley, the Rolling Stones, Korn, and Kenny G don’t have souls? I feel like we’re taking this a little far. And, you know, I think he might like the Stones if he ever gave them a listen. “John Coltrane is rock and roll”??? Jesse, what the fuck are you talking about? No he isn’t! And bringing Jimi Hendrix into it is so stupid! He stands out like a sore thumb compared to the early rock and roll and R&B musicians listed there. Jimi Hendrix came up in late 60s London as part of a very white scene and idolizing Bob Dylan. He’s a terrible example to use for your argument. This disasterpiece concludes perfectly with Mos Def shifting genres just to prove to you that he knows absolutely nothing whatsoever about rock music in case you had any doubt. What is this late 90s nu metal swill? I get really frustrated with this topic because the history of rock and roll is actually quite beautiful. It’s so awesome that when some radio stations desegregated their music in the early 50s, the combination of black R&B and white country-tinged rockabilly created a music genre and youth social movement that still reverberates 70 years later. It’s genuinely a beautiful testament to diversity and the idea of America. This could have been a really cool song that emphasized the forgotten black contributions to these traditions and ended with super cool genre-bending if it came from a place of love at all. Instead, Mos Def becomes the first non-Nazi I’ve seen handle this topic with the grace and care of HowlingMutant. This is a bottom 5 song of the entire project so far for me.

Well, this must be the first time that I’ve hated two subsequent songs so much I had to quarantine them to their own paragraph. Anyway, “Know That” comes in with some of the energy of the record’s first half. Yeah, it has another cardinal direction shoutout hook, but the verses are nice, as is the manipulation of the sample in the instrumental. It helps wash away some of the ashes from my mouth. “Climb” is a soul track in the vein of “UMI Says”, but it’s not anywhere near as good. It goes for a sparse sound over a lush one, which I think was a poor decision. “Brooklyn” opens with an interpolation of a Red Hot Chili Peppers song; ohhhhhhhhhh, interesting, who likes unoriginal white rockers now??? It’s just baffling to me that someone can be so clueless. That being said, this song is fine. The second section is my favorite. Its minimalist boom bap instrumental and no frills rapping is a refreshing change of pace. “Habitat” really just doesn’t need to exist. We just had a song about hometowns! We need another? I’ve lost all my charity at this point, but this song is stupid. Now, I do gotta hand it to Mos Def on “Mr. [REDACTED]”: “White folks gotta mumble the chorus beneath they breath (I didn't say it!)” is pretty damn funny. The second verse about the first class fight is good too. But then he has to go and fuck it up with the moronic third verse where he complains about OJ being persecuted. It’s nice to hear Tip’s voice on the hook, but I would love it if he actually had a verse. I like the piano in the instrumental, but this track is too long, and the lyrics too dumb. There’s only one song with lyrics left. What will “Mathematics” give us? Oh, it’s a list of conspiracy theories wrapped in a shell of numerology. The beat is also nothing special, I do not understand why this song was popular. The album does close on a high note with “May-December”. Maybe I like it because it’s a pretty, meditative closer that reminds me a lot of how Tyler, the Creator ended *Flower Boy*. Maybe it’s because Mos Def finally shut up. Who knows.

God, this album is a pain to rate. There are a few really good songs! I cannot emphasize enough how much I like “Ms. Fat Booty”. But it’s a long album, and the 11 tracks in not on my favorites list range from “fine” to “among the worst thing ever put on wax”. I think I have to put it next to *69 Love Songs*, a zone representing the most frustrating of artists: those with unbelievably high ceilings whose hubris leads them more often to their floors. I feel like my original plan was to put this in low C Tier, but I really worked myself into a tizzy writing about “Rock ’N’ Roll”.

Favorite tracks: Hip-Hop, Love, Ms. Fat Booty, Speed Law, UMI Says, Know That

Least favorite track: Rock ’N’ Roll

# Aidan's review
