# Write-up

Neil Young is a Canadian musician who has been a member of both Buffalo Springfield and Crosby Stills, Nash and Young in addition to a well acclaimed solo career backed by numerous bands, most notably Crazy Horse. Born in Canada, he began playing music on a ukulele in Winnipeg. He joined his first real band, the Squires, at age 18 after dropping out of high school. The band never released a full album, but it was there that Young met future collaborators, Ken Koblun, the Squires' bassist, and Stephen Stills, who was active in the same area. After the Squires, Young started his solo career, running into Joni Mitchell before joining Rick James in the Mynah Birds, along with Bruce Palmer. When Rick James was arrested for desertion (the reason he was in Canada), Palmer and Young made tracks for LA to try to make it big, illegally immigrating to America (someone call ICE!!!).

While in traffic in LA, Young ran into Stephen Stills again. Together with Palmer, Richie Furay and Dewie Martin, they formed Buffalo Springfield. Their first record together featured "For What It's Worth", written by Stills and catapulted the band into the spotlight, starting the entire genre of folk rock along the way. The band quickly began to break apart, as Palmer's drug usage got him arrested and deported, while Young wrote and recorded two of his songs on the second album independent of the rest of the group. The band churned out a third album to fulfill their label contract before breaking up. Young drifted onward, joining the Monkees in the studio before retiring to his solo career.

After a mediocre first album, Young recruited a backing band with Danny Whitten, Billy Talbot and Ralph Molina. All three had played together on a band called The Rockets before joining Young, but they were renamed Crazy Horse. Their first album together, Everyone Knows This is Nowhere, was a huge hit, with notable songs such as "Cinnamon Girl", "Cowgirl in the Sand" and "Down by the River". Young moved on to a new band again, but kept Crazy Horse waiting in the wings. This time, he rejoined Stephen Stills, who had formed a trio with David Crosby and Graham Nash, creatively named "Crosby, Stills & Nash". Young tacked his name onto the end, and "Crosby, Stills, Nash and Young" managed to stay together long enough to perform at Woodstock and put out the best selling album of either CSN or CSNY, Deja Vú. The band broke up after due to Stills' drug use and romance with actress Rita Coolidge, conflicts between Young and Stills and the tensions of constant touring. Each member went on to make their own solo album.

Young's solo album was "After the Gold Rush". Featuring Stills, Crazy Horse, E Street Band member Niles Lofgren and uncredited CSNY member Greg Reeves, the album was inspired by an unfilmed screenplay of the same name by two of Young's friends. Only the title track and "Cripple Creak Ferry" are directly related to the film, but once Young started writing, the album was written in 3 weeks. The recording process was mostly finished before CSNY broke up, but was released a month after the the breakup. The album features a very overexposed image of Young walking past an old lady in Greenwich Village. Critical reception was mixed at first upon its release in 1970, but it has since gone on to be listed among the best albums of all time.

Young would go on to follow up After the Gold Rush with Harvest, another smash hit of an album. His career through the 80s was scattered, he released some of his least popular music, but also one of his most critically acclaimed albums, Rush Never Sleeps. He produced and directed his own movie, got sued by his label, made experimental music, and support Reagan. CSNY would reunite several times, but didn't manage to put out a record as a quartet until 1988. Around the same time, Young put out "Rockin' In the Free World", which, combined with the rise of grunge musicians heavily inspired by his guitar playing style, revitalized his career. Nirvana, Pearl Jam, Sonic Youth, Nick Cave, The Pixies and Dinosaur Jr. were all fans and Young was cool again. In recent years, Young has become very active environmentally, releasing an album to promote a hybrid car, opposing the Keystone XL pipeline and protesting logging. He is also known for his dislike of compression on streaming services and has his own website that hosts all of his music with higher fidelity. He is still touring and recording and even released a song criticizing Trump's deployment of federal troops in DC in August.

# Hargrove's review

My dad loooooooooooves Neil Young, so I’ve absorbed a lot of his music through osmosis my entire life. Neil Young played at West Point on his Ragged Glory tour with openers Sonic Youth and Social Distortion when my dad was a cadet there. My dad loved both the openers, but thought Neil Young was boring old guy soft rock and intended to leave the concert when Sonic Youth finished. A friend convinced him to stay, and he’s been in love with Neil Young ever since. I think “My My, Hey Hey (Out of the Blue)” is one of the best songs I’ve ever heard, and “Cinnamon Girl”, “Down By the River”, “Heart of Gold”, “Old Man”, and “The Needle and the Damage Done” were all songs I liked. However, I had never listened to any Neil album other than *Everybody Knows This Is Nowhere* and I didn’t know a single song off of *After the Gold Rush*, which is pretty crazy. I was a little nonplussed by it at first, but with each successive listen (a great thing about these short albums is you can listen so many times) I came to really like it. There are a few songs that are transcendently good and very few that are even marginally bad. It never overstays its welcome. Young’s warble is an acquired taste, but he does a great job of writing songs that suit it well. And speaking of the songwriting, he just has such an amazing way with words. This is a singer-songwriter record that is an inch wide and a mile deep, providing so much to dig into from so little. It is definitely in the upper quartile of folksy singer-songwriter albums we’ve heard.

“Tell Me Why” is just fantastic. It stands up to how good “My My, Hey Hey” is. It’s so pretty. I love the guitar in the chorus as well as the vocals. And I don’t care if the lyrics are “meaningless”, they go together so well and they make me feel something, god damn it. Great, great track. I’m not crazy about “After the Gold Rush”. I think it’s a good song, but it doesn’t resonate with me like the rest of the album does. The lyrics do nothing for me and the instrumentation is so spars. Its best quality is its catchiness. “Only Love Can Break Your Heart”, on the other hand, is awesome. It is a beautiful little waltz with the message that loneliness isn’t causing you pain, it was actually the love. “Southern Man” is a banger and I love hearing that rock n roll after all the folk. The chorus is so intense, so fiery, so furious. “Till the Morning Comes” might be a little tonally inconsistent coming right after, but I don’t know, I like it. It’s a sweet ditty. “Oh, Lonesome Me” is certainly in the lower tier of this album, but that bridge is powerful enough for me to see some value in it. Young’s voice sounds just fantastic on “Don’t Let It Bring You Down”. His warble is perfect for this song. I also love the vivid urban imagery in the lyrics and the intense build into the chorus. “Birds” has a nice chorus, but overall it’s too sentimental and sappy for me. It’s my least favorite track. “When You Dance I Can Really Love” is a late rocker and I think it does its job well. “I Believe in You” is fine, but it drags a little bit as we approach the album’s end. “Cripple Creek Fairy” is fun and pretty but I unfortunately don’t think it sticks the landing. That being said, how do you end this album?

I liked this a lot. I have a lot I need to dig into with Neil. When I get the chance, he’ll be my next discog deep dive. It’s not perfect by any means, but it’s pretty damn good.

Favorite tracks: Tell Me Why, Only Love Can Break Your Heart, Southern Man, Till the Morning Comes, Don’t Let It Bring You Down, When You Dance I Can Really Love

Least favorite track: Birds

# Aidan's review

I've heard this record a billion times, and it's up there with my favorite singer-songwriter records. No individual song is that much better than the others, but it's a consistent album that feels incredibly well put together. I'd put it up there with the best of Dylan and Cohen. Neil Young's vocal ability to convey the fear of being unable to escape history is so compelling, be it the personal history of "Tell Me Why", the political history of "Southern Man" or the natural history of "After the Gold Rush". Throughout the album, Young comes across as someone who has just left the follies of youth behind and is now anxious and full of doubts if change is possible. Cripple Creak Ferry closes on a bit of a weak note, but after the rest of the album, I can forgive one meh song. I could listen to the title track for hours on end, the single line "Thinking about what a friend had said/I was hoping it was a lie" is just pure magic. All time favorite, went in loving it and didn't change my mind at all.
