# Write-up

The originators of the "motorik beat", NEU! was nominally a duo of Klaus Dinger and Michael Rother, but is best thought of as a trio with their producer Konrad "Conny" Plank. The duo got their start in Kraftwerk, a West German band that is now well known for its role as the progenitor of electronic music. Kraftwerk was founded in the late 1960s by Florian Schneider and Ralf Hütter, and made experimental music with a rotating cast of supporting musicians using normal instruments. During the recording of the first album, the original drummer, Andreas Hohmann left the group, and Dinger was brought in to play on one of the tracks. Rother joined the band soon after on guitar. After Hütter went back to college, the group began to break down, and soon Rother and Dinger struck it out on their own as NEU!.

Dinger is something of a character, having dropped out of school at 16, learned carpentry and learned drums on spare pieces of wood. He was something of a bohemian, having been homeless before joining Kraftwerk and lived in a commune when he started NEU!. He gave the band its name as a form of satire of pop art and the advertising which it drew inspiration from. Rother is far less of a public figure, despite stints playing with the Chili Peppers, Brian Eno and Steve Shelly of Sonic Youth. The more ambient pieces of the band are due to his influence. The band went in the studio with Plank, who had produced Kraftwerk and had built his own 56 track mixing deck. The three basically just messed around until they found something they liked. Dinger's major innovation was the "motorik beat" (he preferred "Apache beat"), a 4/4 endlessly repeated drumbeat which features on Hallogallo and Negativland. He also brought in a Japanese banjo, which can be heart on Negativland. While the album sold a meager 30,000 copies, the "motorik beat" it pioneered has become incredibly influential, becoming one of the trends that defined the broader German experimental music scene at the time, which had earned the joking moniker "krautrock".

The band went on to have the first ever remix on their second album, the creatively named NEU! 2, speeding up and slowing down on of their songs to create two new ones, in what was either a way to save money or a brilliant artistic innovation depending on which story of Dinger's you believe. Regardless, the remixes, Super 78 and Super 16 (named for their RPMs), became the theme songs for the martial arts film Master of the Flying Guillotine, and in turn got Super 16 featured in mega-fan Quentin Tarantino's Kill Bill. The band put out a third album, NEU! 75 to mixed reception before disbanding under tensions between its two members. Dinger released the unfinished fourth album (no points for guessing the name) in Japan in 1995 to the chagrin of Rother before the two reconciled and finished the album in 2001. That proved to be their last album, as the band was permanently ended by Dinger's death in 2008 from a heart attack.

# Hargrove's review

Krautrock is interesting. Obviously I got super into Can’s “Vitamin C” earlier this year. I like drums a whole lot, and it’s an entire genre known for its drum beats, so why shouldn’t I like it? This album is a classic of the genre that i had heard of, but never listened to. I think it’s only okay. The three tracks that are like actual music are quite interesting, but they have their flaws. The three tracks that are ambient experiments are really not that great. On my first two listens, I really didn’t fond much to like in the album at all. Yes, the motorik beat in “Hallogallo” and “Negativland” does drive the music forward constantly, but there’s something unsatisfying about how every other aspect of the music is aimless (with the exception of some parts of “Negativland”). It feels like a missed opportunity at times. Starting with my third listen, I started to appreciate “Hallogallo” more. There’s a lot of creativity here and the rhythmic focus is appreciated. But it’s hard to rank this album particularly high, and I don’t see myself returning to it much at all.

A big issue with “Hallogallo” is that the squishy-sounding guitar is kind of annoying. Otherwise, there are a lot of good elements. I like the space rock guitar that sounds like it could be in *2001*, and the beat is obviously very cool. It’s the most consistently driving, but more than anything else I love those cymbal crashes. As I said before, I do wish the instruments around the beat had more focus and purpose. However, I came to appreciate how they used the beat as a backbone around which to noodle. It’s a good track. “Weissensee” sounds the most like a conventional, non-krautrock song. It has the space rock guitar again, but combines it with a laid back vibe not found elsewhere. It’s decent. “Negativland” is alright but ultimately worse than “Hallogallo”. It’s one major advantage is that it has a guitar line as driving as the drums. However, a little after the first half, things take a turn for the worse. Then, near the end, it gets really fast again and it’s great. It’s just a very inconsistent track.

The ambient half of this album is subpar, and that those tracks are interspersed with the real ones makes this a difficult album to listen to. “Sonderangebot” is the worst offender. It’s a whole lot of not much. “Im Gluck” is better if only because the strings are a nicer texture. “Lieber Honig” is the best of the three. There’s something haunting about the vocals that always gripped me.

I enjoyed this and I’m glad I listened to it, but it just made me want to listen to more krautrock that was hopefully better. I need something a little more refined and more focused.

Favorite tracks: Hallogallo, Weissensee, Negativland

Least favorite track: Sonderangebot

# Aidan's review

Very weird album to review. I really love the motorik beat and Hallogallo is one of my favorite songs of all time. There's also five other songs on this album. Of them, Negativland is good, there's interesting parts of Weisseensee, and Lieber Honig is probably the least annoying of the remaining three. I don't think it's worth listening to any song besides Hallogallo, and that's how I've listened to it for the last 5 years. This is about as formalist as any record we've listened to yet, and the fact that formalist experimentation can create a song as groovy as Hallogallo is a small miracle. Later albums from them were more refined, but the 10 minutes of raw perfection here is something to behold.
