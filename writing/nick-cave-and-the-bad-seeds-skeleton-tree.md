# Write-up

Nick Cave and the Bad Seeds is a nominally Australian rock band formed in 1983. Its namesake and frontman, Nick Cave, was born in 1957 in rural Australia. He was introduced to literature and music at a young age and was a fan of progressive rock and Leonard Cohen. He briefly attended college before dropping out to pursue music. He started using heroin around that time, a habit that would last for the next two decades. When he was 19, his father was killed in a car accident. His mother told him while bailing him out of jail for burglary.

Cave joined his first band when he was in high school, initially called The Boys Next Door before changing its name to The Birthday Party some years later. They started out playing glam rock covers before shifting into the emerging punk rock scene, inspired by The Pop Group and The Fall. In 1980, the band moved to London, and two years later it moved to West Berlin and disbanded there in 1983, with Cave heading back to London.

In London, Cave formed a new band, keeping guitarist Mick Harvey from The Birthday Party. He added JG Thirlwell (who we listened to as Foetus), Blixa Bargeld, the frontman from pioneering experimental group Einstürzende Neubauten, and Barry Adamson, the bassist for punk group Magazine. It was a veritable all-star group and was initially intended to be just a backing group for a single Cave solo project. Their first name was Nick Cave and the Cavemen, before switching to Nick Cave and the Bad Seeds after 6 months, a reference to the last EP from The Birthday Party, The Bad Seed. Thirwell left during the recording sessions of the first album for his solo career, leaving the group as a quartet. They began to make waves with their fourth album, Your Funeral, My Trial, and cemented themselves as a popular underground band with their fifth, Tender Prey, which featured their most acclaimed song, "The Mercy Seat", about a prisoner awaiting the electric chair. They were featured in Wim Wender's film Wings of Desire soon after.

During the 90s, Cave began to try to quit heroin, and the band's sound shifted as a result, with Cave exploring piano driven sound on a few of the group's albums. 1993's Let Love In featured the track "Red Right Hand", which became famous decades later after becoming the theme song to Peaky Blinders. In 1997, after a breakup with fellow singer PJ Harvey, Cave put out The Boatman's Call, which saw him abandon his former style of dark topics for more personal songs about love and loss. It was a massive critical success and was the band's most successful album to date.

The 2000s saw Bargeld leave the band, leaving Cave and Harvey as the only two original members. Cave engaged in several collaborative projects and returned to his gothic rock sound, seeing continued critical acclaim. In 2009, Harvey left the band, ending 36 years of work with Cave.

Skeleton Tree was the 16th studio album of the group, recorded in 2014. Towards the end of the recording sessions, Cave's 15 year old son died in a fall. Cave dove into his work to deal with the loss, and rewrote several of the songs in order to reflect his grief. The album was the most sparse of his to date, featuring influences from electronica and ambient music. The album was extraordinarily well reviewed, and was the band's best selling album ever. Nick Cave and the Bad Seeds are still writing new music and performing, with their latest album being released in 2024.

# Hargrove's review

I have never been able to get into Nick Cave, and unfortunately eight listens to *Skeleton Tree* this week (and one listen to *Let Love In*) didn’t help one iota. I had actually listened to this album before only a few years after it came out. It was after my grandma died in 2019, a year in which I found much solace in the track “Real Death” from the Mount Eerie album *A Crow Looked at Me*. I had read that legendary alternative artist Nick Cave had released a similar album with *Skeleton Tree* in 2016 and I gave it a listen. It was not for me at all. I’ve also always kind of found “Red Right Hand” annoying. I don’t know, man. Cave’s gravelly, emphatic voice just doesn’t work for me at all. Though I like Leonard Cohen, I almost always consider “sounds like Leonard Cohen” to be a negative trait in a singer: Nick Cave, Tom Waits, Bill Callahan, etc. It’s just so difficult to make that kind of voice not sound hokey or forced. To make matters worse for *Skeleton Tree*, Cave pairs his vocals with a unique, distinctive atmosphere in the instrumental. I get precious little out of this atmosphere. It’s too minimal, too austere, too cold. I dislike the vocals and the instrumentals separately, and yet together they’re even less than the sum of their parts. And while there are a few decent one-liners on this album, I found the lyrics scattered and unfocused overall. I wanted Cave to pick a lane between metaphor and direct expression of grief, but instead he was all over the place. But what really made this album brutal for me to listen to was just how bad I felt about disliking it! It feels awful to listen to a man bare his soul and be completely unmoved. I dreaded pressing play on this album more than I have for any album in quite a while. I enjoyed “Girl in Amber” and “Anthrocene”; otherwise, I don’t care for this record at all.

“Jesus Alone” contains two very good lines in “You believe in God/But you get no special dispensation for this belief now” and “You’re a distant memory in the mind of your creator. Outside of that, I found the lyrics an impossible nut to crack and, frankly, not rewarding to decipher. The instrumental’s empty and the vocal melody emptier. It’s not a great start. “Ring of Saturn” has “I thought slavery had been abolished/How come it's gone and reared its ugly head again?”, but I like the track as a whole even less than the opener. The little oh oh oh oh oh oh in the background just sounds silly. It’s my second least favorite on the album after “Distant Sky”. “Girl in Amber” is, by far, the best execution of this album’s ideas. I like how much less gravitas Cave displays in his vocals. He sounds so desperate, especially when his vocals outpace the instrumental to the point of becoming off-beat. I also appreciate the powerful swells in the string for the final section, and the line “I used to think that when you died you kind of wandered the world/In a slumber till you crumbled, were absorbed into the earth/Well, I don't think that any more” destroys me. The title of “Magneto” drives me crazy. Why is it named after the X-Men guy? More importantly, though I like the concept of taking a downbeat folk song and backing it with nonstop tape sounds, I just feel nothing while listening to it. “Anthrocene” works for me because of the frenetic instrumentation. I like the piano chords that don’t seem to follow any sense of time or rhythm. They’re impressionistic, and the atmosphere is apocalyptic. There’s an almost free jazz quality to the chaos of the instrumental. “I Need You” certainly has an impassioned vocal performance from Cave, but it sounds awful to me. He sounds creaky and out of breath. I strongly dislike “Distant Sky”. The ping-ponging back and forth between Cave’s growl and the female soprano gives me whiplash. I find neither pleasant to listen to. And the instrumental is soooooooooo sloooooooooow. “Skeleton Tree” isn’t the greatest song in the world, but it’s better than much of what precedes. The piano sounds pretty and the chords are nice. The line “I call out, I call out/Right across the sea/But the echo comes back empty” stands out to me. Still, my feelings toward it are more tolerance than enjoyment.

I was hoping to avoid this result, but *Skeleton Tree* goes straight to D Tier. Low D Tier, too. Maybe one day I’ll see what other people see in Nick Cave. Today is not that day. I even found my one listen to *Let Love In* grating.

Favorite tracks: Girl in Amber, Anthrocene

Least favorite track: Distant Sky

# Aidan's review

This is the third Nick Cave album I've listened to due to variants of this list. Let Love In and Tender Prey were on previous versions of this list. Neither really worked for me, I liked the title song on Let Love In and The Mercy Seat off of Tender Prey, but the entire work never clicked for me. Both of theses are from when Cave was young, full of vim and vigor, and happy to sing songs about Satan, electric chairs and bloody murder. In the 2000s, he found a softer side of himself, and started singing love songs, which were far more commercially successful. I listened to a few of those singles, and they didn't click either. This album clicks for me. In the wake of the death of his son, Cave finds a synthesis between the morbidity of his youth and the affection of his middle age. His voice is wonderful, full of emotion and shockingly mortal. I've listened to a fair bit of Sinatra during the break, and while Ol' Blue Eyes is wonderful, his perfection would leave an album like this feeling hollow. My two favorite lines on this album come as near-asides after long chants, as if Cave finds some kind of inner truth after all the ritual has passed. When Cave cries out "one more time with feeling" on "Magneto" and "when you're staring in the aisle" on "I Need You", he manages to put mountains of grief into the most normal of moments. They're anodyne, in both senses of the word. His repetition of "love" on "Magneto" also reminds me of Leonard Cohen's fantastic "Lover, Lover, Lover" in how both repeat the word so much as to reduce a word infused with nearly every work of art in history into a robotic syllable and act. The backing instrumentals are minimal, but impressive in how Cave manages to weave in and out of the beats. Possibly the most emotionally powerful album I've heard on this list and I'm curious how Blackstar compares to it. There's some overt similarities to Age of Naples as well, an album I did not really appreciate and this is so good that I'll probably go back to Age of Naples once we finish the list.
