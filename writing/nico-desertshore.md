# Write-up

Christa Päffgen, better known as Nico, was a German model, actress and singer. She was born in Cologne during World War II. Her father was a member of a storied family of brewers, but died during the war. After the war, she moved to Berlin with her mother, who found work as a seamstress. At age 13, Nico dropped out of school to sell lingerie. At age 16, she was discovered by a photographer, Herbert Tobias and given the stage name "Nico" after a (male) filmmaker that Tobias was in love with. Her first real role was in Fellini's La Dolce Vita. Around the same time, she moved to New York, living with the same Nico who Tobias has named her after. She managed to land a leading role in a minor French film and model for the cover of a Bill Evans album during that time. She then made inroads into the American movie scene, managing to befriend Brian Jones and record with a pre-fame Jimmy Page. Through Jones, she met Andy Warhol, who foisted her upon the Velvet Underground for their first album. The band did not like working with her, especially Lou Reed and after their first album sold poorly, they stopped working together and dropped Warhol as a manager.

She began working as a solo artist, singing in the now defunct club "The Dom", a block north of McSorley's (back when women were still forbidden). Her first album, Chelsea Girl, featured contributions from Reed, Cale and Morrison from the Velvets and was entirely composed of cover songs. Her cover of Jackson Browne's "These Days" has become her best known song. Nico had little control over the instrumentation and was unhappy with the final product, specifically taking issue with the presence of flute instrumentals, and finding the whole thing overdone. After the album was released, she spent time with Jim Morrison, who encouraged her to write her own songs. Her second album, The Marble Index, saw her doing exactly that, and was centered around her playing the harmonium, which became her signature instrument. The other best known harmonium player is John Lennon, who used the instrument on songs including "We Can Work It Out", "Being for the Benefit of Mr. Kite" and "Day in the Life". The Marble Index was arranged by Cale of the Velvets and has been described as "the ultimate heroin album". While it was released to little notice, it has come to heavily influence gothic rock and garner fans including Sharon Van Etten, Elliot Smith and Xiu Xiu. In addition to starting to write her own songs, Nico also changed her appearance, dying her hair red instead of blonde or her natural brown, and wearing a style that would heavily inspire goths.

Desertshore took much of the same inspiration as The Marble Index, and the two have retrospectively been viewed as a trilogy, completed by Nico's fourth album, The End... Cale co-produced the album along with seasoned producer Joe Boyd. All three albums were released to muted praise and have only found fandoms later. Desertshore in particular has been appreciated by British industrial music group Throbbing Gristle (of which Coil was an offshoot) and recorded their own "re-interpretation" of the album. Bjork has also covered "Le petit chevalier". She released The End... in 1974, releasing two more albums, one in '81 and one in '84.

Despite working with the infamously dragon-chasing Velvets, Nico only started using heroin in the 70s, but was a heavy user for most of her life. She also had some regressive views, calling herself a "Nazi anarchist junkie" and several of her associates have mentioned her being casually racist. When she said that she did not like blacks and compared them to animals, she was dropped by one album. In her mid 40s, she attempted to quit heroin through methadone therapy and exercise. While biking on Ibiza, she fell off her bike and hit her head, causing a cerebral hemmorage that killed her at the age of 49. "Mütterlein" was played at her funeral.

# Hargrove's review

228 albums in and this list still occasionally introduces me to music that sounds like nothing else I’ve ever heard. Of Nico’s three tracks on *The Velvet Underground & Nico*, the creepy, trance-like, ritualistic “All Tomorrow’s Parties” is easily my favorite. On *Desertshore*, Nico (and the Velvet Underground’s John Cale) isolate exactly what made that song so unsettling and expand it to album length. They correctly identify that Nico’s austere, cold, and unmistakably European vocals combine better with Medieval classical music than they do American rock or folk. In so doing, they tapped into a feeling of ancient horror that can only emanate from the forests of the Brothers Grimm, similar to the fairy tale terror of Norwegian mountains in that Ulver record we listened to. This album is fucking scary, man. The harmonium drones create this thick, unrelenting atmosphere that’s more sopping wet comforter than warm blanket. I was not kidding when I said it freaked me out to listen to it while I walked around at night! The album only lets up twice, first with the thinner a cappella of “My Only Child” and later with the melancholia of “Afraid”. The former is my least favorite track, while the latter shines individually despite how out of place it feels on the album. Otherwise, the album keeps its monolithic nature throughout. I was skeptical going in that a folk album could truly have inspired the swirling post-punk atmospheres of gothic rock, but no longer. This was a cool, alien listen. It’s not one that will enter my heavy rotation, but certainly one that I will continue to think about.

*Desertshore* throws you into the deep end immediately with “Janitor of Lunacy”, a terrifying entryway into the album’s sound. Nico’s songwriting shines here, providing one of the stronger melodic lines. The performances are great as well, both Nico’s severe vocal performance and Cale’s tidal wave of vibrato drones. “The Falconers” is my favorite track on the album. The instrumentation feels more dynamic and impressionistic than the droning opener, flitting around at the drop of a hat. Though the piano section near the end creates some respite, this is otherwise the album’s scariest track. “My Only Child” is undeniably creepy, and I like the chorus, but I sincerely miss the thickness of the instrumentation, resulting in it being my least favorite track. On the other hand, the voice of a child in “La Petit Chevalier” notches up the scary factor enough on its own to make this short interlude one of my favorite tracks. What can I say, I’m a sucker for French. Next, we have somehow the second track on this list to be titled “Abschied”. Cale’s viola steals the show here, creating a nice counterpoint to Nico’s vocals. I also appreciate the introduction of German lyrics. “Afraid” contains the best lyrics on the album, particularly the line “You are beautiful, and you are alone“. It also has a relatively conventional song structure and an atmosphere more melancholic than haunting. It’s a very good song so I’m putting it on my favorites, but I do unfortunately think it disrupts the flow of the album. “Mütterlein” brings back the horror movie atmosphere in spades; those trumpets at the beginning sound like they herald the entrance to Hell. It’s the best track since “The Falconer”. “All That Is My Own” ends the album on an as yet unexplored Medieval feeling, that of the festival or fair(e). However, I don’t get the sense that we’re reveling in something positive. It’s a good track, but I find the abrupt fade out ending unsatisfying.

This album is cool enough to justify the top half of B Tier, but I don’t see myself returning to it enough to justify A Tier or higher. I’m very happy to have added this album to my sonic lexicon, though.

Favorite tracks: Janitor of Lunacy, The Falconer, Le Petit Chevalier, Afraid, Mütterlein

Least favorite track: My Only Child

# Aidan's review

I can see why Throbbing Gristle was so into this track because the first track sounds incredibly like parts of the album we listened to from their offshoot, Coil. The dark atmosphere and the exotic, religious singing are present in both, and both are pretty successful at establishing an atmosphere. There are differences, Desertshore is a far more modest album and doesn't take the experimentalism nearly as far as Age of Naples. Nico's voice is tremendous, the instrumentations are interesting and the German parts add just enough edge to make it feel sinister. Still, the end result isn't captivating to make me appreciate it as a full album and there's no individual song I liked that much. Not bad, and doing some cool stuff, but ultimately I'll probably forget it.
