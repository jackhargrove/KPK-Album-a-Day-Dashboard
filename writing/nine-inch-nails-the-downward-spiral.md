# Write-up

Nine Inch Nails is the (largely) solo vehicle of industrial singer-songwriter Trent Reznor founded in Cleveland in 1988. Reznor was born in 1965 and raised by his grandmother in a deeply rural part of Pennsylvania. He was always musically gifted, but an Eagles concert in the 70s inspired him to try to become a rock star. After a year in college 90 miles outside Pittsburgh, Reznor dropped out in 1985 and moved to the big city: Cleveland, Ohio. While there, he played in multiple local new wave and synthpop bands. He began working as a janitor at Right Track Studio, where the owner allowed him to use studio equipment at odd hours to record demos. Dissatisfied with the quality of musicians he had worked with, Reznor decided to start a solo project where he would play every instrument. He called it Nine Inch Nails, largely for the symmetry in its initials, and created a logo based off of the font on the cover of *Remain in Light*. Reznor’s biggest influences included synthpop artists like Depeche Mode and Gary Numan, gothic rock groups like Joy Division and the Cure, and especially artists under the industrial umbrella like Throbbing Gristle, Coil, and Skinny Puppy, the last of whom had just popularized the industrial/dance fusion style of electro-industrial. NIN’s early songs were largely based on Skinny Puppy’s style, but with catchier hooks and a rock verse-chorus structure. After Reznor’s demos attracted numerous label offers, NIN released its debut studio album *Pretty Hate Machine* in 1989. Multiple tracks became surprisingly large hits, especially “Head Like a Hole”, and the record went platinum within a couple years. Both the music videos and live performances that followed established Reznor’s reputation for pushing boundaries. NIN includes touring musicians when playing live, changing the sound of the songs from Reznor’s solo arrangements on studio albums. After a protracted label dispute, NIN moved to Interscope in 1992. That same year, Reznor moved into and built a studio in the house in Los Angeles where Charles Manson murdered Sharon Tate in 1969. NIN released the EP *Broken* in 1992 featuring a more rock-oriented sound inspired by NIN’s live performances as well as industrial metal groups like Ministry and Godflesh. *Broken* was also a decent success and ended up winning two Grammys.

NIN continued this rock-oriented sound with their second album, 1994’s *The Downward Spiral*. Also recorded at Sharon Tate’s house, *The Downward Spiral* incorporated musical influences (and occasionally collaboration) from a murder’s row of art rockers, primarily *Low*-era Bowie, *The Wall*-era Pink Floyd, and Adrian Belew of King Crimson and Talking Heads, the last of whom plays guitar on the album’s opening track. The album’s concept follows the semi-autobiographical narrator as he allows his life to become consumed by hatred and self-destruction before potentially committing suicide. The lyrics were unprecedentedly explicit, sexual, and violent for an act on the cusp of the mainstream. The album went quadruple platinum, boosted by a legendary performance at Woodstock ’94 in which NIN played while covered head to toe in mud. It also received critical acclaim, though not without numerous detractors. Peter Christopherson of Throbbing Gristle and Coil directed multiple music videos for the album that were banned from MTV, while Coil, Aphex Twin, Foetus, Rick Rubin, and more contributed tracks to the remix album *Further Down the Spiral*. Country legend Johnny Cash would famously cover the closer “Hurt” on his final album before his death in 2003. Shortly after the record’s release, Sharon Tate’s house was demolished for good. Today, *The Downward Spiral* is generally considered NIN’s magnum opus.

After *The Downward Spiral* and Woodstock ’94, NIN was one of the most popular music artists in the world. It popularized the genre of industrial rock (and, for the first time, industrial as a whole), filling the void in alternative rock in the mid-90s now that grunge was no longer fresh and inspiring legions of copycats like Marilyn Manson with diminishing returns. Among Reznor’s fans was David Bowie, who considered NIN a modern Velvet Underground. The two embarked on a double headline tour in 1995. Reznor’s personal life, however, was a complete mess. He had been suffering from severe depression, drug addiction, and suicidal ideation for some time. After a long delay, he released NIN’s third album, the double album *The Fragile*. Though it polarized critics upon release, it is now viewed as a competitor for his greatest album. Reznor hit rock bottom in 2000 while touring the album when he overdosed on heroin. Since then, he’s completely gotten his shit together and is living a happy, healthy life. As of 2026, NIN has released 16 studio albums. In the 21st century, Reznor has perhaps become better known for his second career as a film soundtrack composer. He took Hollywood by storm with his original score for David Fincher’s *The Social Network* (2010), which is rightfully considered one of the greatest film scores of all time. The score was a collaboration with Atticus Ross, a British musician who became the second official member of NIN in 2006. Since then, Reznor and Ross have composed scores for Fincher’s films and others, winning 2 Oscars for Best Original Score for *The Social Network* and Pixar’s *Soul* (NOTE: Trent Reznor having two Oscars is pretty batshit. Oscars are the hardest major award to win. Having multiple puts you in rarefied air). Reznor got married in 2009 and has 5 children he’s raising in Beverly Hills. NIN were inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 2020 and are still touring. In fact, I am seeing them in DC next month and I cannot wait.

While this list has no shortage of industrial rock and metal albums, NIN (alongside Coil) is one of the only artists involved in earlier industrial styles. Industrial as a music discipline began in late 70s London as a sister style to the first wave punk scene. It was spearheaded by British group Throbbing Gristle, who used electronic instruments and sampling to capture the bleakness of dying, post-industrial Britain. Early industrial was highly conceptual, favoring atmosphere over musicality. Industrial exploded in the underground across the UK and Europe, with other significant early names being Britain’s Nurse With Wound and Germany’s Einstürzende Neubauten. The scene quickly gave way to post-industrial in the early 80s, which incorporated the spirit of industrial into a diverse array of musical sounds. Another growing electronic scene at that time was synthpop. In Belgium, artists combined industrial with synthpop to create electronic body music (EBM). EBM is among the harshest electronic dance genres, combining mechanical, metallic synth melodies with pounding bass drums and apocalyptic atmosphere. EBM left a major legacy in Europe, eventually spawning two major EDM supergenres (hardcore (rave) and trance). In the mid-to-late 80s, Canadian band Skinny Puppy took EBM away from dance and back toward synthpop and industrial to create electro-industrial, while Killing Joke, Ministry, Swans, Foetus, Godflesh, Big Black, and others introduced guitars to create the overlapping genres of industrial rock and industrial metal. NIN used electro-industrial and industrial rock/metal to finally break industrial into the mainstream in the 90s as part of the alternative rock scene that did the same for punk. Nowadays, industrial’s DNA can be found everywhere, from the industrial hip hop of Death Grips and Kanye West’s *Yeezus*, to the deconstructed club scene that birthed hyperpop, to the dark synthpop of Lady Gaga and the Weeknd.

# Hargrove's review

I love this album. It gets better every single time I listen to it. Unbeknownst to me, my first introduction to NIИ was at very young age through Weird Al’s “The Alternative Polka”, which features “Closer” as one of the songs in its medley. The melody and lyrics always stuck with me, as did a season 4 episode of HIMYM where Ted’s sister sells his TV to see NIИ in Spain, but I never checked out the music until 2024. That year, I got really into *The Downward Spiral* for a few weeks; Gotti, Carms, and McGary might recall me playing “Closer” multiple times during our first trip to Homecoming which landed right in the middle of that period. Unfortunately, when we started this project not long after, I had to stop listening to a newfound album I loved for nearly a year. Now that I’ve had the chance to return to it, I love it even more than I did back then. First of all, the consistency is remarkable. I said this exact same thing last week for both *In Rainbows* and *Madvillainy*, but it’s true. It’s not my fault we’ve had three albums with extremely high floors in a row. There’s just no bad song on this thing. And though “Closer” and “Hurt” are clear standouts, every last song fights for your attention and memory. This album is positively lousy with catchy hooks, earworm melodies, unforgettable beats, and climactic moments. It almost gives the impression of a singles compilation more than an album. That “almost”, however, leads into point two: *The Downward Spiral* exemplifies the successful concept album. As a pure narrative storyline, I don’t think it’s as cohesive as people make it out to be. But as a meditation on the darkest parts of human nature, the most damaging intrusive thoughts, the utter destruction that nihilism and misanthropy wreak, it’s focused and second-to-none. “The Downward Spiral” and “Hurt” fulfill the only conclusion possible to what “Mr. Self Destruct” started. And yet, among all the muck and mire, *The Downward Spiral* contains multiple of the most staggeringly beautiful moments in all of moments: the malfunctioning drum breakdown at the end of “Piggy”, the extended smoldering instrumental outro of “Closer”, the doomed acoustic strumming of “The Becoming”, the short and unresolved piano melody of “I Do Not Want This”, the uncertain respite of “A Warm Place”, the hidden tenderness of the chorus on “Reptile”, the culmination of the album’s melodic motif in “The Downward Spiral”, and, of course, the unbearably loud guitar that underscores our protagonist’s final lament on “Hurt”. Few albums have even one moment that moves me as much as these do. Perhaps it’s in spite of the grotesqueness and cold machinery that Reznor finds these stark, human moments of transcendence, but I don’t think so. More than anything, this album provides intense catharsis. In probing these outer depths, Reznor’s honesty and vulnerability finds humanity where there should be none. And doesn’t it make you feel better?

“Mr. Self Destruct” establishes the nature of this album both musically and conceptually. I enjoy the rough synth and the ending guitar solo, but on the whole I find it more impressive as a set piece than as a song, so it’s not going on my favorites. “Piggy” begins soft and slow, injecting the first super catchy pop hook: “Nothing can stop me now ‘cause I don’t care anymore”. While that alone would be enough for me, it takes things to the next level on its second half with the chaotic drum machine and the introduction of the recurring piano motif. “Heresy” brings in an even better hook with “God is dead and no one cares/If there’s a hell, I’ll see you there”. Reznor strains his vocals in a way that makes them some of my favorite on the album. The blazingly fast “March of the Pigs” drives an epipen straight into your heart. I love the off-kilter rhythm, the chainsaw guitars, and the anti-climax of the chorus. There’s palpable sarcasm dripping off the commercial jingle “Now doesn’t it make you feel better?”, but screaming out “I want to break it up/I want to smash it up/I want to fuck it up/I want to watch it come down” indicates to me that it might be more sincere than it initially lets on. No song in the universe makes me feel more alive than “Closer”. What an absolute banger. That sleazy, funky bass line goes from Prince to the Prince of Darkness, slinking around like a parasitic worm about to permafuck my brain. The ascending flute-like synth on the second chorus is also a nice touch. Lyrically, “Closer” is full of bangers, from “I've got no soul to sell” to You can have my isolation/You can have the hate that it brings/You can have my absence of faith/You can have my everything” to the “Help me” refrain. And then there’s the extended outro, smoldering and denying any release. It’s pretty much a perfect song. On “Ruiner”, I love the frantic digital synth melody with arpeggios and the drone over the chorus. The ultra low-mixed vocals on the pre-chorus are a nice texture as well. Claustrophobia reaches new heights on the terrifying and mechanical “The Becoming”. Those screaming samples in the background make me feel like I’m being closed in on, lost in a stampede. The bridge introduces the album’s first acoustic moment, though a sense of impending doom overshadows the entire section when you know it will soon be drowned out by clanging machines. This track mixes the human with the mechanical quite well.

“I Do Not Want This” begins the subtler, more restrained second half (“Big Man with a Gun” excepted). It contains some of my favorite lyrics and vocal deliveries from Reznor.;“I want to know everything/I want to be everywhere/I want to fuck everyone in the world/I want to do something that matters” is literally ME. Reznor shows heartbreaking vulnerability with the “I do not want this” pre-chorus, only to put his defenses back up with the screaming of “And don’t you tell me how I feel”, as if embarrassed. The ominous repeated melody is also a favorite of mine. I don’t hate “Big Man with a Gun” by any means, but it feels a little too overt and on-the-nose in its messaging for my taste. Now, “maladjusted men convert sexual frustration into fantasies of extreme violence” has aged tremendously well as a thesis, but I would’ve liked it to be a little less obvious. Thankfully, “A Warm Place” provides significant contrast as a deeply pretty instrumental. It reminds me a lot of Reznor’s film scores. It’s hazy, ethereal, and dreamy while still being ominous and dark. Every time, I’m taken aback by the booming, sweeping, repetitive breakbeat of “Eraser”. It builds instrumentally for over 3 minutes into a hulking tidal wave of sound before breaking on the shore immediately when the vocals come in. The machinery of “Reptile” puts the “industrial” in “industrial rock”. Between the pure bile of the verses is a shockingly gorgeous (and catchy) chorus: “Oh, my beautiful liar/Oh, my precious whore/My disease, my infection/I am so impure”. The bangers just do not stop. “The Downward Spiral” is another one that sounds like one of Reznor’s later film scores. It’s always a delight when this album has an acoustic guitar. The spoken word part reminds me of Slint,  and “A lifetime of fucking things up fixed in one determined flash” is a great line. “Hurt” is an all-time album closer, achieving the nearly impossible task of wrapping every intense emotion from the album into a satisfying, tidy conclusion. That tritone chord is jarring every time it’s played, complimenting Reznor’s haunting vocal delivery. The tribal drums with the sad piano melody pick things up nicely on the chorus. “You could have it all/My empire of dirt/I will let you down/I will make you hurt” deserves the plaudits it receives, but “If I could start again/A million miles away/I would keep myself/I would find a way” kills me every single time. What an absolutely tragic sentiment. Where does he muster up this kind of conviction at the end of this journey? It reminds me a lot of how *Pornography* ends with “I must fight this sickness/Find a cure/I must fight this sickness”. It genuinely makes me tear up.  I don’t even know how he makes the guitar over the ending so loud. It just shatters me.

I’m sad to have to put this one down for the time being. *Pretty Hate Machine* is also quite good, though nowhere close to this one. I still need to listen to *The Fragile*. I cannot wait to see NIИ in concert next month.

Favorite tracks: Piggy, Heresy, March of the Pigs, Closer, Ruiner, The Becoming, I Do Not Want This, A Warm Place, Eraser, Reptile, The Downward Spiral, Hurt

Least favorite track: Big Man with a Gun

# Aidan's review

I find it hard to rate this album because I simultaneously find Trent Reznor's shtick annoying but find his beats at times incredibly good. Mr. Self Destruct is fantastic song, and pretty much any song that even comes close to that energy is incredibly enjoyable. On the other hand, the more conventional Closer gives too much focus to the far less interesting lyrics. It was a good rule of thumb going through this album that if I found myself easily discerning the lyrics, I was annoyed. Thankfully, the mixing does the smart thing and puts the amazing beats forward on most of the songs, Hurt is a bit of a weird song in that I think the Cash version is far better, but I don't mind what Reznor does with it and I think it's a fitting closing to an album. Far exceeds my expectation of what was selling out stadiums in 94, and makes me wish we had more industrial dance albums on here besides this (unless you count Yeezus).
