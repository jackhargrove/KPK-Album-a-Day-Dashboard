# Write-up

Oingo Boingo was a new wave band formed in LA in 1979. Its lineup at the time of its debut album *Only a Lad* consisted of vocalist, rhythm guitarist, and songwriter Danny Elfman, lead guitarist Steve Bartek, keyboardist and trombonist Richard Gibbs, bassist Kerry Hatch, drummer Johnny Hernandez, tenor saxophonist Sam “Sluggo” Phipps, baritone saxophonist Leon Schneiderman, and trumpeter Dale Turner. The band traces its origins to The Mystic Knights of the Oingo Boingo, a surrealist theater troupe founded by Richard Elfman in LA in 1972. Inspired by Frank Zappa, the troupe played songs from 1890-1950 while in full clown makeup and became local celebrities in LA. Richard Elfman grew bored of the troupe by 1976, so he handed control over to his brother Danny (henceforth “Elfman”). Elfman had previously dropped out of high school (where he dated classmate Kim Gordon of Sonic Youth) to tour Africa and learn drums. Elfman took the troupe to new heights artistically, turning them into a funhouse mirror view of the early 20th century and culminating in a film made in 1978. That year, Elfman began incorporating elements of punk, new wave, and ska into the group’s repertoire, ultimately writing original songs for the first time. He disbanded the troupe in 1979 to form Oingo Boingo as a rock band, with many musicians holding over from the original troupe.

The new Oingo Boingo octet dropped an EP of ska-influenced new wave in 1980; a track from the EP, “Only a Lad”, became a minor hit on LA’s KROQ, one of the first new wave radio stations in the US. In response, Oingo Boingo re-recorded “Only a Lad” and released its debut album of the same name in 1981. *Only a Lad* features quintessential new wave elements, particularly complex herky-jerky rhythms and prominent ska elements (the 2 tone genre further mixes ska with new wave). The album’s compositional techniques also reflect Elfman’s background in classical and jazz. The lyrics are comedic and intentionally provocative, particularly the *Lolita*-esque first-person pedophile narrative of “Little Girls”, as well as Elfman’s jabs at the heavily socialist punk scene in “Perfect System” and “Capitalism”. “You Really Got Me” is a cover of a famous song by the Kinks. The album was a major regional hit in the LA area, though many critics did not care for it.

Though this debut was only a modest success, Oingo Boingo would become quite famous by the mid-80s. Its next three albums, all in the same style as *Only a Lad*, were commercial failures. 1985’s *Dead Man’s Party*, however, shifted to a more pop sound and proved a major breakthrough. It contained the track “Weird Science”, associated with the hit film of the same name, which cracked the top 50 on the Billboard Hot 100. In addition to making Oingo Boingo famous, this began Elfman’s storied career as a film composer. He has written countless scores for movies you’ve scene, from *Men in Black* to *Mission:Impossible* to *Good Will Hunting* to the theme song for *The Simpsons*; however, he is most known for his collaborations with Tim Burton, whose movies he has scored for four decades. Oingo Boingo released three more albums, but Elfman slowly lost his passion for the band as he scored more and more films. His efforts for the 1993 film *The Nightmare Before Christmas* proved particularly taxing, precipitating the band’s dissolution on Halloween 1995. Elfman is still active today as one of the most well-known film scorers in history, a classical composer, and the drummer for A$AP Rocky’s 2025 SNL appearance.

# Hargrove's review

This is a really fun listen! I was familiar with Elfman’s film scores and knew that he had previously been in a band called Oingo Boingo, but I had never listened, nor did I even really know what they sounded like. Well, it’s the 80s-est sounding album ever made. Those cheesy synths, stuttering rhythms, mercurial vocals, and triumphant ska horns evoke the early 80s to me as strongly as Britney does the early 00s. I could see that being a nonstarter for some, but I do have a certain fascination with the decade, one that feels completely out of step with the 60s, 70s, and 90s. The album keeps things tight at 10 songs in under 40 minutes, a decision I found very prudent for this style and intensity of music. The terrifyingly catchy “Little Girls” builds a lot of tension right at the start, and the album does a great job of holding that tension tight for the remainder. There are just so many fun songs on this thing without much of a lull or breather at any point. I found it an enjoyable listen every time through, and have added six tracks to my liked songs playlist. And, of course, I was enamored by the sneering at socialists. Look, this album does have a ceiling for me given its singular, unrelenting aesthetic, but I had a lot more fun with it than I expected to. It’s a frequently funny and raucous good time.

“Little Girls” is a genuinely terrifying, super creepy song that works because it’s so catchy and upbeat. It would freak me out so much less if it were slow or overtly macabre. Instead, it leaves me agape as it (pretty accurately) details the thought process of a pedophile over the bounciest beat I’ve ever heard. The “the little girl was just too little/Too little, too little, too little, too little” section actually gives me chills. Cool track. “Perfect System” keeps up the tension, but it’s my least favorite of the first six tracks. I like Elfman’s vocals and the horns in the 7/4 chorus quite a bit, but the lyrics are a little too obvious for my taste. The following track, “On the Outside”, nails the lyrics though. The “I'm just an alien through and through/Tryin' to make believe I'm you” hits me every time and is a nice inversion of the expected trope of an outsider who enjoys being an outsider. I also like the line “I never was a punk, I never shot junk/I never even tried, counterculture passed me right by” because I’m a sucker for being an outsider from the outsiders. Musically, it’s got a slick 80s pop melody but over bizarro instrumentals. “Capitalism” is TRUE and GOOD and a BANGER. “There's nothing wrong with the capitalism/There's nothing wrong with free enterprise” FUCK YEAH DANNY. ““There's nothing wrong with making some profit/If you ask me, I'll say it's just fine/There's nothing wrong with wanting to live nice” PREACH BROTHER. “I’m so tired of hearing you whine/About the revolution or bringin' down the rich/When was the last time you dug a ditch, baby?!” ME TOO. “You're just a middle class socialist brat/From a suburban family and you never really had to work” PEGGED EM, MR. ELFMAN. “What the hell do you know about suffering and pain, you dumb fuck?” A-FUCKING-MEN. Obviously I’m biased, but BANGER BANGER BANGER. Sorry Aidan (not really though). I also like the driving pace and  Elfman’s falsetto.

Oingo Boingo pull off a perfect cover with “You Really Got Me”. It’s sufficiently different in style from the original, decidedly an Oingo Boingo track but with someone else’s lyrics and melody. I love the weirdo 80s Kinks vibe. Of course the original is better, but I like what they did with it a lot. “Only a Lad” is yet another banger, it’s just insane. The weird synths rule, as does the tumbling rhythm and especially the little ascending flute noise that comes in on occasion. The satire is also quite funny and well-done; when I was only passively listening, catching bits and pieces of lyrics, I kind of assumed it was a more straightforward rebel anthem. “His teachers didn't understand, they kicked him out of school/At a tender early age, just because/He didn't want to learn things/Had other interests - he liked to burn things” and “(Only a lad) He really couldn't help it/(Only a lad) He didn't want to do it/(Only a lad) He's underprivileged and abused/Perhaps a little bit confused” are my favorite lyrics. “What You See” and “Controller” are both kind of indistinct, unmemorable tracks that have decent aspects but are less than the sums of their parts. I do prefer the latter, though. The back half of this album just gets a little to Tim Burton and a little too ska. I do like “Imposter” quite a bit, mostly for its rhythmic structure. The verses are in a fast 7/8, making it feel like there’s an eighth note missing from each bar. Then, it shifts to 15/16 in the chorus, making it sound like a sixteenth not is missing. It’s a cool effect that makes the whole track sound frantic and rushed. The album ends on its weakest track, “Nasty Habits”. It just sounds like an outtake from a Tim Burton film, too theatrical and Halloweeny.

I had a great time listening. Sue me, Aidan. Sometimes we need electronics and quirkiness and gloss and capitalism.

Favorite tracks: Little Girls, On the Outside, Capitalism, You Really Got Me, Only a Lad, Imposter

Least favorite track: Nasty Habits
