# Write-up

Ornette Coleman was a jazz saxophonist from Texas but based in New York who is best known as the creator of free jazz (although today’s album is from before that). Born in 1930, he left Texas in 1949 to play tenor saxophone (an instrument he taught himself with no formal training) with a black traveling variety show after he was kicked out of his high school’s band for improvising. After someone attacked him and destroyed his tenor sax, he switched to the alto saxophone, which he played for the rest of his life. He moved to Los Angeles, found friends in the jazz scene with a similar inclination toward the avant-garde, and released his first two albums as a bandleader in 1958 and early 1959. These albums were on the cutting edge of jazz, and were extremely divisive; many jazz musicians hated him, but some other musicians became big fans. In late 1959, Coleman released his third album *The Shape of Jazz to Come*, which was his most daring and controversial yet (more on it in the next paragraph). It was successful enough to earn him a residency at the Five Spot Café in Manhattan, so he moved out to New York, where he would remain the rest of his life. Coleman’s residency was as controversial as his recorded albums; his haters included prominent jazz musicians like Miles Davis and Dizzy Gillespie (who said Coleman’s music wasn’t even jazz), while his adoring fans included jazz musician Charles Mingus, classical composer Leonard Bernstein, and a teenage Lou Reed (who would follow Coleman around New York City). In 1960, Coleman released his fourth album, *Free Jazz*. While Coleman’s first 3 albums subverted the structure and sonic conventions of jazz, *Free Jazz* abandoned both entirely, featuring one continuous improvised 37 minute composition with a double quartet. “Free jazz” became the name of a new genre, taking the world of avant-garde jazz by storm. Coleman would go on to have a long and productive career, notably playing with Yoko Ono in the late 60s and going electric alongside Miles Davis in the 70s. He died in 2015.

Although 1960’s *Free Jazz* was undoubtedly more influential, 1959’s *The Shape of Jazz to Come* is usually considered Coleman’s highest quality work. “Lonely Woman” in particular was the only song Coleman ever wrote that would become a jazz standard. The quartet that play on the album consisted of Ornette Coleman on alto saxophone, Don Cherry on cornet (instrument very similar to a trumpet), Charlie Haden on bass, and Billy Higgins on drums. Notably, *none of those instruments play chords*. Coleman’s complete rejection of chords and harmony in favor of melody was the most radical aspect of his music at this point. Additionally, he chose to play on a cheap plastic saxophone instead of a normal metal one, adding a unique and harsh sound. Although the experimentation of this album informed his later work with free jazz, this is not a free jazz album. Despite eliminating chords, each song still more or less follows the typical jazz pattern of opening-solos-closing. Free jazz would become one of the most famous subgenres of jazz, and many of the jazz albums on this list are free jazz. The album was originally supposed to be titled *Focus on Sanity* after its fourth track, but Coleman’s producer convinced him to change it to a reference to the H. G. Wells novel *The Shape of Things to Come* to reflect its alien and forward-thinking sound.

# Hargrove's review

This is my favorite jazz album, although I’ve only listened to like 8 jazz albums all the way through (17 if you count Radiohead). I’ve noticed that it falls into a similar position within its genre that a lot of my favorite albums in other genres do: right on that cusp between the conventional and the insane, the grounded and the untethered. It sounds so fresh, so unique, so creative, without completely abandoning structure for the unknown. I like this album a lot more than the (limited number of) more standard jazz I’ve listened to, but I also like it a lot more than the (limited number of) free jazz I’ve listened to. The melodies of the head sections at the beginning and end (and sometimes in between) of each track are chaotic and harsh, but they’re also still melodic and extremely emotionally evocative. You never really get the sense that everything is together, but you never get the sense that everything has fallen apart either. The solos are fascinating and liberating; whether fast or slow, the saxophone, the cornet, even the drums and bass when they get their unexpected times to shine, every instrument sounds like it’s wandering, like it’s exploring. This quality from the solos prevents the album from ever sounding aimless. Even when it doesn’t know where it’s going, it knows it’s going somewhere, or trying to anyhow. I love this album.

“Lonely Woman” absolutely blew me away on first listen as a teenager when I didn’t know what to expect from and it blows me away now still. It’s my favorite jazz song, and one of my favorite songs period. Though it starts with some light drums, it’s really the ominous tones coming from the bass that serve as your introduction. Then the sax and the cornet come in simultaneously, both playing the most wistful, yearning melody you’ve ever heard. Or are they playing the same melody? It’s similar but they’re going in different directions at times, and eventually they split off from each other entirely. The effect is almost a synthesis of the phase shifting of Steve Reich and the counterpoint of Pokémon’s music, although this predates both. The use of stereo is brilliant, mounting a full on flanked assault of sound with the sax on the left and the cornet on the right. The drums shuffle nonstop, while the bass sounds like its tuning. And that melody, just so completely evocative of the song’s name. Lonely, despondent, manic, frantic, glum. A composition that deserves its own paragraph.

“Eventually” is the first track to fully unleash the bass, and it sprints through the chaos. I find this album does a great job of answering questions posed by the previous song. After “Lonely Woman”, you might have asked yourself “Is the bass purely atmospheric? Will it only add flavor?” Now, it nearly does too much. The head introduces a very brief musical phrase that represents the only point at which all 4 instruments are completely in sync. This phrase repeats throughout at completely unpredictable intervals, reminding you whenever you think the chaos has taken hold that Coleman is always in control. The solos are truly out there, with both sax and cornet sounding like hummingbirds. The inquisitive, almost prying melody at the head of “Peace” is the one that most rivals “Lonely Woman”. After the last track, you might have asked “Okay, they like to play fast. Is that all they can do?” Now, they’ve slowed everything down and stretched the song to nearly 9 minutes. It takes the time even to give us a legato bass solo, a completely new texture for the album. That this music still sounds just as weird when everything slows to a crawl is a testament to its diversity, showing it isn’t a one-trick pony.

At this point in the album, you might lament that this album doesn’t put a spotlight on rhythm. As if to both answer and mock you, “Focus on Sanity” opens with 2 minutes of just bass and drums. I enjoy how this song plays with structure more than any other track, using an extremely brief head as an interlude between solos from each of the four instruments. And yes, that means a drum solo. And yes, it’s fantastic. This track is a full deconstruction of the broader album, an analysis of how we got here. But now, you may ask yourself “Where is that large automobile?” fuck fuck fuck sorry wrong album. You may ask yourself “Okay, that Ornette Coleman fellow sure can do a lot. But everything has been kind of negative, whether depressed or manic. Can he play something that lifts my spirits?” And the answer is no, but Don Cherry on the cornet sure can. The cornet solo on “Congeniality” is my favorite on the album, it just has such a warm (congenial?) quality to it. I also LOVE the flow of the head sections. It’s composed of these longer valleys that then come up to form these short pointy peaks, kind of like someone is getting down to work but then lifting up his head to dream and sigh. “Chronology” ends the album with such a short head section that it truly does portend the shape of jazz to come. The solos are the highlight of this song, particularly from the sax.

*The Shape of Jazz to Come* is a tight collection of 6 killer tracks that doesn’t overstay its welcome in the slightest. It delivers ideas that still sound new nearly 70 years later, but packages them into a format that is digestible. Its melodies are beautiful, and the lack of any chord structure gives them free rein. This is the platonic ideal of jazz music.

Favorite tracks: Lonely Woman, Eventually, Peace, Congeniality

Least favorite track: Chronology
