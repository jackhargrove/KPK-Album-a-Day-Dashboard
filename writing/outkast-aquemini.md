# Write-up

OutKast was the hip hop duo formed by rappers André 3000 (real name André Benjamin) and Big Boi (real name Antwan Patton) in Atlanta in 1992 when both were 16. It was one of the first hip hop acts from the American South to experience enduring commercial and critical success. While André and Big Boi attended the same high school, the two did not meet until bumping into each other at a shopping mall their junior year. They began battle rapping together in the school cafeteria before forming a duo initially named 2 Shades Deep. When that name proved too similar to another local group, they changed it to OutKast, referencing their unusual fashion senses. OutKast began hanging out at the Dungeon, a studio in the basement of local producer Rico Wade’s mother’s house. Wade ran the production trio Organized Noize along with Sleepy Brown and Ray Murray. Many notable Atlanta artists of the early 90s would hang out at the Dungeon and record music with Organized Noize, including OutKast, TLC (Organized Noize produced “Waterfalls”), and Goodie Mob (the group that included CeeLo Green). This collective became known as the Dungeon Family and continued to foster young talent in Atlanta for decades, including Killer Mike of Run the Jewels and Janelle Monae. Wade’s cousin Future also became an important figure in the Atlanta hip hop scene. Wade died in 2024.

OutKast signed to Atlanta label LaFace Records as its first hip hop signee in 1992. The duo’s first credit was on a remix of a track by TLC, which was also signed to LaFace. OutKast released its debut single “Player’s Ball” in late 1993 with G-funk production from Organized Noize. It was a surprise hit, reaching #1 on the Billboard Hot Rap Tracks chart and earning a gold certification. OutKast followed it up quickly in 1994 with a full album, *Southernplayalisticadillacmuzik*, entirely produced by Organized Noize. Though the beats certainly take influence from Dr. Dre, the overall sound differed noticeably from both the West and East Coast sounds that dominated hip hop at the time, setting the stage for the smooth and dirty sound of Southern hip hop. Despite positive reviews and breaking into the top 20 of the Billboard 200, it sparked a major backlash with hip hop purists; while East Coast and West Coast supremacists hated each other’s guts, the one thing they could agree on was that “real” rap couldn’t come from the South. When OutKast won “Best Newcomer” at the 1995 Source Awards, the crowd booed; André responded, “The South got something to say, that’s all I got to say”.

Thanks to the success of its debut album, LaFace gave OutKast more creative freedom and money. While both members became more confident, André in particular started to develop the eccentric, one-of-a-kind aesthetic he’s now known for, coinciding his decision to abstain from drugs and alcohol. OutKast released its second (and my favorite) album *ATLiens* in 1996 when both members were 21. A double album featuring diverse genre influences and more abstract subject matter, *ATLiens* was an instant hit, peaking at #3 on the Billboard 200. The single “Elevators (Me and You)” nearly reached the top 10 of the Hot 100 and remains one of the duo’s most beloved tracks. While Organized Noize produced 9/15 tracks, André and Big Boi produced the remainder themselves, including three of the four singles.

In 1998, OutKast released its third album, *Aquemini*. After the release of *ATLiens*, André began dating Erykah Badu, and the couple welcomed a child in 1997. Badu created a connection between OutKast and the flourishing neo-soul scene of the late 90s. Organized Noize still handled production on four tracks of *Aquemini*. A further three tracks were produced by Wade’s cousin Mr. DJ (real name David Sheats), OutKast’s touring DJ who would become the duo’s primary producer later in its career. However, André handled the majority of *Aquemini*’s production, largely bringing in session musicians to play live instruments instead of sampling or electronics. Big Boi would then write hooks for each track. This marked the first time that André and Big Boi chose to divide and conquer instead of directly collaborating, but it would certainly not be the last. The two also started to have serious creative disagreements that led to heated arguments. The album’s title, a combination of Big Boi’s Zodiac sign (“Aquarius”) and André’s (“Gemini”), reflects this division. The album’s CD release featured a sticker on the cover billing Big Boi as “The Player” and André as “The Poet”, further emphasizing the theme of duality. In fitting with these archetypes, André’s lyrics became grand and abstract, while Big Boi’s, though inventive, largely stayed grounded in typical hip hop topics. *Aquemini* was the first OutKast album with featured artists from beyond the Dungeon Family, including Wu-Tang Clan’s Raekwon, Parliament-Funkadelic leader George Clinton, and neo-soul sensation Erykah Badu. Still, OutKast remained true to its roots by featuring all four members of Goodie Mob. “Rosa Parks” was the album’s most successful single, charting outside the top 40 on the Hot 100, and remains a common contender for OutKast’s best song. Unfortunately, Rosa Parks herself was not a fan, and she hired Johnny Cochran to sue OutKast in a case that was not settled until 2005. The single version of “Da Art of Storytellin’ (Pt. 1)” features a guest verse from Slick Rick, though it does not appear on the album version. *Aquemini* sold extremely well, narrowly beating out *ATLiens* with a #2 debut on the Billboard 200 and eventually getting certified 3x platinum. It received strong critical praise and is now commonly cited as OutKast’s best album, though *ATLiens* and *Stankonia* both have staunch defenders.

OutKast opened for Lauryn Hill on The Miseducation Tour in 1999 before heading back to the studio. *Stankonia*, OutKast’s fourth album, released in 2000. This album demonstrated further creative drift between the duo; Big Boi spent most of his time alone in the studio honing his lyrics while André worked through jam sessions at his home, singing more than rapping. And yet, *Stankonia* became the group’s most successful album to date, going 4x platinum. While the singles “B.O.B.” and “So Fresh, So Clean” both performed well (Pitchfork named the former the best song of the 00s decade in its 2009 list), it was “Ms. Jackson” that became the duo’s first to top the Hot 100. The song described André’s strained relationship with Erykah Badu’s mother after the two separated. This was also the first album where André officially used the name André 3000 (he had gone by his real name or “Dré” previously).

After *Stankonia*, the precarious creative partnership between André and Big Boi finally disintegrated. There was zero personal animosity; the two had just become wildly different artists. They chose to make two entirely separate solo albums and combine them into one massive double album under the commercially valuable OutKast name. The resultant album, *Speakerboxxx/The Love Below* (*Speakerboxxx* was Big Boi’s album, *The Love Below* André’s), released in 2003. The album was a smash success, becoming OutKast’s first to top the Billboard 200. Additionally, two singles went #1 on the Hot 100: Big Boi’s “The Way You Move” hit the top spot for one week, while André’s “Hey Ya!” held it for an eye-popping nine weeks. A third single, André’s “Roses” featuring a verse from Big Boi, also became a hit. *Speakerboxxx/The Love Below* went 13x platinum, making it the highest certified hip hop album of all time and earning it the rare diamond certification. Broken up in all but name, OutKast decided to release one last project. The duo had hoped to release a film alongside *Aquemini* à la Prince’s *Purple Rain*, but the label shot it down at the time. Now with three #1 singles under their belts, no one could tell them “no” anymore. André and Big Boi released the film *Idlewild* in 2006 with the final OutKast album of the same name attached as a soundtrack. Sadly, this final collaboration wasn’t an *Abbey Road* moment; *Idlewild* is universally considered the worst OutKast album.

OutKast went on indefinite hiatus in 2007. Big Boi immediately started making solo music, releasing the album *Sir Lucious Left Foot: The Son of Chico Dusty* in 2010. It received extreme critical praise upon release, though consensus on the album has cooled considerably over the last 15 years. He released a second album in 2012 and a third in 2017. In 2021, Big Boi finally received the opportunity to record a track with his childhood hero and favorite artist: Kate Bush. Sadly, the track has still yet to release. Ironically, André decided he enjoyed rapping again right after OutKast broke up after a decade of slowly moving away from the style. He began contributing guest verses to numerous singles by major artists, including one verse each on Frank Ocean’s albums *Channel Orange* and *Blonde*. He also sings background vocals on “30 Hours” from Kanye West’s *The Life of Pablo* and co-produced “Fire” from *Kids See Ghosts*. However, he didn’t release any solo music until 2023, when he released the new age ambient jazz album *New Blue Sun*. It primarily featured André playing flute. He followed it up with the highly similar *Moving Day* in 2024 before moving to piano free jazz on *7 Piano Sketches* in 2025. These albums have each received progressively more confused and negative reviews. OutKast reunited for Coachella in early 2014 and played a few more concerts together before returning to hiatus at the end of the year. They played one last show together at a festival in Atlanta in 2016. OutKast was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 2025, becoming the 14th hip hop artist to receive the honor.

# Hargrove's review

Much like the electronic section, we’re closing out hip hop with a record that I’ve loved since well before we started going through this list. Unlike electronic, however, hip hop is a genre that I more naturally connect with and have an easier time articulating what I like and what I don’t. I was a little disappointed by the hip hop selection on this list; four Wu-Tang universe records feels excessive when you only have 30 slots, as does taking up space with a few novelty records and repeats from artists with albums in other sections. Still, I found a few records I had never even heard of before and immensely enjoyed, namely DJ Screw’s *All Screwed Up, Vol. II* and dälek’s *From Filthy Tongue of Gods and Griots*. Even with all of that hip hop listening behind me, OutKast still sounds special. Like most people our age, I only knew of them from “Hey Ya” and “Ms. Jackson” initially. Those are both phenomenal songs, but when I finally listened to advice telling me to dig through the band’s 90s discography, I was still shocked at the sheer volume of quality therein. *ATLiens*, the one immediately before *Aquemini*, is my favorite OutKast record. It’s relatively sleek at just under an hour and with no skits, features some of the group’s highest highs with “ATLiens” and “Elevators (Me and You)”, has no shortage of fire deep cuts, and just has a young, hungry, ambitious energy to it. Still, I understand why *Aquemini* gets to be the consensus best OutKast album. It’s a long album, right at the limit of what a single CD can hold at 74 minutes, but the bangers just. do. not. stop. coming. From the grandiose attack of “Return of the ‘G’” through to the victory lap that is “Chonkyfire”, there are really only two full tracks that aren’t top tier (“Mamacita” and “Y’All Scared”). Every track has a hard-hitting beat, lush instrumental arrangement, memorable hook, and sometimes even showy yet tasteful guest appearances from artists at the caliber of George Clinton, Raekwon, and Erykah Badu. What really makes *Aquemini* special, however, are the relentless, nonstop, never-ending perfect verses from both Big Boi and André. In fact, as a Big Boi fan, one of my only complaints about this album is that André is just *too* good. Big Boi can lay down one of the best verses you’ve ever heard and still get absolutely waxed by André on the very same track. In my opinion, this was André’s finest hours. His verses on “Return of the ‘G’”, “Aquemini” (both), “Da Art of Storytellin’ (Part 1)”, and “Da Art of Storytellin’ (Part 2)” all belong in the conversation for best hip hop verses ever. He was just on another level with this album. Big Boi has a better showing in relative terms on both *ATLiens* and *Stankonia*, but, through no fault of his own, he falls behind on *Aquemini*. Thankfully, the album mitigates even this nitpick by giving Big Boi his own section with “Slump” and “West Savannah” back to back, as well as the lyrical subject matter on “Aquemini” reaffirming the unified artistic vision of the project. There are few hip hop records that you can just put on, kick back, and enjoy like this one.

In all honesty, “Hold On, Be Strong” is not my favorite opener ever. It’s not awful y any means, but I think the album would be better off just starting with “Return of the ‘G’”, where André’s opening verse goes SO HARD. He uses the refrain as an anchor to ground the frantic pace of his bars. My personal favorite is “Return of the gangsta, thanks ta’/Them [REDACTED] who get the wrong impression of expression/Then the question is, ‘Big Boi, what's up with André?/Is he in a cult? Is he on drugs? Is he gay?/When y'all gon' break up?’/When y'all gon' wake up?/[REDACTED], I'm feelin' better than ever, what's wrong with you? You!/Get down”. I love the ominous chords of the instrumental, as well as the strings, lumbering pace, and swelling vocals and horns in the hook. It feels appropriately grand to kick off an album like *Aquemini*. “Rosa Parks”, the albums infectious pop banger, follows. It was the first song to truly sell me on 90s OutKast. It features one of the most clever singalong hooks of all time, a rarely-used-in-hip-hop acoustic guitar, and stellar verses from both Big Boi and André. Even with all of that, my favorite part is easily the hoedown. It’s hard to describe how much I love hearing a harmonica in a hip hop song.”Skew It on the Bar-B” contains another top-tier hook. Where do they come up with these? I like the skittering hi-hat in the beat. André’s verse has unmatched wordplay, most notably “The common denominator, the [REDACTED] numerator”. I appreciate Raekwon’s verse on its own, but I particularly like how it underscores how different OutKast’s triplet flow sounds from traditional rapping. “Aquemini” proves that OutKast’s hooks work just as well slowed down. All four verses are unreal. Big Boi delivers two of his best, and yet he still gets outshone by two career-defining highlights from André. Highlights include “Need some hope, like the words ‘maybe,’ ‘if,’ or ‘probably’” and “Now, question: Is every [REDACTED] with dreads for the cause?/Is every [REDACTED] with golds for the fall?/Naw/So don't get caught up in appearance/It's OutKast, *'Aquemini'* – another Black experience”. “Synthesizer” is a weird fucking track, but it serves as a great demonstration of how to integrate something weird without derailing an album. Clinton is truly a one-of-a-kind iconoclast, and he sounds it here, unsettling me as he whispers from the corner.

Despite how on fire André has been, I truly appreciate the two track stretch of “Slump” and “West Savannah” without him. Big Boi gets a chance to take over before André comes back to take the end of an album in an odd direction. “Slump” is just fun, with a classic 90s style hook and one of Big Boi’s best verses. I like “West Savannah” just that much better thanks to its slower, dirtier, grimier, smoother vibe. “Da Art of Storytellin’ (Part 1)” comes about as close to perfection as any song can. It’s got a phenomenal instrumental with an absolutely lovely melody and a lightly swinging drum beat. Big Boi drops an unbelievably good verse (“She said, ‘Let's hit the parking lot so I can sick your duck’” makes me smile every time) only to get overshadowed once again by an all-timer from André. The entire stretch of “Talkin' bout what we gonna be when we grow up/I said, ‘What you wanna be?’ She said, ‘Alive’/It made me think for a minute, then looked in her eyes/I coulda died; time went on, I got grown/Rhyme got strong, mind got blown/I came back home – to find lil' Sasha was gone/Her mama said she with a [REDACTED] that be treatin' her wrong/I kept on singin' my song and hopin' at a show/That I would one day see her standin' in the front row/But two weeks later, she got found in the back of a school/With a needle in her arm, baby two months due” kills me. The hook gives you space to sit and think after the stories in each verse. “Da Art of Storytellin’ (Part 2)” holds its own as a follow up. The lo-fi effect on André’s vocals makes his verse 100x more terifying. The piano injects so much melancholy, creating one of the thickest moods on the entire album. I don’t know where else I can find this unique atmosphere. As much as I hate agreeing with consensus on specifics, “Mamacita” is pretty rough. I don’t know how a track like this not only slipped through, but was given a full six minutes. The hook is just bad, no two ways about it. It’s incessant and annoying. I don’t mind Masada’s verse but WitchDoctor’s blows. André and Big Boi must have known something was up because even they only contributed decent verses.

After the one and only complete whiff, “SpottieOttieDopaliscious” comes in to restore quality and pull the album into some bizarre places for its final third. I love the hazy, lethargic atmosphere. It primes me to just sit back, relax, and enjoy these stories. The horns are a delight, as are the reggae bass and drums. I’m not crazy about “Y’All Scared”, though I don’t hate it either. None of the Goodie Mob guys match up to the other featured artists on the record, though I do like Khujo’s verse. André lays down a top-tier verse of course, but it’s not enough to save this track from being only okay, especially on an album as long as this one. I generally like the concept of bars delivered via phone from prison, but it feels super out of place on this decidedly not gangsta album. Also, the verse is mid. “Liberation” is a solid late album murky soul track with a hypnotic piano polymeter. CeeLo sings well, but Badu sings even better. I’ve never been a huge fan of here, but she croaks out a great verse here. It’s hard to even describe exactly what the atmosphere feels like but I’m a big fan. It’s like a dive bar at 4 AM. The track ends with an enrapturing spoken word poem. OutKast builds “Chonkyfire” around a distorted guitar riff, something I almost always enjoy in hip hop. Finally, at the end of the album, Big Boi lays down a better verse than André. He leaves it all on the field here. The Source Awards clip at the end reads like a victory lap, revisiting a called shot.

The only thing keeping this out of SS Tier is the late album slump with “Mamacita” and “Y’All Scared”, magnified with the fact that it’s a long album so the existence of even a single bad track means it’s bloated. Additionally, it leaves space for me to place *ATLiens* in SS in the event it spontaneously appears on the list.

Favorite tracks: Return of the “G”, Rosa Parks, Skew It on the Bar-B, Aquemini, Synthesizer, Slump, West Savannah, Da Art of Storytellin’ (Part 1), Da Art of Storytellin’ (Part 2), SpottieOttieDopaliscious, Liberation, Chonkyfire

Least favorite track: Mamacita

# Aidan's review

Prior to this, my entire knowledge of Outkast came from Bombs Over Baghdad, Ms. Jackson and Hey Yeah. All great songs, but none of them are on this album. There's the plague of skits on this album, but they're honestly pretty good, the closing one at the record store and the weed-buying one at the end of "Synthesizer" both get a chuckle out of me. As for the songs, the singles, "Rosa Parks" and "Da Art of Storytellin'" are both great. Big Boi's opening verse on B.O.B. is one of my all time favorites and he replicates the punchy style on "Rosa Parks". The lyric "the Hornets they won" is also very funny in the current day and age where the Hornets haven't made the playoffs since I graduated high school. There's some annoying refrains on "Aquemini" and "Mamacita", but the beats are good enough for me to endure those songs and like the rest of the album. The little melodies going on in the background on songs like "Skew It On The Bar-B" and "Rosa Parks" are also extremely cool, and make good songs exceptional. Solid rap album, as good as most of the Wu-Tang ones.
