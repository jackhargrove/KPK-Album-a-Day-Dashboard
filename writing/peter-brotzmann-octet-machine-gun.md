Peter Brötzmann was a European free jazz (this is our first truly free jazz album) saxophonist born in Remscheid, Germany in 1941. As a young adult, he pursued a career as a painter, belonging to the international Fluxus movement of the 60s. However, he quickly grew bored of the art world, instead becoming obsessed with American jazz. This inspired him to teach himself how to play saxophone and clarinet. He released his debut LP *For Adolphe Sax* in 1967 as a quartet with bassist Peter Kowald, drummer Sven Åke Johansson, and pianist Fred Van Hove.

# Write-up

All three returned in 1968 for his second album *Machine Gun*, an octet recording that also featured tenor saxophonist and bass clarinetist Willem Breuker, tenor saxophonist Evan Parker, bassist Buschi Niebergall, and drummer Han Bennink. Brötzmann played both tenor and baritone sax. The final lineup included three saxophonists, two bassists, two drummers, and a pianist. The album’s title comes from a nickname given to Brötzmann by trumpeter Don Cherry (who played on *The Shape of Jazz to Come*) because of Brötzmann’s violent style. The album was well-received by the avant-garde at the time and is still today considered a shining example of the European free jazz movement. Brötzmann would go on to release over 50 albums in his career. He continued to evolve, eventually taking influence from noise rock and heavy metal. He also played as a sideman on two albums with Cecil Taylor, who has an album on the list. He died in 2023 at age 82. Of the eight musicians on *Machine Gun*, only two are still alive: Evan Parker and Han Bennink. Parker has had the most notable career of any of the players, going on to collaborate with Spiritualized, Jah Wobble of Public Image Ltd, and Robert Wyatt of Soft Machine, in addition to his accomplished solo work. Bennink has played as a sideman for both Eric Dolphy and Cecil Taylor.

This album is our first example of free jazz, so here’s a little history. After breaking many conventions of harmony on *The Shape of Jazz to Come* in 1959, saxophonist Ornette Coleman finally abandoned structure altogether on his 1961 album *Free Jazz: A Collective Improvisation*. A culmination of avant-garde jazz up to this point, the newly coined free jazz became a craze for the American jazz underground in the early 60s, who commonly called it “the New Thing”. Free jazz was unpopular and controversial in the US, so black American free jazz artists began playing in continental Europe. European audiences tend to be more open to the avant-garde than American or British audiences, and many of these artists found success and stayed in Europe permanently. Free jazz became associated with the political upheavals led by college students in Europe in the late 60s. European jazz artists began to emulate free jazz and play it themselves, with Brötzmann among the first to do so. European free jazz artists started to change the genre, abandoning the swing rhythm of jazz along with jazz’ melodic conventions. As a result, European free jazz became its own genre apart from standard free jazz. Over time, European artists moved further and further away from any jazz traditions in their fully improvised works, eventually resulting in the genre of free improvisation. Free improvisation is just what it sounds like: completely spontaneous improvisation with no rules. The track “Moonchild” from *In the Court of the Crimson King* is an example of free improvisation.

# Hargrove's review

I am not exactly sure what to make of this kind of music. It is very easily the most out-there jazz we have heard so far, probably some of the most out-there music period. There are exactly two sections on this album with remotely conventional structures: the short melodic sections at the ends of “Machine Gun” and “Responsible”. Otherwise, this is just three saxophones buzzing over a genuinely schizophrenic piano and four rhythm section players doing their own thing. I have been assuming that the most prominent sax is Brötzmann’s, and it does not make a sound that any saxophone should make. You can even hear the Michael Phelps breaths he makes before he blows, and the errant sound of air escaping from the mouthpiece before it can go into the instrument’s tubing. I have to use a highly technical jazz term to describe it: “skronky”. I don’t think this album is for me, but it does have some redeeming qualities. Though we’ve listened to nothing quite this free, we’ve listened to plenty of avant-garde jazz; I’ve really liked some, and I’ve really disliked others. Of the avant-garde jazz I did not care for, like Dolphy’s *Out to Lunch*, it didn’t feel like the artists fully “committed to the bit”, so to speak. They took away the structure, catchy melodies, and rhythmic groove, but they didn’t also mount a full attack on my ear drums. It’s kind of a worst of both worlds. On *Machine Gun*, Brötzmann at least gives me the full, aggressive experience. I don’t like it, but I am at least better for having listened to it.

“Machine Gun” starts loud, the saxophones like sandpaper in my ears. One thing I like about this track is the way you can often hear the movements valves and other mechanical aspects of the instruments. It reiterates how these instruments are being stretched to their absolute outer limits. I like the recurring parts where the saxes all scream in unison, but I care for the soloing much less. I also want to know what is making that noise that sounds like a rusty viola. I guess someone is using a bow to fucking destroy his double bass. This track also has a bass clarinet, an instrument I have disliked on both *Out to Lunch* and *Bitches Brew*. I feel the same way here. The cacophony starting at 12:00 certainly feels good, and that groovy melody right up near the end rocks, but overall I just can’t put this on my favorites list in good conscience. It is a tough listen. But I’ll give it this: it really does sound like unfettered freedom. I love the way the double bass kicks off my favorite track, “Responsible”. The middle section still has a lot of the same bullshit as the opener, though less cluttered, but around 5:00 a bass establishes an actual pulse and things get kind of cool. The drums are great, playing an awesome rhythm on the tom while the ride keeps things unswung and driving. We even get an actual melody again at 6:45, even better than the one in “Machine Gun”. It’s genuinely quite pretty, and feels like it begins organically. I like those moments where you can feel everything come together. “Music for Han Bennink I” starts off kind of together, but then a drum solo takes over. And look: I am always a sucker for drummers playing things that are not drums. But this one really just goes on a little too long, however impressive it might be. When the saxes finally come back in around 5:45, they are SKRONKY, but I’m a little over it at this point. The album ends with a serious piano freakout, but I’ve mostly moved on by then.

I re-listened to *The Shape of Jazz to Come* today and it really reminded me how well overblowing and other insane techniques can sound when put into something with a little structure. Still, *Machine Gun* is singular enough to top some of the less avant-garde jazz we’ve heard. And I’ll definitely never forget it. I think I’ll stick it in my hardcore punk zone on the tier list with Black Flag and Germs.

Favorite tracks: Responsible

Least favorite track: Music for Han Bennink I
