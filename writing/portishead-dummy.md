# Write-up

Portishead is a British trip hop group formed in Bristol in 1991 who at the time of this album’s recording consisted of turntablist and multi-instrumentalist Geoff Barrow and vocalist Beth Gibbons. Producer and guitarist Adrian Utley also worked extensively on *Dummy* and became a full-fledged member of Portishead shortly after its release. Barrow and Gibbons met in 1991 when both were part of a government unemployment program and formed a band named after Barrow’s hometown, the Bristol suburb Portishead. Barrow was already a gifted hip hop sampler and turntablist, while Gibbons had never worked in music prior. While working in the studio, they met producer Adrian Utley, who was significantly older than either. Nevertheless, he became integrated into the group. Portishead was an important band in the “Bristol Sound” of the 1990s, the scene that developed the genre of trip hop. See the above write-up I did for Massive Attack which describes the scene in more detail.

Barrow, Gibbons, and Utley spent 1993 and 1994 recording Portishead’s debut album *Dummy*. Like other trip hop (and, more broadly, hip hop) albums, the instrumentals are based around sampling; however, a good number of the samples on this album were created by Portishead for the purpose of being sampled. They would record a snippet, put it on vinyl, and then skateboard over the vinyl to make it sound old and beat up. In addition to these artificial samples, they also sampled from soul (Isaac Hayes), funk (War), and jazz (numerous). The downtempo beats, minor key tonality, lo-fi production, and Gibbons’ melancholic crooning have given *Dummy* a reputation as a bleak, wistful album. It also often gets compared to music from spy movies. Speaking of, Portishead created the short spy film *To Kill a Dead Man* to accompany the album and used a still from it as the cover. The album produced three singles: “Numb”, “Sour Times”, and “Glory Box”. Though the former didn’t perform well, the latter two were minor hits, with “Sour Times” hitting #53 on the Billboard Hot 100 chart. The album was highly praised by critics on both sides of the Atlantic. In the 30 years since, its reputation has only grown, with many now considering it among the best albums of the 90s and even of all-time. Given the frequency with which Massive Attack’s *Mezzanine* also appears on all-time albums lists, the Bristol Sound certainly punches above its weight.

The members of Portishead didn’t revel in their success, but they didn’t take it as badly as some others have either. After three years of regrouping, they released their second album *Portishead* in 1997. It was musically more of the same as *Dummy*, though still quite well-received critically. It did not do as well commercially, however. The band then took an extended break before coming back with *Third* in 2008. This album was a radical departure sonically, incorporating psychedelic, electronic, and even krautrock influences to create a unique experimental rock sound. This album received rave critical reviews, with some even suggesting it’s better than *Dummy*. Since the, no more albums have come from Portishead. The individual members have put out solo and collaborative works though. Most notably, Gibbons appeared on Kendrick Lamar’s “Mother I Sober” from his 2022 album *Mr. Morale and the Big Steppers*.

# Hargrove's review

As I mentioned yesterday, though I know “Sour Times” by heart, this was my first time listening to *Dummy* all the way through since I was 16. I think back then I probably enjoyed trip hop far more than the average music listener my age, but the genre seems to have had a major resurgence in the last five years or so while my feelings have remained unchanged. I think it’s a cool genre that has a lot of great stuff in it, but it definitely would not be my go to. I tend to prefer things that are a bit more active, a bit more energetic. Nevertheless, I like Massive Attack quite a lot, and I remembered liking *Dummy*. I would say my experience this week has been…mixed? I’m not sure. This album has a really high floor. The way its sound works, even it’s at its worst, that moody atmosphere still sounds good. Because of this, my guess is that at the end of this review, I won’t place this album any lower than B Tier. The issue on the flip side, though, is that it also has a really low ceiling. With the exception of “Sour Times”, which uses melody and structure to function more as a pop song than anything, there’s not a ton that separates the worst songs from the best songs. That’s not to say there’s *nothing* that separates them; I have clear preferences, as I’ll get into, based on factors like songwriting, choices of instrumentation, Gibbons’ particular vocal performance, and the like. But I have to guess that the gap in my enjoyment between “Roads” and “Strangers” is the smallest gap between my second favorite and least favorite songs on any album we’ve listened to so far. That speaks to *Dummy*’s consistency, certainly, but also to its limitations. One other thing is that I think Beth Gibbons’ vocals can be hit or miss. Overall I think she’s really well-suited to this style of music, and when she nails a performance it feels great, but sometimes she lays it on a bit too thick. Regardless, I do think this is a good record, it’s just one that I think sometimes is more fêted than it needs to be.

*Dummy* opens on one of its strongest tracks with “Mysterons”. I just adore that drum roll they get on beat four every bar. As far as I can tell, “Mysterons” contains no samples, and no one has used this idea since, which baffles me. That roll sounds so slinky and cool, especially with the tempo this slow so it really lingers. The theremin also provides a nice timbre, and the song is well-structured, delivering great release with each “Did you really want?” chorus. “Sour Times” is this album’s one and only total banger. Gibbons absolutely kills it on the vocals, sounding like a damsel in distress from a 40s noir flick starring Humphrey Bogart. The double bass line propels the song forward beautifully, while that continuous rattling sample adds a chilling eerie feel. I love that song. “Strangers” unfortunately just doesn’t work for me at all. This album pulls off light industrial elements on a few of the tracks, but the chugging mechanical beat puts me off here. The structure also feels disjointed, switching between the beat and these daydream sequences. “It Could Be Sweet” has a rad repeating keyboard line, but it’s not a very well-written song and I forget it easily. After two meh tracks, “Wandering Star” brings things right back. Gibbons sounds great here again, wailing despondently at the cold, dark universe. The beat makes heavy use of the ride cymbal, which is always a plus for me, and I like the heft of the keyboard. Like “It Could Be Sweet”, “Numb” is just not a very interesting song. It shocks me that this was the lead single. Additionally, it’s not Gibbons’ finest hour. She really chews the scenery. “Roads”, on the other hand, kicks serious ass and is my second favorite on the album. I’m a sucker for sweeping, cinematic, melodramatic strings, what can I say? They also execute the instrumental build perfectly, a form you don’t see much elsewhere on this record. “Pedestal” is also great, with a bass line that’s pure sex. But like sad sex. Depressing sex. It’s one of the best atmospheres on an album that trades in atmospheres. “Biscuit” also brings something new to the table, this time some real moving parts in the instrumental. I particularly like the dissonance of the Rhodes piano. “Glory Box” ends things on a high note to cap off a pretty impressive four track run. The guitar makes this track work for me, both in the main sections as well as with its solos.

It’s a good album with one great track, some good tracks, and some meh tracks. It’ll never be one of my all-time favorites, but it’ll also always be in my repertoire. I do still prefer Massive Attack; the sleazy, hulking feel just does more for me than the melancholy. But I’m glad both exist.

Favorite tracks: Mysterons, Sour Times, Wandering Star, Roads, Pedestal, Biscuit, Glory Box

Least favorite track: Strangers
