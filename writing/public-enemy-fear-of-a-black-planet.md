# Write-up

Public Enemy is a hip hop group formed Roosevelt, New York (on Long Island) in 1985. Though it’s featured a rotating cast of members over the years, the most well-known and constant members are Chuck D (real name Carlton Ridenhour) as MC, Flavor Flav (real name William Drayton Jr.) as hype man, and the Bomb Squad production team. Chuck D and Flavor Flav formed Public Enemy, then known as Chuck D and Spectrum City, when they met as students at Adelphi University in 1985. Flavor Flav is a cousin of RZA, GZA, and ODB of Wu-Tang Clan. The two put out a few early singles that proved influential on Run-D.M.C., the Beastie Boys, and other early hardcore hip hop acts that brought the genre new life in 1986. One such single was “Public Enemy Number One”, so-titled because Chuck D felt he was receiving pushback from other local MCs. Chuck D worked at the college radio station WBAU at the time under Program Director Bill Stephney.

In 1986, Stephney was hired by Def Jam recordings, whose producer Rick Rubin had heard “Public Enemy Number One”. Stephney and Rubin worked together to convince label head Russell Simmons to sign Chuck D. Now on Def Jam, Chuck D put together a group from people he had met in his early hip hop career in college. He recruited Eric “Vietnam” Sadler and brothers Hank and Keith Shocklee, all known together as the Bomb Squad. Terminator X (real name Norman Rogers) was added as a DJ. Chuck D also brought in his friend, spoken word poet Professor Griff (real name Richard Griffin), as “Minister of Information”. And, of course, Flavor Flav had to be there as Chuck D’s hype man. The group was dubbed Public Enemy by Hank Shocklee, in reference to both Chuck D’s early single, as well as a string of racial incidents affecting black people in NYC. Public Enemy started to open for the Beastie Boys as they toured *Licensed to Ill* in 1987 at the peak of their popularity. That same year, Public Enemy released its debut album *Yo! Bum Rush the Show*. It received rave reviews and became one of the fastest-selling hip hop records of all time, though Chuck D’s black nationalist political stance stirred up controversy. Public Enemy followed this up with the highly acclaimed *It Takes a Nation of Millions to Hold Us Back* in 1988. Featuring hit singles “Don’t Believe the Hype” and “Bring the Noise”, it was even more commercially successful than the debut.

Public Enemy released its third album, *Fear of a Black Planet*, in 1990. The band began recording in 1989 alongside efforts to get signed to a major label. During negotiations, Minister of Information Professor Griff made blatantly antisemitic comments in an interview with the *Washington Times*. Hank Schocklee and Stephney forced Chuck D to fire Professor Griff to save the major label negotiations. This controversy inspired much of the rapping on *Fear of a Black Planet*. Additionally, Chuck D sought to convert Dr. Francis Cress Welsing’s theories of colorism into a full-length album. The album was entirely produced by the Bomb Squad, who built on the dense sampling technique it had developed on Public Enemy’s prior record. Many of the beats were improvised live in the studio. The album contains hundreds of samples; the ones taken from records on our list are a sample of Prince’s “Let’s Go Crazy” on “Brother’s Gonna Work It Out”, as well as samples of the Beatles’ “Getting Better” and “A Day in the Life” on “Who Stole the Soul?” *Fear of a Black Planet* features New York rapper Big Daddy Kane and West Coast rapper Ice Cube. This collaboration spurred the Bomb Squad to produce Ice Cube’s 1990 debut solo album. The cover, one of the first in hip hop to not feature a photo of the artist, features a “Black” planet eclipsing the Earth. Chuck D leads most tracks with the exception of “911 Is a Joke” and “Can’t Do Nuttin’ for Ya Man”, written and performed by Flavor Flav. *Fear of a Black Planet* released in 1990 to greater commercial success than either of Public Enemy’s first two albums. It was instrumental in making hip hop one of the largest genres of the 90s, though the lawsuits that stemmed from its heavy sampling were the beginning of the end of hip hop’s Golden Age. The album also received critical acclaim, and is retrospectively regarded as one of hip hop’s finest albums. Spike Lee used “Fight the Power” heavily in his hit film *Do the Right Thing*, helping make it Public Enemy’s most recognizable and acclaimed song.

In the early 90s, Public Enemy was instrumental in connecting the world of hip hop with rock and metal, collaborating with Sonic Youth on “Kool Thing” and thrash metal band Anthrax. Public Enemy released its fourth album, *Apocalypse 91…The Enemy Strikes Black*, in 1991 to further acclaim but slightly less sales. This rounded out the group’s first four albums, now considered one of the best four album runs in hip hop history. Terminator X got in a serious motorcycle accident in 1994 and left the group in 1998. H e was replaced by DJ Lord (real name Lord Aswod). The group has since released 12 further albums, with the most recent coming in 2025. None have reached the heights of the group’s early work.

# Hargrove's review

Public Enemy is a group that I’ve always liked the idea of, but never put in the work to call myself a fan. I listened to the album before this one, *It Takes a Nation of Millions to Hold Us Back*, like twice when I was 16. Frankly, I listened to it because it has one of the best album titles I’ve ever seen, and I stand by that. However, the only tracks I took away from it were “Don’t Believe the Hype” and “Black Steel in the Hour of Chaos”. Even then, my takeaways were fragmentary: Flavor Flav’s hook in the former track is catchy as fuck, while Chuck D’s “I got a letter from the government the other day/I opened and read it, it said they were suckers/They wanted me for their army or whatever/Picture me giving a damn – I said, ‘Never’” is one of the best openings to a rap verse I’ve ever heard. Beyond that, I’m a huge fan of the movie *Do the Right Thing*, and my numerous watches permanently etched “Fight the Power” into my brain. RIP Radio Raheem. All of this is to say, there are bits and pieces of Public Enemy that appeal to me, but I was never particularly drawn to the music itself. My ongoing fascination with the existence of Flavor Flav provides another example of this. I have found myself randomly thinking to myself how funny it is that the most serious, aggressively political major hip hop outfit of all time also has a weird little dude who wears a gigantic clock and interjects with McGaryisms. It’s so funny! Anyway, it was eye-opening to actually engage with a full Public Enemy album this week. It was…perfectly fine. There were a few bangers, more decent tracks, and a whole bunch of collage. It did not tickle me in the way I would have liked it to. Try as I might, I just don’t see what so many others do in the “Golden Age of Hip Hop”. I like sampling as much as the next guy, but the only late 80s mainstream hip hop record to truly wow me with the complexity of its sampling was Beastie Boys’ *Paul’s Boutique*. Most of my favorite hip hop songs and albums focus heavily on repetition, picking out one or two interesting musical ideas and letting them burrow deep into your brain. You can find variations on this format in the lush funk samples of West Coast hip hop, the mechanical hi-hats and slow grooves of Southern hip hop, and even the boom bap minimalism of 90s and beyond New York. But for some reason, late 80s New York sampling was all over the place. I find it interesting in an abstract post-modernism sense, but rarely does it give me the feeling I want out of hip hop. On this album in particular, tracks like “Brothers Gonna Work It Out”, “Welcome to the Terrordome”, “Burn Hollywood Burn”, and “Fight the Power” stay focused enough to get me going. Unfortunately, so muc of the album falls into not-quite-song, not-quite-skit chaotic middle ground, particularly the brutal stretch from “Meet the G That Killed Me” through “Anti-[REDACTED] Machine”. Add to that the hour plus run time, and I found some listens less than pleasant to get through. Still, there were enough bangers dispersed throughout to make it worthwhile, and when Chuck D and the Bomb Squad are locked in, there are fireworks.

I like a good cinematic opener, and “Contract on the World Love Jam” delivers. I like how it sounds like the intro to a sappy arena concert before morphing into a hip hop jam. “Brothers Gonna Work It Out” starts the album in earnest with a damn good beat. I love both the writhing electric eel guitar sample and the gnawing Prince scream sample. Chuck D sounds charismatic even if it’s not the most impressive MC performance I’ve ever heard. There’s a lot going on, but the beat and the vocal pattern keep it moving forward. In some ways, I find Flavor Flav more engaging on the mic than Chuck D, as in “911 Is a Joke”. He’s clearly less technically gifted, but he’s fun to follow along to. It reminds me of ODB. Sadly, the beat for this track is one of the album’s weakest, and the hook doesn’t grab me at all. “Incident at 66.6 FM” is a great skit. The Terminator X bit kills me. My only complaint is that it comes too early in the album. I think it would work better after the next track, “Welcome to the Terrordome”, which is a banger. Chuck D spits some absolute bullshit, but he does it so well. I like how the Bomb Squad use the vocal sample as white noise to make the track feel claustrophobic and anxiogenic. “Meet the G That Killed Me” is an awful skit and starts the worst stretch of the album. If Chuck D did the voice on “Pollywanacraka”, color me impressed. Outside of that, though, the track is long and slow and not all that interesting. After those two tracks, the disjointed intro to “Anti-[REDACTED] Machine” sounds BRUTAL (derogatory). This album needs structure! Chuck D finally comes in with two fire verses, but the hook blows, and the album continues to spiral. Thankfully, “Burn Hollywood Burn” comes to the rescue. Chuck D gives an insane verse right off the bat. Cube’s verse is basic, but it works well. I’ve never really listened to Big Daddy Kane before but I’m super impressed by his voice too. It complements the shrill sounds in the beat. After an intense lull, “Power to the People” brings the energy flooding back all at once. The drums go CRAZY. Chuck D mostly ad-libs, but it brings the noise. Sadly, the second half devolves once more, making it false hope for a turnaround. We are halfway through the album and only have four complete songs.

“Who Stole the Soul?” keeps the energy high and actually follows through, unlike the last track. It’s not the most iconic song in the world, but it’s the exact vibe I want from classic hip hop. This album has had an alarming absence of strong hooks; “Fear of a Black Planet” finally gives us a worthy one. It’s unorthodox, but I’ve gotten it stuck in my head. “Revolutionary Generation” continues the mid-album renaissance with an uptempo beat and structured rapping. It doesn’t quite measure up to the last two tracks, but it’s damn solid, and that’s enough for me. I much prefer “Can’t Do Nuttin’ for Ya, Man!” to the other Flavor Flav track. The flow is unbelievable catchy, plus it has genuinely funny lyrics. The outro in particular cracks me up. I like the dubby beat of “Reggie Jax”, but Chuck D’s freestyle leaves something to be desired, and it’s far too short to leave a real impression given how slow and laidback it sounds. “Leave This Off Your Fuckin’ Charts” is the exact kind of instrumental I dislike. I find it so rare that I appreciate this style of sound collage. If you’re going to do plunderphonics, give me something more cohesive. “B Side Wins Again” reminds me of Eric B. & Rakim in both the vocal delivery and the simpler beat. I’m going to have to repeat myself for the 100th time in this project, though: pretty good tracks no longer cut it past the 45 minute mark on an album. By contrast, “War at 33 1/3” brings enough intensity to make me forget how long I’ve been listening. With a relentless pace, shrill buzzing that jets from one ear to the other, and amazing spitting from Chuck D, this is a gem of a late album deep cut. “Final Count of the Collision Between Us and the Damned” provides a chill and smooth interlude to lead into the final track. “Fight the Power” slaps, plain and simple. Some of the bars are wack, particularly the Elvis one (see my Mos Def review for my frustration when people try to dismantle the racial exchange of early rock and roll). But I don’t even care because it goes hard, I fear. What an eternal hook.

I re-listened to the other three hip hop albums from the 80s on our list today: Eric B. & Rakim’s *Paid in Full* (1987), Slick Rick’s *The Great Adventures of Slick Rick* (1988), and De La Soul’s *3 Feet High and Rising* (1989). Sadly, at the end of our classic hip hop album runway, none of these four albums have worked for me. My least favorite by far remains De La Soul, who spend most of the album annoying me. Next is Slick Rick; “Children’s Story” is easily my favorite track across all four records, but the rest of that album doesn’t measure up. That leaves a fight for the top spot between Public Enemy and Eric B. & Rakim. Ultimately, while *Paid in Full* stays more consistent across its shorter run time, I have to give the nod to *Fear of a Black Planet*. It just has more memorable tracks. Still, it’s not enough for any of these albums to rise above high C Tier.

Favorite tracks: Brothers Gonna Work It Out, Welcome to the Terrordome, Burn Hollywood Burn, Who Stole the Soul?, Fear of a Black Planet, Revolutionary Generation, Can’t Do Nuttin’ for Ya, Man!, War at 33 1/3, Fight the Power

Least favorite track: Pollywanacraka
