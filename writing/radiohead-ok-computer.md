# Write-up

Radiohead is a British alt rock band formed in Abingdon, England, in 1985 consisting of vocalist and guitarist Thom Yorke, guitarist and multi-instrumentalist Jonny Greenwood, guitarist Ed O’Brien, bassist Colin Greenwood, and drummer Philip Selway. The band formed with the name On a Friday at a boarding school for boys near Oxford. Yorke quickly became the band’s main songwriter. A demo from the band earned a record deal, but the members turned it down so they could attend university, during which time the band took a four-year break. The band reformed in 1991, drawing support from Slowdive producer Chris Hufford. The band generated a reputation as a good band with a terrible name; the band chose “Radiohead” instead as a reference to a Talking Heads song. Radiohead signed with major label EMI and recorded its first album, *Pablo Honey*, in 1993 using former Pixies and Dinosaur Jr. producers. The album was a mild success, but not what the producers hoped, and they were written off in the press as opportunists trying to capitalize off of grunge. Over the next few months, “Creep” slowly became a sleeper hit. This skyrocketed Radiohead to popularity in the still-massive alt rock scene. The band was uncomfortable with its commercial MTV image, so it pivoted toward a deeper sound in 1995 with its second album, *The Bends*. This album was also successful, breaking the band through the mass of Britpop bands that were huge in the UK at the time.

Radiohead’s third album, *OK Computer*, released in 1997. The album’s title stems from a line in the radio adaptation of *Hitchhiker’s Guide to the Galaxy*. Brian Eno commissioned the first song written for the album, “Lucky”, as a charity single in 1995. The band worked with producer Nigel Godrich for this single. When the band went to the studio in 1996 to record the album, they opted to self-produce with Godrich. The band moved out to a mansion in the countryside to seclude themselves as they composed the album; the cavernous rooms helped achieve the natural reverb effect they were looking for on some tracks. According to Yorke, the central influence on the album was the dense sound of Miles Davis’ *Bitches Brew*. Additional influences included contemporary alt rock bands, the Beatles, and krautrock. Though the album is not a narrative concept album, Yorke’s lyrics reflect his growing discomfort with the technologically advanced world of the late 20th century. The track “Airbag” used a sampler for its drums, inspired by DJ Shadow. “Paranoid Android” features three unfinished tracks spliced together, inspired by the Beatles’ “Happiness Is a Warm Gun”. The title of “Subterranean Homesick Alien” is a reference to Bob Dylan’s “Subterranean Homesick Blues”. The outro of “Exit Music (For a Film)” was an attempt to replicate the sound of Portishead. The guitar part on “No Surprises” takes inspiration from the Beach Boys’ “Wouldn’t It Be Nice”. The album’s cover features an edited photo of a freeway interchange in Hartford, Connecticut.

Upon completion of *OK Computer*, Radiohead’s label was worried that the album was too uncommercial for release, particularly the six-minute runtime of lead single “Paranoid Android”. However, that single became their highest charting yet in the UK, while the album itself sold well. *OK Computer* received universal acclaim from critics at the time and has become a mainstay in conversations of the best albums ever. The album helped usher in the end of Britpop in the UK, setting the stage for bands like Coldplay to emulate Radiohead’s as “post-Britpop”. The album has become strongly identified with obsessive internet music fans, holding the top spot on RateYourMusic.com's all-time top album chart for over a decade before being dethroned by Kendrick Lamar’s *To Pimp a Butterfly* in 2022. The story of Radiohead continues in my previous write-ups for *Kid A* and *In Rainbows*.

# Hargrove's review

As one of the first albums the internet recommended to me I got really into music at age 16, *OK Computer* will always hold a special place in my heart. Though I don’t think I ever “officially” considered it my all-time favorite record at any point (I got into *Emergency & I* around the same point, and then Joanna Newsom about 6 months later), there have been multiple points of my life where, for all intents and purposes, it was. I could just listen to this record forever, over and over and over again. It’s a perfect storm of replayability: distinctive sound, cohesive atmosphere, pleasant soundscapes, sufficient edge/aggression, and elite songwriting (pop and otherwise). When you listen to this album, you are sucked into the world of *OK Computer* for just under an hour, those spacey synths and chiming bells and soaring vocals making you forget what other music even sounds like. The singles (“Paranoid Android”, “Karma Police”, “Lucky”, “No Surprises”) are obviously great, but, fuck, those deep cuts are sometimes even better! “Airbag”? ”Exit Music (For a Film)”? “Let Down”? “Climbing Up the Walls”? It feels wrong to even call them deep cuts! These are just stellar, stellar songs in their own right. While I don’t feel the same neuroses about technology as Yorke does, the sheer emotionality of the lyrics moves me every time I listen. Whether it’s the utter despair of “Let Down”, the manic anxiety of “Paranoid”, or the resigned acceptance of “No Surprises”, I feel every emotion Yorke sings through his microphone so deeply. As I’ve said before, *In Rainbows* is my favorite Radiohead album, necessarily entailing that I prefer it to *OK Computer*. And there are a few reasons for that. For one, I like the signature atmosphere of *In Rainbows* just a little bit more than I do the signature atmosphere of *OK Computer*. I also find *In Rainbows* a little more consistent, without any track as weak as “Electioneering” or any momentum killer on the same level as “Fitter Happier”. I also think that some of the qualities that make *OK Computer* a consensus-building favorite also serve to weaken it ever so slightly as an album; it’s so perfectly tailored to *everyone* that it’s much less likely to be tailored to *anyone*, an approach opposite records like *Kid A* or *In Rainbows* that carve out narrower but more intense niches (*Kid A* is my least favorite of the three albums named in this review simply because I don’t belong to its niche, and also because its title track is the only song I genuinely dislike across all three albums). In the grand scheme of things, however, these flaws are exceedingly minor, and few records deserve their place in the canon anywhere near as much as this one.

The album starts at a sky-high point with the sublime guitar riff that opens “Airbag”. I love everything about this track, from the guitar riff, to the hip hop style drums, to the banger bass line. I think it was a good choice to start the record with a straight-ahead rocker; something about it sends me to another dimension and prepares me for the rest of the record. Then you hear the iconic computer beep-boop four count and know you’re in for six of the greatest minutes in music history with “Paranoid Android”. All three sections rule, but, gun to my head, I like each section a little bit less than the last. The first is just perfect, with an amazing vocal melody and delivery from Yorke. I love the chords and instrumental richness of the “What’s that?” sections. There’s a stellar transition between sections one and two, specifically a great 7/8 guitar line. Yorke’s yelling and the guitar timbre make this section feel so heavy. My favorite part of section three is the intricate, overlapping vocal line near the end. The song closes on a frantic, rocking note, a perfect end. I like “Subterranean Homesick Alien” a lot. Though it never builds to the same level of grandeur or intensity as the other tracks, it keeps a thick, unique atmosphere. The drums are a highlight for me, as is the release of the chorus. “Exit Music (For a Film)” contains one of the best extended builds I’ve ever heard in any song, starting from just acoustic guitar to build into a cosmic Shakespearean masterpiece. In particular, I adore the haunting, Church choir style backing vocals. The climax may be short, but holy FUCK is it intense. “Let Down” is one of my favorite Radiohead songs on any album. Those chiming bells give the track a hollow brightness betrayed by the crushing chorus. The phasing of the instrumental creates an absolutely hypnotic effect. I find the use of a building chord progression on the verses interesting for such a depressing track.

“Karma Police” is the best pop song on this album. The verses and chorus are both so catchy, as is the jangly piano line. And I love how it disintegrates at the end. “Fitter Happier” feels trite and obvious (at least now) and is a chore to get through each listen. However, it was perfectly executed for what it is and that has made it undeniably iconic. “Electioneering” is nowhere near as bad as some make it out to be, but it certainly lacks a certain spark that the rest of the album has. It sounds like an excerpt from a different album. I think they could have made this style of hard rock work, but it’s lacking the synths that identify everything else as being part of *OK Computer* world. By contrast, despite being perhaps the most unique sounding track,  “Climbing Up the Walls” sucks us right back into that recognizable *OK Computer* sound. The songwriting reminds me a bit of what you hear on *In Rainbows*, as do the orgasmic strings during the chorus. The whole track sounds so creepy and unlike anything else, and yet, what a gorgeous song. I really, really, really like it. “No Surprises” is the single that has least resonated with me historically, but for whatever reason, I’ve been liking it a lot this week. The bells create a suffocatingly bright atmosphere that makes the positive depiction of suicide palatable. It’s like a poppier version of “Let Down”. “Lucky” takes a second to find its legs, but when it does, it’s an immensely powerful track. Yorke’s vocals are the highlight as he yells on the chorus and particularly when he belts “It’s gonna be a glorious day” on verse two. I’m not crazy about “The Tourist” on its own, but I do think it’s a banger closer for the album. “Hey, man, slow down” is as good a mission statement as any for *OK Computer* and I love that the album ends at the chime of a bell. However, it’s a bit slow for my taste.

God, I’m so glad I can listen to this album again after a year and half. Few albums have this level of aura every time you listen. It’s just so obviously one of the best albums ever released.

Favorite tracks: Airbag, Paranoid Android, Subterranean Homesick Alien, Exit Music (For a Film), Let Down, Karma Police, Climbing Up the Walls, No Surprises, Lucky

Least favorite track: Electioneering

# Aidan's review

This album starts out with two great tracks in "So Easy" and "Elpe". "So Easy" combines a nice melody with just enough action in the background to keep it moving, and closes with a sample of a phone call that does a lot to set the mood. "Eple" has similar ideas, with interesting stuff going on underneath a bouncing melody. The rest of the album just coasts, doing nothing impressive at all. "Sparks" is a perfectly adequate trip hop song, "In Space" explores the same territory as the opening pair but with less conviction and energy, and then "Poor Leno" stars with the extremely bland vocal samples. I've liked other stuff from the vocalist, Erlend Øye, both under his indie folk duo Kings of Convenience and his indie electronic group Whitest Boy Alive. Here, he lends his voice to the most generic European song imaginable, where Röyksopp just loops the same vocal sample over a EDM-ish beat and calls it a day. Øye has 3 lines to get looped on "Remind Me" and it's marginally better for it, but it's still not a great song. There's just so many mediocre tracks on this album and not really a coherent idea anyway, just a melange of electronic styles. No song really takes a risk, and nothing is that bad, but the end result is just really bland.
