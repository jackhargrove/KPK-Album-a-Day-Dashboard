# Write-up

Red House Painters was the first musical project led by singer and guitarist Mark Kozelek and this is the only album from his long and storied career to be on this list. While Kozelek’s other projects were more explicitly solo vehicles, RHP at least started out as a real band and consisted of guitarist and vocalist Mark Kozelek, guitarist Gorden Mack, bassist Jerry Vessel, and drummer Anthony Koutsos. Kozelek was born in 1967 to a real good American white trash family in Massillon, Ohio, a state that he will reference quite frequently in his future music. Once he came of age, he moved to Atlanta to pursue music, where he formed RHP with Koutsos in 1988. In 1989, the pair moved to San Francisco, where they added Mack and Vessel; Kozelek has remained in SF since, and the city is also quite frequently referenced in his music. RHP began playing a sad, folksy form of alternative rock that would eventually be called “slowcore”, a genre that they would help define and popularize. RHP are set apart from other slowcore bands by their emphasis on influences from folk music. They put together a demo tape that ultimately made its way to Ivo Watts-Russell, head of legendary indie label 4AD and namesake of the Cocteau Twins song “Ivo”. He signed them and they polished six of their demos to release as their debut album, 1992’s *Down Colorful Hill*.

*Down Colorful Hill* may be a debut, but it’s a perfect encapsulation of the sound that RHP would pursue for the rest of the 90s. The songs are long and dominated by Kozelek’s deep voice and deep sadness. The songs focus on aging and ex-girlfriends (in this case, an older woman from Japan), two topics that Kozelek has never stopped singing about. The cover photo was taken photographer Simon Larbalestier, whose photos were also used by the Pixies and would be used by RHP for future albums. In 1993, RHP released two self-titled albums that are commonly referred to as *Rollercoaster* and *Bridge* in reference to their cover photos. The former is often considered RHP’s best and includes “Katy Song”, while the latter has a much more mixed reception. Their next album, *Ocean Beach*, came in 1995. Mack left the band after this, and RHP started to become a Kozelek solo project. They left 4AD and released *Songs for a Blue Guitar* in 1996, their most commercially successful album. They completed their next album in 1998, but label and band issues meant it wouldn’t release until 2001. Entitled *Old Ramon*, it would be their final album. RHP released 5 albums in 8 years.

Kozelek was left in a weird place by the late 90s. RHP had been popular enough to make him well-known in the American indie scene and support international touring, but he wasn’t quite a rockstar like he had always dreamed of. He pursued an acting career briefly, having a small part in the very good 2000 film *Almost Famous* and later appearing in a Steve Martin movie, but this didn’t break him through either. He also at some point became obsessed with the indie band Modest Mouse, and frontman Isaac Brock’s lyrical style in particular changed the way Kozelek wrote. In 2003, Kozelek reunited with Koutsos of RHP and added two more musicians to form his new band Sun Kil Moon. This was more or less a continuation of RHP, but Kozelek thought a new name would reinvigorate critical interest (he was right). SKM released *Ghosts of the Great Highway* in 2003 to great acclaim. In my opinion, it’s the best project Kozelek has ever done, and the track “Carry Me Ohio” is in the top 10 songs ever written. You really have to hear it. SKM released an album of heavily altered Modest Mouse covers in 2005 that was poorly received, but their albums over the next 10 years would get consistent critical acclaim, if not commercial success.

In 2014, SKM released *Benji*, an album trying out a new lyrical style for Kozelek: rambling through stories of his life. *Benji* was an unexpected commercial success and is to this day Kozelek’s most well-known album. It started somewhat of a career renaissance for him that he unfortunately squandered by being a complete asshole. You see, Kozelek is a tough man to know, and had developed a reputation of being kind of an abrasive prick over his career. He’s frequently rude to audiences at shows and to other bands at festivals. Shortly after *Benji*, Kozelek cause massive drama by hurling insults at fellow performers The War on Drugs because their sound was bleeding into his. A few days later, he would release a single entitled “War on Drugs: Suck My Cock”. Episode like this, combined with his music diving more and more into mundane descriptions of his day-to-day life, wore out his welcome with critics very quickly. By 2019, Kozelek’s music was, no exaggeration, a podcast half spoken, half sung over a light acoustic guitar. Still, he had earned enough respect over the years that most people were happy to let him ride off into the sunset. Except, one of the enemies that he made by being an asshole was Pitchfork writer Laura Snapes. Kozelek got mad at a review she wrote in Pitchfork in the mid 2010s and ranted about her being a “bitch” at a concert. This ill will between Kozelek and Pitchfork would only grow over time, and made Pitchfork very receptive to stories that might hurt Kozelek. In 2020, a woman came to Pitchfork with allegations that Kozelek had sexually assaulted her in a hotel room, and they broke the story. Kozelek denied all wrongdoing, but by 2021 more than five woman had come forward with similar stories. Everything in the stories lines up, and pretty much everyone who knew Kozelek completely believes them. As a result, he’s been completely blackballed from the music scene. He still tours and releases music, but no journalists will cover it. One of the greatest musical minds of the last 50 years has been reduced to perhaps the most pathetic, and he has only himself to blame. A sad, cautionary tale.

# Hargrove's review

I am a huge fan of Mark Kozelek’s music. My first exposure to him was when I was a young teenager and my family’s car had a CD of his then-recent 2013 album *Perils from the Sea* with Jimmy LaValle. This is a pretty deep cut album for Kozelek, or at least it was until Phoebe Bridgers (who was a Kozelek superfan as a teenager) covered “You Missed My Heart” from the album. The songs “Gustavo”, about a group of illegal immigrants he hires to fix up his Tahoe lake house, and “1936”, about selling a sentimental keepsake of his mom’s for weed money, were two songs that blew me away as a kid and that I still love today. His style of storytelling is so unique, a weird mix of matter-of-fact and deeply emotional. I never explored much of his other stuff until very early COVID, when I got super into Sun Kil Moon. I fell in love with all of their major albums and songs. I knew about Red House Painters and I dabbled in some stuff like “Katy Song”, but I actually had never listened to any of their albums all the way through. I am glad that I have now heard *Down Colorful Hill* from front to back numerous times. I’m the perfect age for it now, and there’s something about Kozelek’s fears and insecurities that track with mine so well. He is absolutely terrified of aging, of growing out of the edgy cool demographic and becoming a has-been. This creates an interesting dynamic with his later period, where he often writes about feeling like an old has-been. I appreciate his brutal honesty, and I especially appreciate that his brutal honesty extends to himself, not just others. I think it’s a credit to Kozelek’s writing that he was someone who didn’t have a whole ton of reasons to be sad, and yet he makes such a compelling case that his life is the sorriest of all sorry stories. He’s convincing enough to turn “woe-is-me” into “woe-actually-is-me”. I often think of him like Jeff Mangum of Neutral Milk Hotel, someone who feels things to an abnormal magnitude. But on top of that, he’s so good at conveying those elevated feelings to everyone else. All of these elements make his behavior toward women especially hard to cope with. Everything I know about the man tells me he has the capacity to know better, to empathize with people he does harm to, and yet he does it anyway. It’s beyond tragic how much of his RHP output is fears about becoming a creepy old man, and he legitimately became a creepy old man. His current exile from all things cool and chic is a punishment befitting Greek Mythology. Still, I can’t help but to feel the vitality of his work, if only as a cautionary tale, if only to recognize that whatever ways he chose to cope with his fears was not successful. “Carry Me Ohio” still kills me. “Katy Song” still kills me. “Gustavo” still kills me. “Carissa” still kills me. And this album killed me. It’s something I’m still struggling with.

The first two tracks on this album are among Kozelek’s finest. The remaining songs are uneven in quality and a little bit scattered, dragging down the album as a whole, but the first two are sublime. “24” is a hard-hitting portrait of an awkward time of life. You’ve entered your mid-20s, you’re well out of college, you’re solidified as an adult, but still you’re unimportant and powerless. You’re young enough to remember what teenage you thought you might look like at this time, and you feel every success and failure deeply. Kozelek’s vocals sound so resigned, neither pessimistic nor optimistic, just giving in to the inexorable marching of time. The parts of the song with vocals are backed by a twangy guitar and a bouncy piano that give it all a folk charm, some sense that things are going to be okay. But the last minute and a half twists everything into a disturbing, distorted mess of guitar. What does it mean? Is Kozelek accepting the pressures of age, or is he lashing out? He doesn’t know, and I don’t know either. It’s all unresolved. “Medicine Bottle” moves onto a topic that hits home for me a little less, but I think it’s undeniably a stronger song. It’s a proto-“Katy Song” in a lot of ways, a well-structured and well-written 10 minute track about an ex-girlfriend. This is vintage Kozelek, and it sounds damn good. I don’t know how he makes everything sound so dark and sad, but he does. It’s a step up from pretty much any other depressing artist I can think of, and this isn’t even e a particularly sad song. The lyrics are a fleshed-out but spotty delivery of scenes from the relationship, scenes that he’ll keep with him in his medicine cabinet. It’s a really, really good song.

The remaining songs vary in quality, but don’t touch the last two. the title track “Down Colorful Hill” is the longest song on the album at over 11 minutes, and it really did not need to be. There’s a militaristic drum line backing the entire track which gets a little tiresome at points. The lyrics leave something to be desired, more abstract than the typical Kozelek outing. The song does get much better about two-thirds of the way through with an explosion of volume. I still like this song, but it is certainly flawed. “Japanese to English” is much less grand, but also much less memorable. In fact, the most memorable part is the horrific pronunciation of “Japanese” in the chorus that makes it really hard for me to like this song. It has a good atmosphere, particularly in the beginning, but it’s my least favorite track. “Lord Kill The Pain” wins me back though if only because Kozelek is the only person I’ve ever heard describe praying to God to kill those who annoy him, something I do frequently. “Kill my girlfriend and kill my best friend Sam”, “Lill my neighbors and all my family too”, and “Drown my country/Drown everyone but me” are all ridiculous lines that are very similar to thoughts I have had. The music itself is also much louder and more upbeat than the other songs, which is appreciated. “Michael” is interesting because no individual element is particularly impressive or memorable, but everything combines nicely to make this a very good song. It’s built really well and has momentum in all of the right places. It’s also a nice ode to a lost friend, another classic Kozelek topic.

This album is flawed, as is the following RHP album *Rollercoaster*, honestly as is Sun Kil Moon’s *Ghosts of the Great Highway*. Kozelek was always self-indulgent, which is both his best and worst quality as an artist. It meant that there was usually something off with albums, but some of his songs are among the greatest on God’s green Earth. I will happily throw “24” and “Medicine Bottle” into that canon and be on my way, glad with the existence of *Down Colorful Hill*.

Favorite tracks: 24, Medicine Bottle, Lord Kill The Pain, Michael

Least favorite track: Japanese to English

# Aidan's review

Hargrove has raved about Mark Kozelek a LOT, and I will admit that Benji is damn good. I just don't find myself as emotionally connected to this album as Benji. 24 specifically just doesn't work for me, I don't find the lyrics emotionally moving and it's so slow that if the lyrics don't hit, there's nothing else there. There rest of the album is better, Medicine Bottle is probably the most emotionally resonant for me, and the repeated chorus just swirls in a really nice way for lack of a better word. Lord Kill the Pain is basically a slowcore version of Shellac's Prayer To God (to be clear, Red House Painters did it 8 years early), and the songs are both lyrically unique as well as musically interesting. It's easy to see how much influence this had on other bands, and I'm constantly wracked by deja vu for other songs when listening to this album. Still, those other bands have stylistic touches that I like more than the raw lyricism of Kozelek, which isn't really to my liking here. It's not bad, and it's definitely not the self parody of a style he got into later, but I just fail to find emotional resonance for too many of the songs. Might be that I just started listening to Kozelek with Sun Kil Moon, but I just prefer the instrumental style there. Still, solid album that has a lot of elements I like.
