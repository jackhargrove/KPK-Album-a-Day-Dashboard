# Write-up

Slick Rick (real name Ricky Walters) is a British-American rapper born in London in 1965. Both of his parents were immigrants from Jamaica. As a baby, he was blinded in his right eye by a shard of broken glass leading to his signature eye-patch he wore as a rapper. In 1976, at age 11, Slick Rick’s family moved to the Bronx, and he has called NYC home ever since. The Bronx in the late 70s was the epicenter of early hip hop and, as a student at an art high school, Slick Rick became enmeshed in the hip hop scene and learned to rap. In 1984, Slick Rick met Doug E. Fresh, commonly referred to as the Human Beat Box. As his nickname attests, Fresh was one of the world’s premier beatboxers, and he kept a crew of rappers to perform over his beats known as the Get Fresh Crew. Fresh added Slick Rick as the third member of his crew. Slick Rick came to the forefront of the blooming hip hop scene in 1986 when the Doug E. Fresh singles “The Show” and “La Di Da Di” both became international hits; both singles featured Slick Rick’s rapping. He soon became known as “hip hop’s greatest storyteller”. In 1986, Russell Simmons became Slick Rick’s manager, and he signed to Simmons’ and Rick Rubins’ Def Jam Records as its third signee.

Slick Rick released his debut solo album *The Great Adventures of Slick Rick* on Def Jam in 1988. The album exemplified Slick Rick’s signature narrative storytelling style, often including characters with voices and extended jokes. No track demonstrates this more than “Children’s Story”, Slick Rick’s most famous contribution to hip hop history. The track is framed as a cautionary tale about a young man who falls into a life of crime that ultimately results in his death. To this day, it commonly appears on lists of the greatest rap songs ever. Slick Rick’s storytelling style was particularly influential on Nas, who considers this album one of his favorites of all time. Four tracks were produced by Public Enemy production team The Bomb Squad, while one was produced by Run DMC’s Jam Master Jay. The album was a major success, topping the Billboard R&B/Hip-Hop chart and producing four charting singles.

Slick Rick’s career was derailed by significant legal problems after his debut album. His mother hired his cousin Mark Plummer as a bodyguard in 1989, but Plummer turned out to be a violent and troubled individual. Slick Rick fired Plummer in 1990, at which point Plummer began continuously robbing and threatening him. At the end of his rope, Slick Rick fired four shots at Plummer in July 1990, hitting both Plummer and a passerby. Both survived, but this resulted in Slick Rick going to prison until 1997 for attempted murder. Slick Rick released his second album, *The Ruler’s Back*, in 1991 while on bail during his trial. It was commercially successful, but received poor reviews. He recorded a third album, 1994’s *Behind Bars*, from prison. After his release, Slick Rick released his fourth album, *The Art of Storytelling*, in 1999. Featuring heavy-hitters like Nas, Snoop Dogg, Raekwon, and OutKast, it was a successful comeback record that broke into the top 10 of the Billboard 200. Slick Rick spent most of the 2000s fighting deportation, a case he ultimately won. In 2008, New York Governor David Paterson pardoned him of his attempted murder charges. He became a naturalized citizen of the US in 2016. After a quarter-century hiatus, Slick Rick released his fifth album *Victory* in 2025.

*The Great Adventures of Slick Rick* came out during a period known as hip hop’s golden age. Hip hop of the late 70s and early 80s was quite limited, typically featuring rudimentary rhymes over sampled disco bass lines or cold 808 beats. Many assumed at the time that it was a novelty genre with a limited shelf-life. In 1985, Def Jam put out its first release, LL Cool J’s *Radio*. Featuring skilled rapping from LL Cool J and focused, minimalist production from Rick Rubin, this record reinvigorated and diversified the hip hop scene. Many pinpoint it as the beginning of hip hop’s golden age. Run DMC’s *Raising Hell* and Beastie Boys’ *Licensed to Ill*, both released in 1986 and produced by Rubin, solidified the popularity of this new sound and provided the template upon which all hip hop since has followed. Other major artists of this period include KRS-1 and his Boogie Down Productions, Public Enemy, and, of course, Slick Rick. Native Tongues artists like De La Soul and A Tribe Called Quest are sometimes associated with this era, though their alternative, jazzy sound was more reflective of the hip hop of the 90s. Eric B. and Rakim also belonged to the hip hop scene during the golden age, but their music was so influential on 90s New York hip hop that it is better classified as boom bap. The golden age ended in the early 90s when the center of hip hop shifted from New York to California during the gangsta rap explosion.

# Hargrove's review

Slick Rick is a rapper that my parents got me into when I was in middle school, right around the time period when I steadfastly believed 80s Golden Age hip hop was the genre’s peak. This lasted until I was around 15, when I told a fellow counselor at the summer camp where I worked that Run-DMC was the greatest hip hop artist of all time and he laughed at me. Nevertheless, I loved Run-DMC, I loved Beastie Boys, and I loved Slick Rick. Well, more precisely, I loved “Children’s Story”. And I still love “Children’s Story”. What a phenomenal song that is. From the believable framing, to the enrapturing storytelling, to memorable lines like “Dave, the dope fiend shootin' dope/Who don't know the meaning of water nor soap”, to the silly voices, “Children’s Story” stands alone as one of the greatest tracks of the 80s. The most comparable album on this list to *The Great Adventures of Slick Rick* is Eric B. & Rakim’s *Paid in Full*, which doesn’t have an individual track anywhere near as good as “Children’s Story”. However, *The Great Adventures of Slick Rick* is incredibly top-heavy, with no other track coming close to “Children’s Story” and my interest in the album falling off a cliff somewhere around the halfway point. Sure, there are other decent tracks. “Treat Her Like a Prostitute” and “The Moment I Feared” are the most admirable efforts narratively, while “The Ruler’s Back” contributes the best beat and “Indian Girl (An Adult Story)” makes me laugh. Otherwise, though, the tracks really start to blend together. I blame two things for this. First, with a few exceptions, the beats on this album are not that good. I was really disappointed by that because The Bomb Squad contributed so many instrumentals and I trusted them. Unfortunately, few of the beats rise above a rudimentary drum line and maybe one melodic lick. The second issue is that many of the stories, though extraordinarily well told, don’t make a whole ton of sense. What was the point of the episode in “Mona Lisa”? How many fights are there in “Kit (What’s the Scoop)”? Even “Indian Girl (An Adult Story”, a track I liked, is mostly just gobbledegook. Much like with *Paid in Full*, I’m forced to rank this album much lower than I’d like to because of these flaws that, frankly, only exist due to the album’s age. The 80s were a transitional time for hip hop and no one really worked out some of these kinks until the early 90s. So as much as I admire Slick Rick generally and “Children’s Story” specifically, as an album, I have to be realistic here. The highs are high, but the average quality is much lower than I’d like it to be.

I like “Treat Her Like a Prostitute” a good bit. The three story structure works well, as does the classic drum beat. The little soundbite of “Treat her like a prostitute (Do what?)” has colonized my brain. I just love stories. I like hearing stories. I like telling stories. I like everything about stories. So a track like this works for me. I appreciate that this album doesn’t have full-on skits, instead opting for small gags like the Medieval one that opens “The Ruler’s Back”. This track doesn’t have the most interesting rapping in the world, but the combo of the piano and the horns makes it one of my favorite beats from this era. RIP Jam Master Jay. “Children’s Story” is just so good, man. The framing and the silly voices work so well. Rick’s story is top notch, and the following lines will stay with me forever:  “Once upon a time not long ago/When people wore pajamas and lived life slow”, “Dave, the dope fiend shootin' dope/Who don't know the meaning of water nor soap”, “Rat-a-tat-tatted and all the cops scattered”, “Pointed at her head, he said the gun was full of lead/He told the cops, "Back off, or honey here is dead”/Deep in his heart he knew he was wrong/So he let the lady go and he starts to run on”, “This ain't funny so don't you dare laugh/Just another case about the wrong path/Straight and narrow or your soul gets cast”, and “Knock him out the box, Rick”. The stories of “The Moment I Feared” are a little bit nonsensical but they’re interesting to follow. I also enjoy the subtler beat here. “Let’s Get Crazy” is well constructed, but it’s a bit of a boring party cut. I like the drum rhythm, but overall it sounds dated and doesn’t hold a ton of interest for me. “Indian Girl (An Adult Story)” is another nonsense story told well. It’s saved by the fact that it’s funny to listen to, particularly Rick’s lady voice. I’m not crazy about the slower ballads on this album, and “Teenage Love” is the worst offender. It just drags on and on and on with those 80s synths. I love Rick’s flow on “Mona Lisa”, but this was the breaking point for me on gibberish stories. What even happens in this one? It feels like not much. I don’t know. “Kit (What’s the Scoop” is pretty much just filler. It has a nice drum beat but I’m not invested in the lyrics at all. “Hey Young World” is the better slow track compared to “Teenage Love”, though I’m still not enamored. It has one of the few catchy hooks on the album. Still, it’s a little too laidback and loses my attention. The synths on “Teacher, Teacher” sound a hell of a lot like “Bad” by Michael Jackson”. The chorus is a bit too repetitive; it would be fine if the bars were said once each chorus, but the fact that they’re said twice makes them annoying. “Lick the Balls” is a real letdown because I had high hopes for a Slick Rick track titled, well, “Lick the Balls”. Unfortunately, the phrase feels shoehorned in, another indistinguishable track on the second half of this album.

I have enough love for “Children’s Story” to keep this in C Tier, but only barely. Overall, it’s a weaker record than *Paid in Full*. However, I definitely like it way way better than De La Soul’s *3 Feet High and Rising*, the next most comparable. Hopefully Public Enemy finishes our Golden Age hip hop selection on a high note.

Favorite tracks: Treat Her Like a Prostitute, The Ruler’s Back, Children’s Story, The Moment I Feared, Indian Girl (An Adult Story)

Least favorite track: Teenage Love
