# Write-up

Songs: Ohia was a solo project of Jason Molina, a country musician born in 1973 and raised in Lorain, Ohio, outside of Cleveland. He grew up in a trailer near Lake Erie. Molina learned to play guitar as a child and bass in high school. He graduated from Oberlin College in 1996 and played bass in a few local metal bands before going solo, at which point he changed focus to indie rock-influenced country music. Instead of using his own name, he released these early solo projects under various names that all started with “Songs:”, e.g. “Songs: Albian” and “Songs: Unitas”. The first to stick was “Songs: Ohia”, taken from the name of a Hawaiian tree that sounds like the state of Ohio. Songs: Ohia released its first full-length album, *Songs: Ohia*, in 1997. It received little notice at the time. The same was true of the project’s second and third album, 1998’s *Impala* and 1999’s *The Ghost*. However, 2000 proved to be a small breakthrough for Molina. Songs: Ohia released three albums that year: *The Lioness*, *Protection Spells*, and *Ghost Tropic*. While *Protection Spells* was a solo singer-songwriter album like Molina’s earlier work, the other two albums featured Molina playing electric guitar in front of full backing bands. These latter two albums received strong reviews, with *Ghost Tropic* in particular regarded as an early masterpiece. This minor critical recognition continued with Molina’s eighth full album as Songs: Ohia, 2002’s *Didn’t It Rain*. It featured a shift a toward a fuller, gospel-inflected sound and has also become a fan favorite.

The most successful Songs: Ohia album would also be its last under that name: *The Magnolia Electric Co.*, released in 2003. Molina and his backing band recorded the album at Steve Albini’s studio in Chicago, making it the eighth and final album on our list to involve Albini (in addition to *Atomizer* and *Songs About Fucking* from Albini’s band Big Black, he also produced Nirvana’s *In Utero*, Pixies’ *Surfer Rosa*, Joanna Newsom’s *Ys*, Peter Sotos’ *Buyer’s Market*, and one track on Whitehouse’s *Bird Seed*). This album is our list’s only example of alt-country, also known as y’allternative, a form of country heavily influenced by the culture of punk and alt rock. Though folk punk bands like Violent Femmes and cowpunk groups like Meat Puppets had been working country sounds into punk rock since the early 80s, the origin of alt-country is usually attributed to the 1990 album *No Depression* by Uncle Tupelo. While Uncle Tupelo worked in the spirit of punk and guitar riffs influenced by Minutemen, the music was decidedly country with punk influences and not vice versa. Uncle Tupelo’s Jeff Tweedy would later form the indie rock band Wilco, which had the album *Yankee Hotel Foxtrot* earlier on the list. Alt-country and its sister genre Americana have gone on to produce a number of successful artists. *The Magnolia Electric Co.* received critical praise and is now seen as both Molina’s masterpiece and a high watermark of alt-country.

While touring the album in 2003, Molina decided to abandon the Songs: Ohia name. Instead, his solo project would be known as Magnolia Electric Co. going forward. Magnolia Electric Co. released four albums between 2005 and 2009. Molina also released three albums in this timespan under his real name. All of these albums continued in the musical direction of *The Magnolia Electric Co.* Unfortunately, Molina had been a serious alcoholic since at least 2003, a fact that he hid from even his close friends and family. Molina’s alcoholism began to take a serious toll on his health in 2009, at which point he was forced to stop recording or playing music at the age of 36. After nearly four years spent in rehab, hospitals, and farms, Molina died of organ failure brought on by his drinking in 2013 at the age of 39. Molina’s reputation has only grown since his death, including a tribute album from modern indie rock and alt-country artists released in 2025.

# Hargrove's review

This has been one of the single hardest albums for me to stop myself from listening to as we’ve gone through the list. It’s an unconscionable cruelty that I had to wait until our third-to-last week to be reunited with “Farewell Transmission”. *The Magnolia Electric Co.* starts with one of my ten favorite songs ever, then more or less keeps up that quality for seven more tracks. I can’t think of another album where I struggled more to decide which track was my least favorite. I knew it had to be one of the two songs not sung by Molina, “The Old Black Hen” or “Peoria Lunch Box Blues”, but, fuck, both of those songs rule too. I first heard this album when I was 16, and I realized straight away that I was listening to something special. I had been introduced to plenty of outlaw and alt-country artists as a little kid (Merle Haggard was one of my first concerts), but when I came to this album, country was not a genre that I much associated myself with. For an album this unapologetically twangy and fiddly to be so undeniably, heartrendingly beautiful was a real signal to me about the underlying quality of the music. Over the last ten years, the emotional intensity of this album has only grown for me. I can count on one hand the number of songwriters whose experience with depression, disappointment, existential angst, and all the challenges that come with the beauty of life traces mine so perfectly. I try to avoid comparing albums on this list by different artists with each other too much, particularly when the comparison only springs to mind by coincidence of the albums being chosen in the same week, but I find that my reservations with *“The Glow” Pt. 2* have helped me elucidate what it is about this record that resonates with me so powerfully. While both Elverum and Molina faced legitimate reasons for depression and a bleak outlook, Molina’s poetic language gives an unfathomable, mystic depth to his pain: “Mama, here comes midnight with the dead moon in its jaws/Must be the big star about to fall”. More important, however, is his reaction to this reality. No matter how bleak his outlook, how doomed it all seems to be, how many failures he accrues one after the other, Molina never once, not one time, loses his resolve to keep trying. Whether his exhortation to “Come on, let’s try and know whatever we try/We will be gone, but not forever” on “Farewell Transmission”, his confidence that he’ll make the change on “I’ve Been Riding with the Ghost”, his humor about his flaws on “Just Be Simple”, his reveal that “For once, almost was good enough” on “Almost Was Good Enough”, his pride in his ability to “sing the blues the way [he] find[s] ‘em” on “The Old Black Hen”, or his fiery demands in “John Henry Split My Heart”, Molina always tries. He doesn’t give up. You can’t give up. When you give up, you lose. He ends the album with “Hold on Magnolia”, one final plea not to let go even when the night is darkest. You can’t let go. Even if you’re doomed, there’s virtue in holding on until the end. I’d be lying if I said Molina’s own demise didn’t heighten this for me. I hope he held on through the end. And I’ve done all of this yapping without reference to the instant classic guitar riffs, the beautiful vocal harmonies, the shockingly hooky choruses, and the motion in the drums. It’s damn near perfection.

“Farewell Transmission” starts the album on the highest of notes. It begins so enticingly twangy before Molina’s vocal perfection comes in. The instrumental grows into an immensely satisfying country jam. Molina’s lyricism pulls me so thoroughly into his view of the world: grim but determined, not optimistic but tragically hopeful, defiant in the face of cosmic depression and certain death. When the chorus finally comes in after nearly three minutes, it intensifies everything, adding new voices to show he’s not alone. It’s remarkable how little this song changes over its seven-minute run time without losing my full attention one but. The drums are a big part of keeping it exciting, as well as Molina’s delivery. I’m endlessly enraptured by both his vivid imagery and how smoothly he pieces together figurative language: There ain't no end to the sands I've been tryin' to cross/The real truth about it is/My kind of life's no better off/It's got the map or if it's lost”, ““Real truth about it is/There ain't no end to the desert I'll cross/I’ve really known it all along”, “Real truth about it is/No one gets it right/Real truth about it is/We're all supposed to try”. On the surface, it’s one of the most depressing songs I know; whenever I listen, however, it locks in my resolve like nothing else. After all of that, the two-minute outro somehow becomes the track’s best part. I love how the instrumental drops out one-by-one, leaving Molina and company alone with the thumping of the drums. It’s hard to compete with “Farewell Transmission”, but Molina keeps both his vocals and lyrics at an elite level through the album. The choral vocals sound genuinely ghostly on “I’ve Been Riding with the Ghost”, an effect that makes this song strongly stand out. I love when the drums kick in and the guitar picks up. My favorite line is ““I'm running out of things/I didn't even know I was using”, though I think “And while you've been busy/Learning how to complain/I’ve been busy learning/How to make the change/Now I made the change“ best expresses its spirit. “Just Be Simple” contains another one of my all-time favorite lines in “And everything you hated me for…/Honey, there was so much more/I just didn't get busted”. Molina reiterates his resolve at the end with “I ain't looking for that easy way out/This whole life it's been about/Try and try and try/Try and try and try/To be simple again”. It’s the slowest track on the album’s first half, but it works perfectly with that anthemic chorus and the slowly driving drums. I particularly love the bridge, which drive sup the intensity if not the tempo. “Almost Was Good Enough” picks the pace back up before Molina’s short break. The guitar chords create an ominous, somber atmosphere, driven by a kind of drone. Molina shifts into an angrier gear here, talk-singing through the thickness of the guitar attack. Though he laments how “I remember when it didn't used to be so hard/It used to be impossible” and “Did you really believe?/C’mon, did you really believe/That everyone makes it out?/Almost no one makes it out”, he surprises me at the end by discovering that “For once, almost was good enough”.

While “The Old Black Hen” and “Peoria Lunch Box Blues” both suffer from a lack of Molina’s vocals, I still like both quite a bit. In the former track, the male bass vocalist adds in a connection to more classic country and gives the lyrics heft. I’ve always strongly identified with Churchill’s description of his depression as a “black dog”; the “old black hen” here plays a similar role. I find the description of intergenerational depression with “Now sing it over the cradle of the child who's born next/Bad Luck Lullaby/Leave all the truth in so they know what comes next/With the Bad Luck Lullaby/Leave in the true love they never find/Show how they're looking for it all of their lives“ deeply moving. I also love the fiddle. “Peoria Lunch Box Blues” brings in an angelic female voice that I like a little better. The drums are slow but not lethargic, slowly building the rest of the instrumental around the way they dance. It’s hazy and downtempo and beautiful. I have to pick one of these two to be my least favorite, and I reluctantly must go with “The Old Black Hen”. I’ve never paid much attention to “John Henry Split My Heart” before, but I’m absolutely loving it this time around. It comes in with such force and intensity after the haziness of the previous track; you can really hear Molina’s heavy metal roots here. The short piano interlude at the beginning of the vocals creates an unreal amount of suspense before the guitar comes roaring back in. I love the lyrical concept but, more than that, I love how Molina sells it, booming “Swing the heaviest hammer you got/Hit this one out of the park/John Henry split my heart”. The final reprise of “Farewell Transmission” is one of my favorite moments on the album. In fitting with everything I love about this album, it ends on simultaneously its darkest and most hopeful note, “Hold on Magnolia”. As everything falls apart, Molina begs us to hold on, no matter how quickly the end approaches. Though the music starts slow, it only grows in power and intensity to end the album on its best foot.

I’ve yapped long enough. I love everything about this album. Just go listen to it. I rarely feel as invigorated as I do after a listen to *The Magnolia Electric Co.*

Favorite tracks: Farewell Transmission, I’ve Been Riding with the Ghost, Just Be Simple, Almost Was Good Enough, Peoria Lunch Box Blues, John Henry Split My Heart, Hold on Magnolia

Least favorite track: The Old Black Hen
