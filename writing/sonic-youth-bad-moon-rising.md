# Write-up

Our second of two Sonic Youth albums on the list, Bad Moon Rising is one of only two to feature their first drummer, Bob Bert, the other being their debut, Confusion Is Sex. The album took its name from the Creedence Clearwater Revival song, frequently interpreted to be about the political climate of the late 60s (although CCR denies that the song is explicitly about Nixon, who was inaugurated shortly before the song was written). Sonic Youth adopted the name for their own criticism of the times, the middle of the Reagan era. Most of the pieces in the album were built off of small snippets the band had developed in their live shows to kill time between breaks. The albums samples Lou Reed’s Metal Machine Music between the songs “Brave Men Run” and “Society Is A Hole”. The final song, "Death Valley '69" was a collaboration between the band and Lydia Lurch, who was the lead singer of no wave band Teenage Jesus and the Jerks. It is mixed by JG Thrilwell, a frequent collaborator of Lurch. He has also released albums under the name “Foetus” including one album on this list. The album was much better received than their debut. That album's mixed critical reaction caused lead singer Thurston Moore to rename one song "I Killed Christgau with My Big Fucking Dick" as part of a beef with local music critic and Dartmouth alumni Robert Christgau. The reception to the album was especially warm in Europe, where there was far more interest in experimental music, and they even got a collaborator to found a label specifically to distribute the music in the UK. The album caused the band's drummer, Bob Bert, to quiet after he got tired of playing the album live. The addition of Steve Shelley would allow the band to embark on its most productive period, releasing EVOL, Sister, Daydream Nation and Goo, four of their most beloved albums.

# Hargrove's review

This was a transitional album and you could certainly feel the growing pains at times, but overall I was rather happy with it. I had never actually listened to early Sonic Youth, though my understanding was that it was similarly aggressive and abrasive to early Swans. While that turned out to be true to some extent, overall I think it’s a really limited understanding of what this album is and what it does well. Even when Sonic Youth attacks you with sharp guitars and unrelenting dissonance, it always feels like there is something left in the tank, some level of restraint to keep cool. You can feel the slacker ethos even back then in a try-hard scene. On top of that, tracks like “Brave Men Run (In My Family” and especially “Death Valley ’69” show the first glimpses of their pop songwriting ability and make it feel like their ascent to the big leagues was pre-destined from the start. Through it all, the album keeps a remarkably consistent atmosphere of dark Americana via relentless unresolved dissonance, simmering feedback, and ominous noise. It definitely has its problems, chief among them Thurston’s clunky lyricism. “Society Is a Hole” in particular makes me roll my eyes every time. I also thought the “tuning” interlude sections went on too long at times. But for what it is, I was pretty happy with it. I definitely found it enjoyable. And I will definitely keep listening to “Death Valley ’69”.

“Intro” kicks the album off with some pretty nice and twinkly guitars by Sonic Youth standards at the time. I really enjoy the central play on words in “Brave Men Run (In My Family)” as well as the nice guitar work in the first couple of minutes. It’s a song that strikes the perfect balance of noisy and melodic. “Society Is a Hole” finds Thurston employing a Michael Gira-esque cadence to delivery some very stupid lines. “Society is a hole/It makes me lie to my friends/It's running down my street/With white powers sneakers/On the beautiful beat of black feet/Society is a hole”? Very subtle! “We're living in pieces/I want to live in peace”? What a flip! The music itslef is also kind of bleh outside of the non-drum metallic percussion. I really like the ominous and spooky intro of “I Love Her All the Time”. The track alternates between two sections, the first containing an uneasy and tense quiet guitar line, the second releasing a fiery guitar to play over tribal drums. Both sections are great and interact with each other well. The lyrics on “Ghost Bitch” aren’t stellar by any means, but Kim starts to sound like Bob Dylan after hearing Thurston for a while. I enjoy the song’s final third after a mechanical beat kicks in, but the ethereal section in the beginning goes on too long. After more eerie feedback, “I’m Insane” brings in awesomely pounding drums and church bells to create a perfectly hypnotic boom chuk groove to set the stage for some of the creepiest guitar work yet and Thurston’s finest vocal performance. It’s a great track. “Justice Is Might” seems like it’s going in the same direction at first, but unfortunately squanders it to detour into aimlessness and formlessness. Thankfully, the absolute BANGER “Death Valley ’69” ends the album on its highest note. From the fantastic Jah Wobble-esque bass line, to Lydia Lunch’s heavenly vocals, to the blistering guitar work, this track gives us the first real glimpse into the delightfully weird pop Sonic Youth would become known for.

I don’t want to oversell my opinion here. It’s an inconsistent album, and certainly no *Daydream Nation*. But, man, is it better than a record released by this band at this point has any right to be. I listened to all of SY’s discography leading up to *Bad Moon Rising*, and hopefully soon I’ll give *EVOL* and *Sister* listens as well.

Favorite tracks: Brave Men Run (In My Family), I Love Her All the Time, I’m Insane, Death Valley ’69

Least favorite track: Society Is a Hole

# Aidan's review

Coming right before 3 of my favorite albums in EVOL, Sister and Daydream Nation, I'm mildly surprised that I haven't spend the time listening to Bad Moon Rising before. Listening to it now, I am more convinced that Bob Bert was the missing piece that this band needed. My favorite Sonic Youth songs are the ones where they take a pop melody, slowly build up a mass of feedback and chaos around it only to seemingly wrangle all of that chaos back into one final bust of energy. There's none of that here, and while there are some excellent songs in "Death Valley '69" and "Brave Men Run (In My Family)", the stuff they bookend is too often formless and meandering from a band that has so many hard-driving songs. Compared to their next few albums, it's like an Australopithecus, technically interesting and important to the evolution of the band, but I prefer the finished model.
