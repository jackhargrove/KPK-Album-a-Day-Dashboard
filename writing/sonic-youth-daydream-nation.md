# Write-up

Sonic Youth is perhaps the single band that has done the most to define what indie rock is in America. The band got its start in the New York No Wave scene where Thurston Moore met his future wife Kim Gordon. At the same time, Moore met electric guitar composer Glen Branca (whose album The Ascension is on the list) and joined his orchestra, meeting fellow guitarist Lee Ranaldo. Other notable figures who touched the scene at the time include Swans frontman Michael Gira as well as the duo Suicide (an influence more than a contributor) and Brian Eno, who made the definitive compilation album No Wave New York. Ranaldo, Gordon and Moore quickly started their own band and after cycling through several drummers including Bob Bert, who played on their sophomore album Bad Moon Rising(which is on the list), landed on Steve Shelley. The band's initial sound was heavily influenced by the no wave scene, and was heavily abrasive. Most critics loved them and most listeners couldn't stomach their sound, but they quickly became a favorite of artists including Neil Young, who called "Madonna, Sean and Me" on EVOL his favorite song. During the time, the band was also jumping from label to label, first leaving Branca's own label for Black Flag's SST due to difficulties with royalties, before leaving SST for the same reasons. Daydream Nation was released under Enigma Records, a subsidiary of the major label EMI. The album was a massive breakthrough, garnering critical praise and popular recognition, but failures in distirbution left the band frustrated again, so they jumped ship for Geffen records, where they stayed for the next 16 years. Kim Gordon was instrumental in convincing a small Seattle band called Nirvana to follow them to Geffen, giving Kurt Cobain a phone call to assure him of his creative freedom on the record.

Daydream Nation was Sonic Youth's longest album at the time, with a more jam-heavy sound, but featured much of what the band had been known for, long songs inspired by authors such as William S. Burroughs and Phillip K. Dick, and unorthodox guitar tunings. The guitar tunings started as a way to stand out as a low budget band, with one author writing "[Sonic Youth] could only afford cheap guitars, and cheap guitars sounded like cheap guitars. But with weird tunings or something jammed under a particular fret, those humble instruments could sound rather amazing – bang a drum stick on a cheap Japanese Stratocaster copy in the right tuning, crank the amplifier to within an inch of its life and it will sound like church bells," but grew into their signature sound. The record was produced by Nick Sansano, who had previously only worked on rap albums, including with Public Enemy, whose release It Takes a Nation of Millions to Hold Us Back was the album that beat out Daydream Nation for the Pazz and Jop Poll album of the year. Daydream Nation, along with Double Nickels on the Dime by the Minutemen and Zen Arcade by Husker Du, became the founding text on which the American indie rock scene developed its sound. There's a movie called Daydream Nation, a band called Eric's trip and a song titled "I wanna be your Thurston Moore". The band went on to fully "sell out", and in doing so provided a road map for contemporaries to do so, but they were fastidious about keeping their creative independence. Likewise, the public image of the band, simultaneously too cool to care while also releasing such political songs as "Youth against Fascism" was enormously influential on their peers.

# Hargrove's review

I’m very familiar with Sonic Youth as a band and their history, but I’ve never actually listened to *Daydream Nation* which is a bit of an embarrassment for me. When I was listening, I knew I had heard “Teen Age Riot” before and I assumed it was just from osmosis as a kid, but then I realized it was on the Rock Band 2 core list of songs and I used to shred it as a kid. I immediately knew that “Teen Age Riot” was a top 5 song we had listened to so far, it’s just indie rock perfection. The melody is fantastic, the guitars get unleashed at the end of every bar, and the vibe is immaculate. I struggled with the other tracks on my first few listens. They’re a little too jammy, a little too noodly, a little too unstructured for my taste, especially given that the album opens with “Teen Age Riot”. In my opinion, I think this album has a sequencing issue. “Teen Age Riot”, as the structured pop perfection that came out of Sonic Youth’s years of jamming, should have come nearer to the end of the album instead of at the start. As it is now, you’re introduced to the outcome followed by an hour of progression. I think it would be better if you got the hour of progression and then got the outcome at the end. That’s a minor quibble though. I gave this one a lot of listens, and the later songs for the most part really grew on me. There’s still a few, particularly on side 2 (of 4, it’s a double album) that I don’t love. I also tend to find this kind of aimless jamming less pleasant on a double album. Overall though, really enjoying this album now!

Everything about “Teen Age Riot” is perfect. The beginning is such a pleasant introduction to both the instrumental sound of the album, as well as to Kim Gordon’s vocals and personality. All of a sudden, the song picks up and the guitar goes on a generational run with one of the greatest repeated 6 bar riffs in rock history. Thurston Moore also gives one of his finest vocal performances on the album, sounding genuinely like a disaffected slacker. And the drums keep the pace driving, something interesting about this whole album to me is just how high the tempo is on so many tracks. “‘Cross the Breeze” is another song that really stood out to me as one of the best. It’s kind of like a bizzaro bluegrass track instrumentally, and Kim just kills the vocals (she wants to know, goddammit!). Drums are tribal and stellar as well. “Candle” is my favorite on side 3 and potentially my second favorite on the album after “Teen Age Riot”. It sounds to me like Sonic Youth’s take on post-hardcore (although you can hear plenty of hardcore influence on this record, particularly in the tempos), and the guitar intro and interludes throughout the song are haunting and almost achieve emo. There’s also a chord change in the chorus I really like. “Kissability” is another great Kim vocals track where the tempo and guitar feedback just drive it forward relentlessly. The back and forth between two chords throughout also works really well. All of “Trilogy” at the end is great, but “B) Hyperstation” is my favorite leg. It’s rumbling, it’s growling, it lurches forward slowly at the beginning. The chorus melody is laid back, and he says the title of the album!

I think this album’s strength lies in the mellow, unfazed attitude it puts over the same emotions you get in punk and hardcore. It’s very clear at many points throughout the album how it grew out of punk, but you just never get the same aggression that you do in punk. Instead, there’s more of a slow burn, the above-it-all scoff of a young adult instead of the angry sneer of an adolescent. Kim’s vocals on the beginning of “The Sprawl” sound cooler than anyone else in history has ever sounded. The guitars on “Silver Rocket” are loud and distorted, but they can never find a groove, always searching for the right feeling instead of settling into anger. “Hey Joni” is one of the most aggressive tracks, but all the breaks between the vocals have the most beautiful, contemplative instrumentals.

There are some songs on this album I do not love, and also some parts of songs that I don’t care for even if I like the rest of the song. The breakdown in “Silver Rocket” is kind of annoying. “The Sprawl” goes on just a little too long, although it’s growing on me with every listen. The drums on “Eric’s Trip” are a little overbearing, and Thurston sounds too much like he’s doing a Lou Reed impression. “Providence” is kind of funny, but also kind of feels like a waste of time interlude on an album that’s already too long.

With each subsequent listen though, my complaints get quieter and my enjoyment grows. If I wrote this after first or even second listen, the theme of this review would be “‘Teen Age Riot’ is perfect but the rest is kind of annoying”, but the number of songs I find annoying has dropped by like 75%. I very much enjoy that perfect balance between aggression and subtlety, I think they kind of nailed it there, and I think that’s exactly why it’s endured so well.

Favorite tracks: Teen Age Riot, ‘Cross the Breeze, Hey Joni, Candle, Kissability, B)  Hyperstation, Z) Eliminator Jr.

Least favorite track: Eric’s Trip

# Aidan's review

I love this album. On a purely emotional level it might not be my absolute favorite (but it's damn close), but most of the albums I really love have Daydream Nation deep in their DNA. The entire project of indie rock can be bluntly summarized as a thousand bands trying to remake Daydream Nation in their own way. The album opens with Teen Age Riot, the most popular song in the entire Sonic Youth discography. There's hardly a better song to represent what Sonic Youth stands for, the worship of youth, violence and music, put over some of the best guitar playing you've ever heard. You can call it a betrayal of their outsider bona fides that they made a song with a chorus, but who cares when it sounds like this. The steady 4/4 beat, that incredible riff to open the song, and then they just play the same riff over and over again, like a call to prayer at the altar of President J Mascis. Silver Rocket follows it up with the breakneck pace that characterizes the rest of the album, as well as the cacophony halfway through that harkens back to the band's noise rock roots. Their ability to transfer from complete control to utter chaos and back, as well as their usage of feedback to fill voids are on display here. The Sprawl is the first proper Kim Gordon vocals on the album and in my opinion is one of the weaker songs on the album. Sonic Youth was never a lyrically strong band, relying on a general evocation of ideas rather than any sort of clever wordplay. Most punk bands would not dream of putting out "You got a stupid man, you got a Ku Klux Klan" (from a later album) as a radio single, and The Sprawl's basic putdown of 80s consumer culture falls flat when it's been done a thousand times by wittier and more persuasive people. The most interesting part about the song is Gordon's opening lines. "Skirts and cheap nylon slips/I've gone native" takes an ethnographer's lens to feminism and is smarter than the next 7 minutes of the song put together.

'Cross the Breeze opens with 2:30 of trance-like guitars, and it's incredible how much energy Steve Shelley can convey on a pretty mid-tempo steady beat. It's only when the song picks up nearly 5 minutes in that you realize that they could pick up the tempo even more, and when it dissolves into a tight jam right after that, it's perfection. This restraint to simply let the moment sit is what made this album so perfect for Sonic Youth, the point in their careers where they had the creativity to churn out incredible moments on guitar tunings you've never heard of, and the maturity to let you hear and understand it. Eric's Trip is the first Lee Ranaldo song, and it's remarkably coherent and light by his standards, as he somehow makes his spoken word style fit perfectly with the oscillating background guitars. The guitar nearly act more like traditional vocals than Ranaldo's singing in the second half, with his constant staccato lyrics contrasting with the whirring and flailing guitar. Total Trash harkens back to the punk inspiration of the band, with a more steady guitar and hammered chords resulting in a relatively simple opening, before halfway into the song where another tangling and untangling occurs, then back to your regularly occurring programming. I'm fascinated by how big the recording space sounds during these sections, the guitars sound like they echo, when the song resumes, it sounds far more distant and half hearted, as if the ghosts of the middle section are still floating around the music.

Hey Joni is inspired by rock standard Hey Joe (best known version being Hendrix's), with the titular Joni being Joni (McGary) Mitchell. It starts out as the fastest song on the album, and then gets even faster after they "kick it", as Sonic Youth's control over tempo is again showcased. There's also hints of the cyberpunk influence on the whole album, with lines like "in this broken town can you still jack in" and "A snap of electric whip crack" showing the influence of Burroughs and more specifically Gibson, whose debut Neuromancer, which took the world of the Beats and put a futuristic sheen on it, was just 4 years old at the time. Providence marks the end of the best 45 minutes of music Sonic Youth ever put out, and is a fitting coda, as a simple work that combines the static of an amp, a tinny piano piece and a phone call by Minutemen bassist Mike Watt.

I find the remainder of the album far less interesting. In any other context, the last 25 minutes of the album would be a triumph, but after such a start, it's a disappointing finish. Candle has shades of Sonic Youth's later work, and relies to heavily on lyrics, which I think is the weakest aspect of the band. Rain King is a pretty typical Ranaldo piece. He's certainly the most influenced by literature out of the group, and serves up a proper Beat piece. It's faster than most of his songs, and benefits from that, but his typical shock factor is saved for the last lines of the song. Kissability is my favorite song of the second half, as well as my favorite Gordon song on this record. It has wit, it has tempo, and her feminism skewers the skeeviness of celebrity sex appeal quite well. The racing guitars are given room to breathe again and I think this probably the single best vocal performance on the album. Gordon oozes sex and danger, simultaneously encompassing the role of the victim and perpetrator the way I think very few other vocalists could do. The closing trilogy is nothing special, Moore's lyrics are effective at evoking a general vibe and I'm partial to Gordon's punch delivery, but as a whole, it lacks the sheer creativity of the rest of the album.

I know this all comes off as relatively harsh for an album that I've said is among my all time favorites, but these are the little things that grate on me after 30 or 40 listens. I really appreciate the ambition of this album, it's a lot easier to make 30 minutes of perfect music than an hour, especially on an album that does so much.
