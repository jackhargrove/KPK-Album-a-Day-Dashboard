# Write-up

Walter “Sonny” Rollins is tenor sax player born in 1930 and raised in Harlem. His parents had moved to NYC from the Virgin Islands. He learned piano at a young age, followed by alto sax at age seven and tenor sax at age sixteen. The tenor sax became Rollins’ primary instrument. He became a professional musician upon graduating from high school in 1948, starting as a sideman in exciting new bebop ensembles. Rollins recorded albums with such musicians as Bud Powell, Miles Davis, Charlie Parker, and Thelonious Monk in the late 40s and early 50s, helping pioneer the transition from bebop to hard bop. Like many of those guys, Rollins developed a serious heroin habit. He also served ten months at Rikers Island for armed robbery in 1950. In 1955, Rollins became a patient at a prison hospital in Kentucky. While there, he became one of the first patients to try methadone to treat his heroin addiction, which worked. Rollins was briefly a member of Miles Davis’ quintet in 1955, but he left and was replaced by John Coltrane, a substitution that began the First Great Quintet. Still, Rollins found success on his own, striking up a professional partnership with legendary drummer Max Roach (whose album *We Insist!* we listened to last year) in 1956.

In 1957, Rollins released his most famous work, *Saxophone Colossus*. It featured Rollins as bandleader on tenor sax, Roach on drums, Tommy Flanagan (who would later become notable for his playing on John Coltrane’s “Giant Steps”) on piano, and Doug Watkins on bass. It was Rollins’ sixth album as a bandleader. The group recorded at Rudy Van Gelder’s studio in New Jersey. The opener “St. Thomas”, featuring one of the most well-known melodies ever, became a jazz standard. While the record label forced Rollins to claim songwriting credit, the melody was in fact taken from a nursery rhyme in the Virgin Islands that Rollins’ mother sang to him, which itself was based off a Caribbean calypso from the Bahamas. Calypsos subsequently became popular in American jazz. “You Don’t Know What Love Is” is a standard that originated in a 1941 Abbott & Costello film and has been played by many artists. “Moritat” is a version of a composition that is better known in the US as “Mack the Knife”. It was originally written by composer Kurt Weill for German playwright Bertolt Brecht’s 1928 musical *The Threepenny Opera* (Brecht wrote lyrics, but they are omitted from this rendition). “Mack the Knife” was popularized in American jazz thanks to a 1955 version by Louis Armstrong, followed by a 1959 version by crooner Bobby Darin that topped the Billboard Hot 100. Rollins’ 1957 rendition falls between those two landmark recordings. “Strode Rode” and “Blue 7” are both original compositions by Rollins. The album was a critical success, increasing Rollins’ profile and helping usher in the hard bop era. The Library of Congress added it to its National Recording Registry in 2016.

Rollins went on to have a long and influential career. He pioneered a form of trio that was just sax, bass, and drums, notably excluding piano. He befriended a young Ornette Coleman in 1957, who would later use ensembles with no chordal instrument to develop his avant-garde and free jazz. Rollins famously took a two-year sabbatical from professional music from 1959-1961. He lived in the LES in Manhattan and began practicing sax for 15+ hours a day on the Williamsburg Bridge, becoming an NYC fixture. The plot of land he lived in during this time now hosts an apartment building named The Rollins. After returning to professional music, Rollins kept to the forefront of jazz, playing avant-garde in the 60s and fusion in the 70s. He also maintained prominence in the mainstream, playing *The Tonight Show* in 1979 and contributing sax to three tracks on the Rolling Stones’ 1981 album *Tattoo You*. He won Grammys in 2001 and 2006. Rollins’ health forced him to retire from music in 2014, but he is still alive today at age 95.

# Hargrove's review

This was the most standard, straightforward, and moderately above average jazz album we’ve had in some time. It is a perfectly fine, often fun record. It’s uncomplicated, no frills, just five tracks of swing, melody, and solos. I found it a little bit refreshing honestly. This isn’t meant as a diss on any previous jazz records, it’s just that this was my preconceived notion of how jazz sounded before starting this project, and it was nice to have some fun with that with all the knowledge I’ve accumulated. Whether standard or original, *Saxophone Colossus* comes full of catchy and memorable melodies, from the “oh that’s where that’s from” calypso of “St. Thomas”, to the driving bebop of “Strode Rode”, to the evergreen beauty of “Mack the Knife”, uh, I mean, “Moritat”. Rollins is a great soloist, often finding his groove on the medium-slow side of things. His solos aren’t terribly ambitious, but I do consistently enjoy them. Flanagan puts down a few good solos on his piano too, though I can’t get his struggling on Coltrane’s “Giant Steps” out of my head whenever I hear him play. My biggest issue with this record is that I just don’t like Roach that much. *We Insist!* is my least favorite jazz album I’ve heard to this point, but I really did try to give him another shot as a sideman. I love drumming as a concept, and he’s allegedly quite gifted. But it just doesn’t connect with me. He and I are on completely different wavelengths. Every time he pops in, he just plays…not what I want to hear. It’s hard to explain. Sometimes you just don’t connect with an artist. Outside of that, I liked this one quite a bit. Just some solid hard bop.

Don’t you just love it when you put on a jazz album for the first time and you instantly are able to place some melody that’s always been in your head? I had that experience had with “St. Thomas”. I don’t know whether I played it in band, or if I’ve just heard it in a movie or TV show, but it felt ingrained within me like “Happy Birthday” or “Old MacDonald Had a Farm”. And it is so Goddamn catchy! It’s like a pretty long melodic line, but I haven’t been able to stop humming it all week. Rollins delivers two perfect medium tempo solos, and Flanagan has a decent one on his piano. The biggest downside is that they give Roach 40 fucking bars to do a drum solo. That part is a bit hard to get through. But when the head recapitulates, it’s all okay. I really like the slow and delicate “You Don’t Know What Love Is”. It’s one of those jazz tracks that transports me to the dirtiest, grimiest dive bar you can imagine in 50s NYC as the rain falls in the night. I actually like Roach’s slow and quiet drum rhythm, not to mention the beautiful sax playing and tasteful piano. The whole thing is a vibe. “Strode Rode” starts off with a fun, catchy melody and keeps it up with a driving swing pace. It’s the most bebopping track on the album by far. Watkins’ bass goes insane, while Flanagan nails his best solo on the record. And Roach again impresses with his workhorse galloping pace. I have loved “Mack the Knife” ever since we played it in my middle school band. It’s just such a great melody, with fun lyrics too once you get into those. As such, I was pretty stoked to recognize it in “Moritat” upon first listen. Unfortunately, this song stretches out over 10 minutes when it would have been perfect in 5 or 6. Rollins and Flanagan both put down replacement level solos, but the dagger is Roach’s stupid drum interjections into Rollins’ sax line and the awkward, overlong bass solo. But man, I love that melody though. It would have been perfect without the fluff. “Blue 7” is just as long but without the benefit of being a time-tested ditty. It’s so low-key that it feels it doesn’t want to draw any attention to itself. Roach has his worst solo of the album. It’s like he’s never even touched drumsticks before! He sounds clueless! At least the bass solo is much better than the previous track’s. Still, it’s an unfortunate way to close a pretty good album.

I complained about Roach a lot, but it was really a small part of this. It’s a good record and I enjoyed it a lot. It’s nice to have a jazz album I can throw into B Tier without much thought like so many rock albums.

Favorite tracks: St. Thomas, You Don’t Know What Love Is, Strode Rode

Least favorite track: Blue 7
