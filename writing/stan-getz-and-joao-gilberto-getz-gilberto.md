# Write-up


# Hargrove's review

When we started this list, I had listened to approximately five jazz albums. I was as vaguely familiar with the genre and its standards as any self-respecting American, but far from an aficionado. A year-and-a-half and thirty jazz albums later, I’ve been taken on a journey through just about every style of jazz since the 50s. From the fiery and soulful improvisation of hard bop, the melodicism of cool jazz, the boundary breaking of avant-garde jazz, the indescribably insanity of free jazz, the triumphant meditations of spiritual jazz, the mind-bending grooves of jazz fusion, and everything in between, I’ve heard a lot. And in a beautiful bit of serendipity, we’ve ended our journey through jazz with one of its loveliest albums sonically, *Getz/Gilberto*. From beginning to end, this thing just sounds so smooth. I know it isn’t cool jazz per se, but it certainly works off of the same ideas. I love the syncopation of João Gilberto’s guitar and the high-pitched crooning of his unique voice, Getz’s adventurous but accessible soloing, Jobim’s excellent songwriting and tastefully spare piano, and Astrud Gilberto’s ethereal contributions. There’s not a moment on this short record that doesn’t sound good. And yet, that right there is also the problem. As far as I can tell, bossa nova sticks to strict and narrow genre conventions. Jobim’s songwriting talent helps keep the tracks distinct for a while, but it still succumbs to an almost dreary uniformity by the end. That’s not easy for an eight track, 33-minute album to do. Ultimately, it’s fine; I have little desire to dig into much other bossa nova, but I certainly have a desire to keep listening to this album. It does make this feel like more of a novelty than a serious work of art at times, though. Still, at the end of the day, I’m not putting an album with “The Girl from Ipanema” on it below B Tier. Let’s be real.

One of the cool things about this list is that I would have never known the source of “that song where the lady says ‘Ipanema’” otherwise. I just wouldn’t have thought of it. And yet, it feels like I’ve listened to “The Girl from Ipanema” a million times. I like João Gilberto’s singing, but Astrud Gilberto’s vocals blow him out of the water. She’s otherworldly. Getz provides an expressive solo where he goes all over the place while still keeping the core of the melody. Jobim’s piano solo is more tasteful still. I love bossa nova’s take on scatting, and “Doralice” includes my favorite examples about that. I also like the bass pulse of this track quite a bit. It’s nice to have a track like this that’s so upbeat and driving. I like the voal melody of “Para machucar meu coração” a good bit, but I feel this track gives  outsize prominence to a mediocre Getz performance. The opening of “Desafinado” sounds almost indistinguishable from the ending of the last track, showing the limited range of this sound already. Between the two tracks, though, I’ll easily take this one. It takes some odd chord detours, getting unexpectedly dark at points. Getz also delivers a much better sax solo. Astrud Gilberto returns on “Corcovado (Quiet Nights of Quiet Stars)” sounding just as lovely as she did the first time around. Jobim’s piano softly underscores her voice in the beginning before delivering a fantastic solo later on. He’s there exactly when you need him. After how strong the songwriting has been to this point, I find “Só danço samba” disappointing. It lacks the longing and melodrama that fives the rest of the album weight. It was already fun, we didn’t need a stupid silly one! The sax melody and chord progression of “O grande amor” remind me of *A Charlie Brown Christmas*. They have the same icy melancholy you get from that record. Whatever it is, I love it, and it finally creates somewhat of a different feel to the earlier tracks. “Vivo sonhando” is fine, but there are just better songs on the album. At the end of the day, there are only so many directions you can go with this sound.

In addition to listening to this record, I also re-listened to six other jazz records that I found comparably chill just to sense out where this one falls within that ranking. Cannonball Adderley’s *Somethin’ Else* remains at the very bottom. It’s just not a good album at all. Next up is Ryo Fukui’s *Scenery*, which largely passes me by outside of the riveting “Early Summer”. In the next tier comes Dave Brubeck’s *Time Out*, Bill Evans’ *Waltz for Debby*, and Grant Green’s *Idle Moments*. Frankly, I’m having a seriously difficult time ranking the three of them. The highest highs come from Brubeck while the greatest consistency comes from Green. Evans sits directly in the middle on both scales. To complicate matters, I also think *Getz/Gilberto* belongs roughly on this tier. Ultimately, I’m going Green > Brubeck > Evans > *Getz/Gilberto*, but I cannot emphasize enough how extremely close they all are. They all basically take up the same line in the middle of B Tier. And at the top, of course, is Vince Guaraldi’s *A Charlie Brown Christmas*. What a banger. Jazz, I’ve had a great time entering you.

Favorite tracks: The Girl from Ipanema, Doralice, Desafinado, Corcovado (Quiet Nights of Quiet Stars), O grande amor

Least favorite track: Só danço samba
