# Write-up

Sufjan Stevens is a musical artist born in 1975 and raised in northern Michigan.  Though he is usually thought of as a major part of the 00s and 10s indie folk scene, he releases music in a wide variety of styles, as evidenced by today’s album. He is also known for his distinctive given name, which is Arabic thanks to his white parents being spiritual hippies. Stevens’ parents separated when he was young because of his mother Carrie’s schizophrenia and bipolar disorder; he was primarily raised by his father and stepmom in Michigan, but he would spend summers with his mother and his stepdad Lowell in Oregon. He recorded his first album, a standard folk album titled *A Sun* Came, as a college senior in 1998. He and his stepfather Lowell formed the independent label Asthmatic Kitty in 1999 to release the album. That same year, Stevens moved to NYC for grad school, earning an MFA in creative writing. His second album, 2001’s *Enjoy Your Rabbit*, made a hard shift from the folk of his debut into harsh glitch electronic music. In 2003, he released *Michigan*, a concept album dedicated to his home state that radically changed style again back to folk, but this time a chamber folk with lush, orchestral arrangement. *Michigan* was Stevens’ breakthrough, earning critical acclaim and selling 27,000 copies (which is not a lot, but is way more than his first two albums). It inspired him to announce the Fifty States Project, his plan to release an album for every US state in the style of *Michigan*; this outlandish, obviously impossible promise generated a ton of buzz and is still something he is generally known for. After a detour into minimal, sad folk on 2004’s underrated *Seven Swans*, Stevens began work on his second state album: 2005’s *Illinois*. A maximalist work of chamber folk that makes use of full orchestras and paints a complex lyrical portrait of the state, *Illinois* is Stevens’ best-known album and “Chicago” his best-known song. Its massive critical and commercial success rocketed him to indie darling status, beating out Kanye West’s *Late Registration* as Pitchfork’s 2005 album of the year.

Despite his newfound success, Stevens was relatively quiet over the next five years. In 2006, he released a compilation album of *Illinois* extras entitled *The Avalanche* and a box set of 42 Christmas songs, both classic and original, called*Songs for Christmas*. He had veered way off the beaten path by 2007, creating a three-hour classical ode to the Brookly-Queens Expressway. As the music press became impatient, Stevens finally admitted in 2009 that the Fifty States Project was a gimmick and he would not be completing it. Combined with an extended illness, he unexpectedly found himself at a mental low point. He returned in 2010 with the *All Delighted People* EP, a return to form that saw him experimenting but remaining within folk. Only two months later, he released *The Age of Adz*, his first full album since *Illinois* five years earlier. While it retained the maximalist character, its sound was radically different: glitchy electronic beats that hearkened back to his second album *Enjoy Your Rabbit*, introspective lyrical themes, the sprawling 25-minute “Impossible Soul” as its closer, autotune??? What the hell? The album was another critical and commercial success, solidifying his eclectic and virtuosic reputation. The album’s cover is based on the work of schizophrenic visual artist Royal Robertson, whose ravings also supplied the title and informed the lyrics. “I Want to Be Well” expresses frustration at his long illness in 2009, while “Get Real Get Right” explores religious themes typical of Stevens’ work. Kendrick Lamar sampled “All For Myself” as the beat of “Hood Politics” on his 2015 album *To Pimp a Butterfly*.

After writing another symphonic classical piece and making a second Christmas album, Stevens released his next studio album *Carrie & Lowell* in 2015 to yet more acclaim. Musically, it returned to the sparse, minimalist folk of *Seven Swans*, while the lyrics explored his feelings following the death of his mother. Fans today still debate whether*Illinois*, *The Age of Adz*, or *Carrie & Lowell* is his best album. He wrote multiple original songs for the 2017 film *Call Me by Your Name*, earning him an Oscar nomination for Best Original Song and comparisons to Elliott Smith, who had received the same nod exactly 20 years earlier for *Good Will Hunting*. His eighth and ninth albums, 2020’s *The Ascension* and 2021’s *Convocations*, were the first to receive mixed reception from his fanbase in nearly 20 years. However, he released *Javelin*in 2023, an album that some put on the same level as his three masterpiece albums. It was dedicated to the memory of his recently deceased partner Evans Richardson. This was the first public confirmation that Stevens is gay, something long speculated by fans. In 2024, his album *Illinois* was turned into a Broadway musical and won a Tony. As perhaps the most unpredictable and creative artist in modern indie, many are excited to see what he’ll do next.

# Hargrove's review

I was first introduced to Sufjan’s *Illinois* when I was pretty young, but I didn’t discover *The Age of Adz* until I was 16. While I do prefer *Illinois*, I would put both, along with *Carrie & Lowell*, among my favorite albums of all time. I still remember the first time I heard the frail beauty of “Futile Devices” transition into the nightmarish bong rip noises that start “Too Much”. That was truly a seminal moment for me in getting used to new sounds. While I’ve listened to “Futile Devices”, “Too Much”, “Get Real Get Right”, and especially “I Want to Be Well” many times over the years, it’s been a long long time since I’ve memorized your face listened to the full album. After doing so numerous times this week, I’ve discovered that I like *The Age of Adz* even more than I had realized. It’s such a cool album. In addition to the songs I’ve already mentioned, “The Age of Adz”, “I Walked”, “Vesuvius”, “All for Myself”, and “Impossible Soul” are all bangers too. While I do think Sufjan does the storytelling thing on *Illinois* better than just about anyone else, the personal, embarrassing nature of the lyrics on this album are right up my alley. Musically, I adore Sufjan’s maximalist approach to composition on *Illinois*, and he expands on it here (NOTE: ironically, Sufjan was very influenced by the 20th century classical style known as “minimalism”. I am using “maximalist” in a different, perhaps idiosyncratic, sense here, so I believe there is no contradiction in saying *The Age of Adz* is both minimalist and maximalist. However, the point I would like to make pertains to the maximalism only). This thing is packed full of fluttering woodwinds, soaring strings, booming horns, and frantic backing vocals. Every piece of available real estate on this album has been purchased and developed on by some kind of flourish. Even compared to other glitch music, a genre known for busy rhythms, the stuttering electronic beats on this thing feel particularly schizophrenic, adding some new unexpected feature just about every bar. The only real comparison I can think of to *The Age of Adz* in terms of sheer, over-the-top grandiosity is *My Beautiful Dark Twisted Fantasy*. Is it self-indulgent? Sure. But I’m happy to indulge Sufjan as much as he wants so long as the outcome is this beautiful. Is it sometimes a complete mess, particularly on “Impossible Soul? Absolutely. But that musical chaos makes the perfect match for the personal turmoil he so achingly expresses on these songs. This is a wonderful, gorgeous clusterfuck of an album that I love dearly. Writing this review is painful because it means I don’t get to keep listening to it afterward.

I love that this album starts off with an acoustic ballad, let alone one so achingly beautiful as “Futile Devices”. Still, it has a tense, tightly-wound quality to it that presages the depths of paranoia to come. From “It’s been a long, long time since I’ve memorized your face” to “And words are futile devices”, it has some of my favorite opening and closing lines of any song. It’s a magnificent song. “Too Much” is the perfect track to introduce the album’s primary sound, exemplifying both the signature textures and the extravagant arrangements. It’s one of the pinnacles of glitch pop. That 7/4 rhythm provides the perfect complement to the anxious atmosphere, removing one beat every two bars for a feel that’s unbalanced but not frenzied like the 15/16 of The Dismemberment Plan’s “Gyroscope”. The outro breakdown of the last two minutes is one of the finest musical moments I’ve ever heard, building up a collage of different timbres from almost nothing. “The Age of Adz” takes a similar template but exchanges some of the grandiosity for an imposing apocalyptic atmosphere. I love the way the lyrics play on “rot” and “death”, with “When I die, when I die, I’ll rot/But when I live, when I live, I’ll give it all I’ve got” standing out as particularly beautiful to me. The strings take a more central role than other tracks, injecting plenty of melodrama. There’s also a chugging, industrial groove that scares me a bit. I” Walked” is not one that I’ve listened to terribly much over the years, but I’ve been enjoying it during these listens though. It’s more of a straightforward glitch pop track instead of a grand orchestral work; I don’t like it quite as much as the others, but it’s catchy and fun. “Now That I’m Older”, sadly, is far and away the worst track. Why does the song with St. Vincent have to blow? It’s a slow, shapeless slog. “Get Real Get Right”, on the other hand, is not only one of my favorites on the album, but is I think Sufjan’s most underrated song. The self-aggrandizing manic atmosphere reaches a fever pitch with the prophetic religious lyrics here. I love the funky elements like the weird synth and the vocal effects on “You know you really gotta”. And every melody is just so catchy! “Barricade the bathroom doors (I know you want it, I know you really want it)”. “For you will not be distracted by the signs”. I could listen to this song for a million years.

“Bad Communication” is pretty, but as the shortest track it doesn’t leave a huge impression on me. “Vesuvius” is another track that I haven’t given as much attention too as I should have, but I really liked it while listening this week. I imagine this is the first time any of his tracks have said “Sufjan”, emblematic of how personal this album is. There’s no explosion like you might expect from a track entitled “Vesuvius” but it simmers as everything boils beneath the surface. And damn if those vocals aren’t catchy. I’ve heard “All for Myself” much more often as the foundation of Kendrick Lamar’s “Hood Politics”, which is always a weird listening experience. Still, I adore the way the vocals run into each other as they build. This feels like what “Now That I’m Older” was trying to be. “I Want to Be Well” is my favorite Sufjan song and one of my favorite songs of all time. It is six-and-a-half minutes of pure sonic perfection. It starts off strong and only gets better and better and better. The only adjective that really describes it is “urgent”. The electronic beat drives the music at a blistering pace, much faster than any song on the album. The flutes turn their fluttering up to 11, tumbling up and down at breakneck pace. You can feel how badly, how desperately Sufjan wants to get better. The “I’m not fucking around” segment is pure, unadulterated catharsis. The final build, beginning around 5:00, is utterly orgasmic. “Impossible Soul” is a mess, but it’s a beautiful mess. It’s sprawling and bloated, but, fuck, if it isn’t ambitious. The first two sections are definitely my favorite. In the first, I love the piano stabs that form the track’s backbone, the frantic breakbeats that come in and out, and the horn stabs in the chorus that give a dub reggae feel. The vocals are also catchy as hell. In the second section, I adore the female voice that provides a new, welcome texture and hypnotizes me as she says “Don’t be distracted, don’t be distracted”. Unfortunately, the biggest misstep is the Sufjan autotune section. It only last about three minutes, but without it I would call this track one of pop’s great masterpieces. Things kick back into gear around 13:00. The “It’s a long life” section isn’t as good as the first ten minutes, but it’s quite catchy and I appreciate some optimism. The acoustic folk of the ending is gorgeous and the lyrics wound me. “I never meant to lead you on/I only meant to please me, however” and “Girl, I want nothing less than pleasure” both stab me in the heart.

I really do love this album. I’m bummed we won’t be doing *Illinois*, but at the same time I appreciate that this on the list instead because it gets less attention.

Favorite tracks: Futile Devices, Too Much, The Age of Adz, I Walked, Get Real Get Right, Vesuvius, All for Myself, I Want to Be Well, Impossible Soul

Least favorite track: Now That I’m Older
