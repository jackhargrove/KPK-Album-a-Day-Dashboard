# Write-up

Sun Ra was born Herman Poole Blount in Birmingham, Alabama in 1914. He was a young prodigy, playing semi professionally by the time he was in high school and reportedly had the ability to write down the full arrangement of songs that he had heard once before. His first full time job as a musician was thanks to his biology teacher (and the last person to portray Aunt Jemima), Ethel Harper, who was pursing a career as a singer.	When she moved to New York to act and sing, Blount was left in charge. While the group eventually folded, Blount found a solid place in the Birmingham black music scene. He attended a local college before dropping out after 1 year. Later in his life, he would say he had a vision of aliens on the planet Saturn. This was well before belief in aliens and flying saucers was common.

After dropping out, Blount became devoted to his music. He barely slept, constantly rehearsed with whoever was around and wrote pages of music. He also began to develop his interest into esoterica, discussing hidden meanings of the Bible and other historic texts with colleagues. In 1942, at the age of 28, he was drafted into WWII. He refused, and claimed to be a conscientious objector, among several other reasons he couldn't serve. He eventually managed to secure a role doing public service with other conscientious objectors, but bailed on that too. He was arrested and tried. While at trial, he told the judge that if forced to serve, he would murder superior officers. He was sent to jail, where he was diagnosed as "a psychopathic personality and sexually perverted" before being let out as unfit to serve due to a hernia. He left Birmingham and moved to Chicago.

In Chicago, Blount came into contact with a variety of fringe Black movements, including the Black Muslims and Black Hebrews. After a few years of playing with prominent Chicago musicians, he formed his own group, the Space Trio with Deval Patrick's father, Pat, on saxophone. Blount also changed his name to "Le Sony'r Ra" or "Sun Ra" for short, inspired by the ancient Egyptians that he felt connected and possibly his childhood nickname "Sonny". Around that time, he also meet Alton Abraham, who would serve as his friend and manger. The two shared an interest in esoterica and the occult. Abraham would publish many of their ideas in pamphlets and form book clubs to discuss them. Together, they founded their own label, El Saturn Records. Sun Ra changed the name of his backing band to the Arkestra and had them dress in futurist Egyptian costumes, for both branding and to serve as comic relief for the avant-garde music they played

Sun Ra and his Arkestra then decamped for the Big Apple, moving to NYC in 1961 and living communally around 70th street. They secured a regular gig at Slug's Saloon in the East Village and became a fixture in the jazz scene, earning praise from Thelonius Monk and Dizzy Gillespie. He also recorded an album consisting mostly of versions of the Batman theme song in a collaboration with The Blues Project, a band featuring future Dylan collaborator Al Kooper. In 1968, the band moved again, to Philadelphia's Germantown, there they would stay for the next 30 years, calling their house "The Arkestral Institute of Sun Ra". The Arkestra began to tour more widely around this time, first to the West Coast, then to Europe and Egypt. Sun Ra taught his own class at UC Berkley, blending jazz music with his own esoteric beliefs. The group played with a variety of NYC-based musicians, including John Cale, Nico, Sonic Youth and Debbie Harry. Sun Ra died in 1993 after a mounting series of health problems including heart failure and strokes. The Arkestra is still performing to this day.

Sun Ra's influence is vast. He was one of the originators of Afrofuturism, pioneered using many different instruments in jazz, was on the forefront of modal and free jazz, and had a highly unique individual philosophy. He drew on basically every form of esoterica, including, the Kabbalah, Gnosticism, numerology, Freemasonry, Zen Buddhism, and Black Nationalism. He claimed to possibly be related to Elijah Muhammad, the founder of the Nation of Islam. He believed that the Abrahamic god was a powerful, but not all-powerful evil being. His beliefs were never organized in a single place, but were taught to his friends and collaborators via absurd, poignant remarks, pamphlets and logical reasoning. The best single source on his beliefs is his film, Space is the Place, in which Sun Ra goes back in time to free the human race from the evil "Overseer" in a game of cards and transport the black youth of the world to outer space. He succeeds, despite the opposition of white NASA scientists, and destroys the rest of Earth after he leaves.

# Hargrove's review

After 18 jazz albums that flirted with but never gave in to complete freedom, we finally broke the dam to true free jazz two weeks ago with Peter Brötzmann’s *Machine Gun*. While it had elements I respected and even some that I enjoyed, it really was not my thing. Now, with our very next jazz album, we’re diving back into that whole free thing. Except, Sun Ra doesn’t really fall into the same tradition as all the other free jazz who can trace their sound back to Ornette Coleman. He was a true original, his sound coming from nowhere. And while I can dig that, it’s unfortunately still not really my thing. I did sadly form a bad first impression of this album through a mistake on my part: I put the wrong version of “Yucatan” on my playlist, recreating the later Impulse! release instead of the original Saturn release that Ra intended. That track listing absolutely destroys side 1 for me. By far the coolest element of this album is Sun Ra’s clavinet electric piano that puts the “space” in “space jazz”. When the Impulse! version of “Yucatan” runs directly into “Bimini”, that results in over 13 minutes of almost just drums, removing the album’s best element. The Saturn version reduces this to 7 minutes of just drums, which still blows, and “Bimini” is easily my least favorite track, but it’s a major improvement. All of that being said, however, the meat of the album clearly comes from the title track “Atlantis” which spans the entirety of side 2. It’s just…not appealing to me. It just sounds like disc 2 of CAN’s *Tago Mago*, which I didn’t want to listen to then. And that’s a huge dealbreaker for me.

“Mu” starts the album with a nice bouncy rhythm, smooth sax, and the *appropriate* amount of tribal drumming. I do actually like this particular style of free jazz a good bit, the kind that’s neither too frantic nor too loud. The jaunting of Ra’s clavinet takes me on a fun, roundabout journey. “Lemuria” is more of the same, introducing a good bass line. I’m not crazy about either of these two tracks or anything, but I do enjoy them. My favorite track is the Saturn version of “Yucatan”, titled “Yucatan I” on streaming. It starts with the only real groove on the album, created by the tribal drums. It’s largely in line with the sound of the first two tracks, but the groove makes it feel like more of a fleshed-out song. The Impulse! version of “Yucatan”, on the other hand, kicks off a brutal, brutal 13 minute stretch of nothing but a cascading avalanche of drums. Look, I really like drums. I really, really do. But I do not want to hear this. it completely arrests the momentum of the album and removes its best elements. “Bimini” is worse because it’s longer and comes later. I absolutely cannot wait for that track to end every time. “Atlantis” starts off promising with a controlled cacophony and an intriguing spaceship beeping noise. After a couple minutes, though, it jumps into *Tago Mago* disc 2 territory. I guess I didn’t give that record enough credit when we listened for its influence from free jazz. I’m not putting “Atlantis” as my least favorite track because I respect the ambition, but I don’t particularly desire to hear it again.

I think we have two more fully free jazz albums remaining. I’m hoping one of them can win me over. But maybe this style just isn’t for me. I don’t know.

Favorite tracks: Mu, Lemuria, Yucatan (Saturn)

Least favorite track: Bimini

# Aidan's review

Being a white ride or die Sun Ra guy is a really funny thing to imagine. I don't even have to imagine, because I saw several of these guys when I went to a Arkestra concert because Yo La Tengo was opening. They looked really funny. They might have the right idea though because this album is great. I've enjoyed both the large ensemble jazz albums in this list and the sole free jazz selection so far and this combines both of those into one well constructed album. Despite nominally being free jazz, it feels a lot more solid than other jazz albums we've done, mostly due to the dominance of the drums, which take over an entire 7 minute track and have heavy contributions on the rest of the album. The clavinet is a relation, the album is nicely paced and builds up nicely to the 23 minute closer. Just a well executed, clean album that is a pleasure to listen to.  Outside of the album, Sun Ra's ability to blend genuine talent with clear schizophrenia is fascinating, and I look forward to checking out more of his music and paraphernalia once this project is over.
