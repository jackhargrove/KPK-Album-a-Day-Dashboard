# Write-up

This is our fourth of five Swans albums on the list, so see above for the three previous write-ups I’ve done which thoroughly cover the band’s history. This write-up will narrowly focus on today’s album. Swans released *Children of God* in 1987 as its fifth full-length album. At the time, the band consisted of vocalist and guitarist Michael Gira, vocalist and keyboardist Jarboe, guitarist Norman Westberg, bassist Algis Kizys, and drummer Ted Parsons. As the only constant member of Swans, Gira is the only contributor to appear on all five albums on our list. Westberg comes in second, appearing on all but *Soundtracks for the Blind*. Jarboe appears on both *Children of God* and *Soundtracks for the Blind* as a member of the band and on *The Seer* as a guest vocalist. Kizys and Parsons both only appear on this record; interestingly, both later became touring members of Foetus, the artist behind tomorrow’s album. Parsons also became a member of Jesu and played drums on the album *Jesu* which we listened to last year. *Children of God* also features instrumentation from other musicians. One was cellist Audrey Riley, who later arranged the strings on “Tonight, Tonight” from the Smashing Pumpkins’ *Mellon Collie and the Infinite Sadness*. Another was oboist Lindsay Cooper, a former member of Henry Cow who played on *In Praise of Learning*. She was also a member of Comus, though she did not play on the album we listened to.

Though this was Swans’ third album to feature Jarboe as a contributor, it was the first where she had significant creative input alongside Gira. Jarboe wanted to add melody and complexity to the brutal, grinding sound that Swans had become known for; Gira, who felt that the early sound had run its course anyway, agreed. As a result, *Children of God* was an important transitional album for Swans, marking the beginning of the incarnation for which it is best known. It is often remembered as one of the band’s best records.

# Hargrove's review

I had never heard this particular Swans album before. My general understanding was that it was a major transition point, the album where Jarboe initiated the band’s shift away from the unrelenting brutality of its early work and toward its later ambitious post-rock. And while that was partially true, it manifested in the music of *Children of God* entirely differently than I had expected. Gira and Jarboe had mixed, certainly, but the mixture was still highly heterogeneous. On his tracks, Gira still sounds recognizably in the *Filth* frame of mind. The majority of his tracks are just as punishing, grinding, and aggressive as ever. That isn’t to say he hadn’t grown at all; on the contrary, I found his contributions here to have more depth, subtlety, and dynamism than on earlier Swans albums. He had clearly matured as an artist significantly. And yet, the overarching feel of his tracks in isolation still remind me more of *Filth* than any other Swans album I’ve heard. However, Gira’s tracks weren’t, in fact, presented in isolation. Jarboe intersperses four tracks throughout the album, each as delicate as they are haunting. Two of them in particular, “In My Garden” and “Children of God”, are highlights of this album. Interestingly, by the time of *Soundtracks*, Gira’s music became much smoother and understated than on *Children of God*, while Jarboe embraces unabashed ugliness on tracks like “Yum-Yab Killers” and “YRP”. Instead of Jarboe and Gira combining somewhere in the middle, each slowly pulled in the other over time until they potentially even crossed paths. But on this album, Gira certainly brings the intensity while Jarboe brings the fragile beauty. And it works…pretty well. It’s not a perfect album by any means. At 71 minutes, it’s overlong for what it set out to do, and would have benefitted from being like a 55-minute CD. Some of the tracks are forgettable; Gira and Jarboe are both guilty of this but, because Gira contributed nine tracks to Jarboe’s four, Gira’s tracks in particular fall into a rut of similarity at times. When this album works, though, it really works. There are some damn good songs on this thing.

“New Mind” starts with a similar grinding, aggressive brutality as *Filth*, but noticeably slower and with subtle accoutrements in the instrumental. It’s easily one of the best Gira contributions to this album. The refrain of “damn you to Hell” terrifies me as much as any music can do. And I love the scraping and swirling at the end as the drums grow into pure power. Its intensity is broken unexpectedly by the gorgeous “In My Garden”. I love the piano line, Jarboe’s vocals, and especially the flute. Yet, despite its beauty, it leaves me just as disturbed as “New Mind”. “Our Love Lies” contains a memorable and twangy acoustic guitar line, but the main thrust of the song comes with the Church choir refrain and I sadly don’t love it. It also drags on for far too long. “Sex, God, Sex” reintroduces one of my favorite Swans signatures, an unbearable pounding on beat three of every bar that makes me wince with pain again and again. It’s crazy how much of Swans’ confrontational nature just comes from avoiding backbeats. Unfortunately, this track doesn’t grow into much of anything either. This lull in the record continues with the least interesting Jarboe track, “Blood and Honey”. It just doesn’t stand out to me at all. “Like a Drug (Sha La La La)” is fucked up from the get-go and doesn’t lose its intensity for one second. It’s one of the most bruising experiences on the album. I love it. I also love the quiet acoustic guitar ballad that is “You’re Not Real, Girl”. Quite the range, Mr. Gira! He ahs this weird vocal inflection that just works for me. The strings are unsettling and, frankly, nerve-wracking. The song itself is oddly heartbreaking.

“Beautiful Child” conveys a terrifying ritual just as well in its music as in its lyrics. It’s a pretty horrific song (complimentary). It doesn’t flow well at all, but, then, that’s the point. “Blackmail” is the third best of Jarboe’s contributions; her voice is so perfect for the album, but the song is only okay. The intro to “Trust Me” is one of my favorite parts of the entire album. Cooper’s oboe fucks SO damn hard. The rest of the song is pretty standard but I like the intro so much that I almost want to put it on my favorite tracks list. “Real Love” runs the risk of being my least favorite track thanks to the incessant “real love, real love” refrain. However, the harmonica part sounds so good that it saves it from the bottom. “Blind Love” takes a little while to find its groove but, once the volume kicks up, it’s a shot of adrenaline straight to the heart. There are sections of pure, thrashing aggression paired with hypnotic drumming. God, is it LOUD. “Children of God” is the best closer to any album I’ve heard in a long time. It’s the best Jarboe track and one of the best Swans songs I’ve ever heard period. The overlapping voices combine with the grinding rhythm to create one of the strangest textures I’ve ever heard.

It took a while to grow on me, but I really did like this album. Sadly, I think I have to put it as my least favorite Swans album on the list; it just doesn’t match the consistency of those records. Still, it falls smack dab in the middle of B Tier.

Favorite tracks: New Mind, In My Garden, Like a Drug (Sha La La La), You’re Not Real, Girl, Beautiful Child, Blind Love, Children of God

Least favorite track: Blood and Honey

# Aidan's review

This is now our second to last Swans album, and it's the first one that's really clicked with me. All three Swans albums until now have been varying degrees of me respecting what Gira is going for, but not enjoying how he does it. The mix of softer neofolk and harsher industrial songs is fantastic, making them seem more beautiful and ugly respectively. It brings to mind Gira's explanation of why he chose the name Swans. They're beautiful birds that are assholes at heart. Jarboe's softness is the key influence on this album, but by far the best sections are Gira's vocals. On "You're Not Real, Girl", he flirts between devotion and domination, tenderness and brutality. On "Our Love Lies", his drawl manages to twist the phrase "We believe in love" to something menacing. On the harsher tracks like "Sex, God, Sex", Gira is reduced to a pleading voice in the face of a guitar that nearly drowns out his voice at parts. Gira's god is the one of the Old Testament, indisputably masculine, capable of great vengeance but also great acts of mercy. It lacks the excess of Filth and is much more focused in its goals than Soundtracks or To Be Kind. Gira believes in God and love, and he thinks we should all be terrified of them.
