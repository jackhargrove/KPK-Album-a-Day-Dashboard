# Write-up

See above for the write-up I did on Swans' history when we listened to *To Be Kind*. This is the third Swans album we've listened to out of the five on our list and, as their debut, it's the first chronologically. *Filth* is a brutal, aggressive, punishing album that doesn't sound like much else. Its unusual lineup included two bassists (bandleader and vocalist Michael Gira, Harry Crosby), two drummers (Jonathan Kane, Roli Mosimann), and a guitarist (Norman Westberg). Instead of bass lines, the two bassists instead each played the same chord repeatedly. Both drummers would bang on random objects in addition to their drum sets. These unorthodox uses of instruments are analogous to techniques by their contemporaries, such as Sonic Youth’s guitar tuning. The lyrics are relentlessly violent and often concern power dynamics. The intense anger and nihilism stems from Gira’s experience being dirt poor in run-down old NYC. The cover was provided by Mosimann’s girlfriend, who worked as a dental hygienist. In addition to no wave, the sound was heavily by early British industrial groups like Throbbing Gristle. *Filth* received little commercial or critical attention; the only critic to review it was esteemed Dartmouth alum Robert Christgau, who called it “a hoot”. However, it would become a touchpoint for avant-garde metal, industrial rock, and even some alternative rock in the future.

Swans’ second LP, 1984’s *Cop*, continued the same style as *Filth* but is considered by some to be even more brutal. They released the EP *Young God* (often called “Raping a Slave” after one of its tracks) later that year, taking the sound of their first two albums to its absolute limit. It sold 1,000 copies, becoming their first commercially successful record. A copy made its way to Nirvana’s Kurt Cobain, who listed it as one of his 50 favorite albums in his journal. Justin Broadrick of Jesu also called it one of his primary inspirations for forming his first band Godflesh. Having perfected their early sound, Swans moved toward a more conventional industrial sound in 1986 with the twin albums *Greed* and *Holy Money*. The former contained the first contributions of Jarboe, *Filth*-superfan and friend of the band. She would take on an expanded role and finally introduce Gira to the concept of melody in their next album, 1987’s *Children of God*. As that album is on our list, I will continue this story when we get there.

Swans formed in New York in the waning days of a multimedia art scene centered in downtown Manhattan known as "no wave". Although we don't have any albums on our list that were directly a part of the no wave scene, this seems like the best time to talk about it. The American punk scene formed in the mid-1970s in NYC at the music club CBGB in the East Village. Pioneers like the Ramones, Television, and Patti Smith developed an irreverent, back-to-basics, and fast style of rock that rejected the overly complex and stagnating progressive rock scene of the time. After the Ramones played in London in 1976, bands like the Sex Pistols and the Clash helped set off the broader punk movement in both the US and the UK. The US scene continued to revolve around CBGB, where newer punk acts like Talking Heads and Blondie introduced jerky rhythms inspired by dance music and synthesizers to create a sound known as "new wave". New wave would become a commercial sensation, dominating mainstream music for the first half of the 1980s.

Back to the mid-70s, another CBGB band called Suicide also began experimenting with synthesizers, but they took it in a minimalist, challenging direction that became known as "synth punk". Their 1977 debut is on our list. Suicide's anti-commercial experimentation helped spur a new scene in downtown NYC that fully rejected the commercial direction of new wave. The art world of SoHo and the working class of the East Village came together in the late 70s to make extremely experimental music that left the bounds of rock entirely. Some bands played brutally loud and punishing industrial music, some played fully improvised music resembling free jazz, and some pushed the boundaries of new electronic instruments. One notable figure was Glenn Branca, who conducted orchestras of guitars tuned to sound like chainsaws. We listened to his album *The Ascension* previously. This scene was termed "no wave" to contrast with "new wave". No wave also generated a film scene, including future notable directors Jim Jarmusch and Vincent Gallo, as well as actor Steve Buscemi. In 1978, while in town to record Talking Heads' second album, Brian Eno heard numerous no wave bands playing at a show. He could not believe what he was hearing. He invited four of those bands to record with him and released a compilation album called *No New York* later that year. It is one of the few records to capture the authentic no wave sound. However, recording and naming the scene began its downfall. By 1982, all four bands from *No New York* had disbanded. New York punk largely moved on to new styles like hardcore and mutant disco soon after. But in 1981, as the no wave era was coming to a close, two bands formed and began producing brutal, unlistenable music who would eventually grow into alternative rock icons: Sonic Youth and Swans.

# Hargrove's review

Man, this album is powerful. It feels like I’m being punched in the face over and over again. I’ve been a fan of Swans’ later work since I was a teenager, but their early stuff has never been my thing (I chose the *Filth* t-shirt at the concert I went to because it was one of the few I could wear in public). However, after spending the last 9 months developing my ear for styles I’m not used to, I found this round of listening for *Filth* very rewarding. It’s remarkable how much of their future output you can hear in this album if you try. Everything is there, save the melodies. But the repetition, the pounding accents every bar, the grim darkness, all of it is there. It feels like the distilled base of Swans, and everything that follows is them building on top of that base with more and more elaborate architecture. I spent a lot of time this week listening to other no wave stuff, much of which I enjoyed, but none of it works for me in quite the same way. Something that particularly confounded me is why I enjoy Swans while finding Glenn Branca’s *The Ascension* (the one we listened to a few months ago) to be so, for lack of a better word, bland. On the object level, it’s clear that Swans directly get much of their technique and sound from Branca. But with *The Ascension*, it just feels like it’s not leading anywhere. It’s building a grand, elegant work from the ugliest of components, sure. It’s certainly stylish. With Swans, however, there’s an evil, evil core. Anton Chigurh evil. Cthulhu evil. There’s something terrifying and sinister lurking between the hypnotizing siren song layers of sound. So when the rhythm section that makes up 80% of the band punches me in the nose over and over and over and over and over again at regular intervals, it’s not trying to help me find some hidden beauty. It’s trying to beat me into submission for reasons so malevolent and enigmatic they exceed my understanding. Every time I listen, I not only get a taste of the evil that exists in our world, I defy it through my enjoyment. And I think that’s neat.

Though the unified wall of sound across the album is necessary to create its intended effect, the trade-off is that the sameness between individual tracks is its greatest weakness. As such, the earlier a track appears, the bigger boost it gets in my estimation. The opener “Stay Here” feels fresh every time you listen, containing the perfect amount of shock value as you enter. The waves of bass are just relentless. The drummers sound like they’re banging on a metal wall. I like the background vocals with the weird effects. Great song. “Big Strong Boss” has more of a hypnotic industrial groove to it than the previous track, which I really enjoy. It reminds me *To Be Kind* in that there is so much emphasis on the downbeat it makes you wince every time comes around. The fade in on “Blackout” is a great effect. The syncopation in the drums creates less of a rigid industrial feel, but it makes up for it with the swinging cyclicality. I don’t understand the guitar in “Power for Power” — why is it playing different notes in succession? A “melody”? Huh. Kidding aside, this is one of the best songs on here. The ringing, distorted quasi-melody in the guitar creates a hazy atmosphere that’s slowly but surely sinking. It feels like walking down a stairwell at MC Escher’s house. I don’t mind that “Freak” is so short, but the linearity of it bothers me. It’s the only track that isn’t really hypnotizing in any way. “Right Wrong” returns to the quality of the first half of the album though with its stellar guitar riff. It sounds like it’s surging with energy. “Thank You” is the second-weakest on the album after “Freak” and certainly the worst of the full-length tracks. I’m getting a bit fatigued by this point, and t doesn’t add anything the other tracks don’t. “Weakling” is better, but nearing the album’s finale it becomes difficult to distinguish between tracks. Ditto for the closer, “Gang”.

This isn’t an album I see myself listening to on the regular. For that reason, I can’t rank it too highly. But, fuck, do I appreciate this album now. It is truly a one-of-a-kind experience and, when you’re in the proper mood, there’s nothing that can match its intensity. It’s a very cool album, about on par with my enjoyment for Henry Cow and Tim Hecker.

Favorite tracks: Stay Here, Big Strong Boss, Blackout, Power for Power, Right Wrong

Least favorite track: Freak
