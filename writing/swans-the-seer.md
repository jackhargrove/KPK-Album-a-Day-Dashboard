# Write-up

See above for my four (!) previous write-ups on Swans. In particular, see my write-up for *To Be Kind* for more information on Swans’ 2010s revival, as *The Seer* also falls into that era of the band. *The Seer* is Swans’ 12th studio album and the second released as part of the band’s 2010s reformation after *My Father Will Guide Me Up a Rope to the Sky* in 2010. Swans’ lineup for *The Seer* consisted of vocalist and guitarist Michael Gira, guitarist Norman Westberg, lap steel guitarist Kristof Hahn, bassist Christopher Pravdica, drummer Thor Harris, drummer Phil Puleo, and multi-instrumentalist Bill Rieflin. Westberg has played with Swans since its 1983 debut *Filth* and has appeared on four of the five Swans albums on the list (all but *Soundtracks for the Blind*). Rieflin first played with Swans on 1995’s *The Great Annihilator* and has played on three Swans albums on the list: *Soundtracks for the Blind*, *The Seer*, and *To Be Kind*. Rieflin also played drums on the 2013 Taylor Swift single “The Last Time” (?????). Hahn and Puleo had each played on one record from Swans’ original incarnation, but only played on the 2010s albums *The Seer* and *To Be Kind* for the purposes of our list. Harris and Pravdica joined Swans only for the 2010s incarnation. In addition to official band members, *The Seer* includes numerous guest musicians. Karen O of indie rock group the Yeah Yeah Yeahs sings lead vocals on “Song for a Warrior”. Alan Sparhawk and Mimi Parker (RIP) of Low sing vocals on “Lunacy”. Ben Frost, who played keyboards on Tim Hecker’s *Ravedeath, 1972* contributed “fire sounds” to “A Piece of the Sky”. And Jarboe, Gira’s creative partner during the group’s original run, contributes backing vocals to “The Seer Returns” and “A Piece of the Sky”. However, Jarboe never officially rejoined the group for its revival, and *The Seer* remains the last Swans project on which she has appeared.

While *The Seer* was the second 2010s Swans album, it was the first to contain the extremely long, densely atmospheric tracks that have defined the Swans revival. The album’s title comes from an episode where Gira spontaneously yelled “I see it, I see it all” while the band was performing the then-instrumental track that would become “The Seer”. The album’s cover features a picture of Gira’s teeth overlaid on a drawing of a wolf. *The Seer*’s production was funded from the proceeds of a live album, as well as through direct donations from fans. The album was a massive critical success, earning rave reviews from critics. It was also commercially successful, hitting number 114 on the Billboard 200. Once again, see my write-up on *To Be Kind* to see what Swans has been up to since 2012. Yesterday, September 5, was the one year anniversary of Carms and I seeing Swans play live in DC. It was awesomeeeeeeee.

# Hargrove's review

*The Seer* was the first Swans album I ever listened to all the way back when I was 16. It was definitely not something I was ready for. I knew it was transcendent in some way, and I became a Swans “fan”, but I just don’t think I had the capacity or faculties at that time to really understand what was going on. I’ve revisited it very infrequently over the years, which I think demonstrates how precarious my appreciation of it was. Still, I commonly refer to it as my favorite Swans album, even if it’s been a long time since I’ve listened to it. So I was excited, if a little nervous, to revisit it this week with the ears of a grizzled 25-year-old music listening veteran. I had a lot of trouble connecting with it on my first few listens, especially the gargantuan title track. I even re-listened to *To Be Kind* and found it much more focused and consistent by comparison. I became worried that I’d have to eat crow on calling this my favorite Swans album. However, on my final note-taking listen before this review, the gears finally began turning and everything clicked into place. With its massive scope and diverse array of sounds, it’s much easier to become fully overwhelmed while listening to it passively than *To Be Kind*. But when you’re in the right headspace and ready for a brutal journey, it delivers. From beginning to end, it sounds like pastoral Gothic, but not like an abandoned cabin in the woods. Instead, it evokes ancient epics, stories of a fight between good and evil in pre-industrial untamed woods. It’s interesting how Swans evolved over time; in the era of *Filth* and even *Children of God*, Swans’ music was obsessed with embodying great evil. In the 2010s, and especially on *The Seer*, Gira’s obsession with all-encompassing, world-swallowing evil remains. But instead of embodying it, he stands nearly helpless in the awe of its might. More than any other album, Gira sounds determined to fight this evil, from the creeping of madness in “Lunacy” to the resistance against demonic possession of “The Seer” to the valiant hope of “Song for a Warrior” to, ultimately, the uncharacteristically dreamy section that ends “A Piece of the Sky”. Of course, good can never triumph in the world of Swans, and he sounds utterly defeated by the end of “Apostate”. Still, there’s something invigorating about hearing him put up a fight for maybe the first time. Sonically, the pervasive acoustic guitar, recurring bell chimes, and even many of the song structures evoke folk and country more than I’ve ever heard on a Swans project. Combined with the typically punishing repetition that I’ve come to know and love Swans for, it evokes traditional rituals, the darkness looming in the hills of Appalachia. The combination of Dionysian ecstasy with ancient folk reminds me a lot of both Comus and the second half of Tim Buckley’s *Starsailor*. It’s a high variance album, and one I have to be in the right headspace to enjoy. I’m surprised that I’m saying this, but on an off day, I would take the laser-guided precision of *To Be Kind* over this. When it hits, though, as it just has for me on my fifth listen, it’s the most satisfying Swans project from front to back.

*The Seer* enters with the unnerving brilliance of “Lunacy”. Gira desperately staves off madness and perhaps possession over unbelievably tense acoustic guitar and wails on the downbeats. There are many songs that attempt to simulate madness, but for my money, this is one of the most effective. It builds into a pounding cacophony, the first of many on this album, before settling into a ritual trance as if I’m being worked into a lather to commit human sacrifice. It’s amazing how full the vocals sound for just three verses. Though the cacophony picks up a second time, it ends on a tender and wistful note. “Mother of the World” goes straight into classic Swans grinding. I adore the pulsing breath on top of the punishing guitar as the drums and bass slowly make themselves known. Gira’s voice and the full drum beat create an intense explosion after spending five minutes on that unchanging, brutal opening. It sounds like what Euripides’ Maenads would listen to as they tear people limb from limb in drunken bliss. It shifts into a softer piano-led section that I enjoy, but not quite as much as the beginning. Still, those keys twinkle and Gira sings beautifully. “The Wolf” is a rare misstep. I’m not a fan of how the white noise is used. Thankfully, it’s an interlude running less than two minutes that simply prepares for “The Seer”, a sprawling behemoth of over half an hour. It starts with a wall of bagpipes and slowly adds sound effects, drums, and bells for over four minutes before settling into a standard rhythm. The bass drum pounds on 1 and 3 with a guitar twang on every downbeat, creating a groove that really picks up steam after six minutes. When Gira comes in to repeat “I see it all” as if possessed in a trance, I start to feel the build in my bones, like I’m actually fighting demons. In addition to the dissonance of the guitars, the skittering of both drum sets, and the repetition of the vocals, what sells the tension is the quarter note on every fourth beat after six eighth notes. It makes for a slight break between the slamming that makes it impossible to relax. At 11:40, the album’s most intense explosion happens, searing my brain until it collapses a minute-and-a-half later with nearly 20 minutes left in the track. Instead of collapsing into silence, it punches you in the mouth every five seconds with the full force of the ensemble. If the opening was the process of a possession, this section is where Gira’s will to fight slowly dies, kicking and screaming. Admittedly, this goes on a bit too long, but I’m fully back on board when it starts building again. The new rhythm is more sinister before it collapses back into the ether, finally replaced at the end with one last section at 28:00. Gira starts to speak in tongues doing his gruffest, most sinister voice to sing an honestly pretty catchy rhythm. It’s an absurd journey of a track that leaves me speechless.

After all of that, “The Seer Returns” feels like a hangover. It starts out hazy and ethereal before kicking into a groove reminiscent of “A Little God in My Hands”. It’s easily the track that foreshadows the sound of *To Be Kind* the most. Gira sounds sleazy and unmoored from morality over the swaggering groove as Jarboe provides a haunting wall of droning vocals in the background. It’s possible this wouldn’t be a favorite track if it were slotted elsewhere on the album, but I like how much it feels like a cooldown from the record’s longest track. “93 Ave. B Blues” starts with screeching woodwinds. It builds, but never past an unintelligible cacophony. As we’ve already properly cooled down from the intensity of the title track, this feels like entirely the wrong place for this track. “The Daughter Brings the Water” has a a good folk atmosphere and a decently catchy chorus, but it’s just too short to develop what I want it to develop into. Worse, I find its meaning impenetrable. After so much good music, disc 1 ends on a weak note. Thankfully, “Song for a Warrior” starts disc 2 with a needed change of pace, a simple but powerful folk pseudo-standard. This shift in sound helps justify the album’s length. As an aside, it’s remarkable how much Karen O nails Gira’s style of singing. She sounds gruff and old and wizened like he does. I love how the bells create the rhythm at the beginning of “Avatar”. It feels like a dark omen, the bells that portend doom. It rapidly builds it this epic melody out of nowhere about halfway through. Something about the chords sounds majestic and determined, making it the most unified this album has sounded in some time. It’s as if the focus has sharpened and we’re heading quickly toward a destination or climax. Just when you think it’s over, the last minute explodes with fire and brimstone not heard since 11 minutes into the title track. “A Piece of the Sky” starts with a sample of crackling fire before transitioning into a wall of Jarboe vocals, then into an instrumental drone. This goes on for nearly 10 minutes before forming into a song. Instead of a huge explosion, however, it’s a slow tempo and thoughtful guitar riffing over a steady beat, plus a sharp piano adding small phrases here and there. The chords and piano remind me of “O Children” by Nick Cave & the Bad Seeds, one of his songs that I actually like. Eventually, this leads us to the only part of the album that can be described as lovely; bells softly chime in the wind as Gira sings his most hopeful melody ever over a dreamy acoustic guitar riff. I love the vivid, pretty imagery with an undercurrent of darkness, like “In the blood of the Swans/As the sun fucks the dawn/In the mud of the lake/In the drunk and the dazed/Are you there?”. Of course, this idyll is too good to be true, and “The Apostate” takes us toward an apocalyptic ending. It’s another one that starts with a long drone, this time adding chaotic drums and lasting over 13 minutes. When that’s finally over, a jazzy groove kicks in that sounds a lot the second half of Tim Buckley’s *Starsailor*. I adore this final section, but it’s one of the few minutes that maybe doesn’t feel entirely worth the time and effort it took to build ip to it. If the opening section were maybe half the length, this could be my favorite track, but right I’m just torn. I’m going to leave it off my favorites list, but I do love that section.

For a while, I was worried I would be forced to rate this below *To Be Kind*. However, I’ve gotten to a place with it where there’s very little I would change. When I’m in the mood for *The Seer*, it’s easily the pinnacle of Swans’ slow-building, existentially terrifying style.

Favorite tracks: Lunacy, Mother of the World, The Seer, The Seer Returns, Song for a Warrior, Avatar, A Piece of the Sky

Least favorite track: 93 Ave. B Blues
