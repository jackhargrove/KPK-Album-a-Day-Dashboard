# Write-up

I did a write-up for Swans when we listened to *Soundtracks*, but it was short and incomplete, so I’ll take it from the top here. Swans is a band that has existed on-and-off since 1981 with the only consistent member being bandleader Michael Gira. Gira led an unconventional and unhappy childhood, being born to an alcoholic mother and largely absent father in LA in 1954. After amassing a criminal record as a child, he and his father moved to Germany when he was a teenager, where he promptly ran away and ultimately found himself in an Israeli prison after getting caught selling hashish in Jerusalem. After turning 16 in prison, he decided to turn his life around; he returned to LA in 1971, where he got his GED, graduated from community college, and attended art school. In 1979, at age 25, he moved to Manhattan to pursue music, although he worked as a construction worker for his day job. He became involved in the No Wave scene, a noisy and brutal reaction to New Wave’s commercialization of punk that was fronted by composer Glenn Branca. Gira founded Swans in 1981, at the tail-end of No Wave’s relevance as a scene. Thurston Moore, who would become the guitarist for Sonic Youth, was a founding member.

I’m going to speed through Swans’ original incarnation because there are two albums from this era on the list. Swans released their debut record, *Filth*, in 1983. It’s on our list, and it’s considered one of the most brutal, punishing albums of all-time. Swans’ early style was crushing, dissonant noise and screamed vocals without a hint of melody. Swans released a second album in this style in 1984, *Cop*, as well as an EP officially titled *Young God* but more commonly referred to as *Raping a Slave*. Swans became notorious in NY for their brutally loud and often violent live shows. In 1985, fan of the band Jarboe officially joined, and she began to very slowly introduce melody into their music. She first appeared on Swans’ third album, 1986’s *Greed*, but her only contribution was screaming. By their 5th album, 1987’s *Children of God* (on our list), Jarboe had taken on a much more major role, including some songwriting duties. Her melodies became a counterpoint to Gira’s brutality. In 1988, Swans released a single that included two covers of Joy Division’s “Love Will Tear Us Apart”, one by Gira and one by Jarboe. The covers became a surprise hit on college radio, and this resulted in Swans getting signed to a major label. The result was 1989’s *The Burning World*, the closest thing to a pop record Swans ever released. It was a massive commercial failure, becoming the lowest selling LP the label had ever released. Swans was quickly dropped. Gira did, however, take this as an opportunity to shake up Swans’ sound, and 1991’s *White Light from the Mouth of Infinity* and 1992’s *Love of Life* found Swans playing acoustic, neofolk music. 1995’s *The Great Annihilator* was even one of Swans’ most accessible, straightforward albums. However, Gira suddenly decided he wanted to retire the Swans name, so he released *Soundtracks for the Blind* in 1996 as a farewell.

Over the next 14 years, Gira was involved in various solo projects. In 2010, He announced online that Swans would be coming back with new music. Later that year, Swans released *My Father Will Guide Me Up a Rope to the Sky*, which was decently well-received despite Jarboe’s decision not to return. It was followed up in 2012 with *The Seer*, which received immense critical acclaim and is on our list. *The Seer* was 2 hours long and featured long, slow builds, starting Swans’ “big sound” era. Next was 2014’s *To Be Kind*, which expanded on the sound started by *The Seer*. The album features guest vocals on four tracks from indie musician St. Vincent. The second track is dedicated to Chester Burnett, the real name of legendary blues artist and Gira’s personal hero Howlin’ Wolf. “Kirsten Supine” features lyrics based off of Lars von Trier’s 2011 film *Melancholia*. “Nathalie Neal” is a tribute to one of Gira’s friends. The tracks “A Little God In My Hands”, “Oxygen”, and “Screen Shot” were all released as singles, with the latter being the most successful song Swans has ever released. *To Be Kind* peaked at 37 on the Billboard Hot 200, the highest placement ever for a Swans album. The album received a lot of buzz from critics, most notably receiving the second 10/10 ever given by popular internet critic Anthony Fantano (the first was Death Grips - *The Money Store*). Swans followed *To Be Kind* with 2016’s *The Glowing Man*, which was their third album in a row to receive high critical acclaim. They took a brief hiatus after, but returned in 2019 with *Leaving Meaning* and 2023 with *The Beggar*. Swans just released their 17th album last month, *Birthing*. Gira claims that this album and the upcoming tour (that Carms and I will be seeing) will be the last of Swans’ “big sound” era. Gira said he isn’t retiring (although he is 71), but that, going forward, Swans will be exploring different sonic textures.

# Hargrove's review

I believe I had listened to this album once all the way through, but that would have been when I was 17 and I didn’t remember any of the music. The first Swans album I got into was *The Seer* and though I liked it, it was a little much for me at that time. I listened to *To Be Kind* as well because people said it was also good, but it was really just too much music to digest at the level of experience I was at. Listening to it now (and I completed 6 full listens this week. 12 hours. Madness!), I still don’t enjoy it to the same level I enjoy *The Seer*, but they’re very similar albums, and this one is also quite good. The two albums are so similar that I’m going to try to avoid comparing them going forward, and just speak about *To Be Kind* as if *The Seer* doesn’t exist. One thing I really like about Swans is how they don’t abandon the blues. Many other artists in this space of “complex and experimental rock music” strip out the blues; they often really lean into the Western classical style of composition and performance, and any remnants of the blues are in the style of guitar playing. Swans, on the other hand, reaches similar or greater levels of complexity while retaining both the sounds and the spirit of the blues: 4/4 time, the swung rhythms of a blues shuffle, the twangy guitar playing, the vocal quirks, the feeling of an artist constantly on the edge. It really sets them apart from a lot of other bands that make a similar style of music. Instead of trying to progress past rock, Swans move forward with rock, hand-in-hand, on each others’ terms. To that end, Swans emphasize the aspects of late blues and early rock that set it apart from the music that proceeded: high volume, devil-may-care attitude, a degree of self-indulgence, a primacy of the artist above the listener. This album is *punishing*. It’s long, the individual songs are long, the noises are earsplittingly loud, the vocals border on obnoxious, and the lyrics are opaque. It’s the logical conclusion of a tradition including hardcore punk, No Wave, punk rock, metal, krautrock, garage rock, vocal surf, all tracing back to the electric blues, of putting the listener through an ordeal. Meeting the artist on his terms, bending to his will. It’s something I admire, even if I’m not always able to enjoy it.

On *To Be Kind*, though, we find a master of his craft using his 30+ years of experience to produce his uncompromising vision in a way that is eminently enjoyable. “Screen Shot” is one of the best songs Swans has ever done, compressing so much experimentation in sound and structure into a tight 8 minutes. The bass part in the beginning absolutely rips, and the opening sets you up for the kind of repetition you’ll hear throughout the album. The drums are great, which will be consistent throughout this record; they perfectly quantize the repetition, bringing the brutal pulse into your ears at regular intervals. And that guitar pulse, man. There are very few reprieves from it across the entirety of this album. Every 4 beats, the guitar pounds straight into your brain. It’s the kind of thing that causes migraines. The volume in this track officially hits overload around 6:30, and yet they kick it up another notch at 7:00. I love the suspenseful piano line that kicks in after the second verse, and I think the lyrics that promote living in the moment are great. Banger opener. “Just A Little Boy (For Chester Burnett)” is, by contrast, painstakingly slow. It starts out ominous, and before you know it, you’re halfway through and there’s a cascade of bluesy guitars. I do have some issues with this track, namely that the pulse is less strong and that it doesn’t justify its length, but it is quite good. “A Little God In My Hands” is a personal favorite of mine. It starts off driving in the best way, full of swagger in the guitar, drums, and vocals. The drums are just so good, and the noise wall at the end is both crushing and ornately textured. “Bring The Sun/Toussaint L’Ouverture” is a monster of a track, but I think Swans pull of the 34 minute run time. The two-note guitar opener was the first part of the record on my final listen where I simply had to turn the volume down a notch. Total brutality, maximally oppressive. The build in the “Bring The Sun” part is executed perfectly, and the climax is an orgasmic, pulsating Cthulhu of noise. The 6 minutes that follow are a bit too abstract for my taste, but once the “Toussaint L’Ouverture” part kicks in at around 21:00 things get good again. I love the vocals, the twang of the guitars, and the cacophony at the end is bizarrely pleasant and even comforting. “Some Things We Do” is lyrically a retread of “Screen Shot”, but it stands out on the album not only as the shortest track, but also as the only full reprieve from the pulse. The cello strings sound terrifying, and the apocalyptic atmosphere is unmatched.

“She Loves Us” kicks off disc 2 with a return of the pulse, but I admit I don’t love the breakdown section in this song. Once it gets to around 7:00 there’s forward propulsion once again, but that’s a long wait to get there. The song overstays its welcome, but the ending noise is once again great. “Kirsten Supine” sounds almost like a pop song compared to the rest of the album, with its sweet vocal melody and restrained instrumentation in the first half. The bright xylophone (?) part in particular is really nice. At times, this almost sounds like a Radiohead song. The first half is gorgeous, and the second half does a great job at merging the atmosphere of the first with the return of the pulsating noise. “Oxygen” rips the guitar riff straight from Branca’s “Lesson No. 2” and turns it into something so much better than any track on *The Ascension*. The rhythm section is flawless, with the drums complementing the bass line perfectly. This track is so rhythmically complex that it makes the noise feel even noisier than it does on any other song. A standout from disc 2. “Nathalie Neal” has some serious power, and I like the idea of immortalizing a random friend, but it’s definitely my least favorite track. The opening is too long and doesn’t have the strongest atmosphere, and the meat of the track is just too static. It certainly feels like some time was wasted here, which is not good on such a long album. “To Be Kind” is the perfect closer. The melody of its opening section, though understated, is very strong. The final bits of noise are just absolutely brutal and are a great conclusion to and summation of this entire album. It’s just about a thesis statement for the whole album.

*To Be Kind* is brutal, punishing, harsh, and exhausting. Listening to it can feel like climbing Mt. Everest. But there’s something so satisfying on being taken into an artist’s world like this. Beyond even the brutal bits, so much personality shines through: Gira’s baby voice on “Just A Little Boy (For Chester Burnett)”, him screaming “Toussaint L’Ouverture” on “Bring The Sun/Toussaint L’Ouverture”, and the bizarre way he pronounces “oxygen” on “Oxygen”. There’s really no other artist who can make me see things exactly the way that he does. I see myself returning to it only periodically, and there are many other albums that just resonate with me more, but damn do I respect this album.

Favorite tracks: Screen Shot, A Little God In My Hands, Bring The Sun/Toussaint L’Ouverture, Some Things We Do, Kirsten Supine, Oxygen, To Be Kind

Least favorite track: Nathalie Neal
