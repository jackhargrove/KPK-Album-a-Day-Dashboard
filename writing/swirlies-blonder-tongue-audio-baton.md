# Write-up

Swirlies are an indie rock band formed in Boston in 1990 originally consisting of guitarist and vocalist Damon Tutunjian, guitarist and vocalist Seana Carmody, bassist Andy Bernick, and drummer Ben Drucker. They are often seen as the American answer to my bloody valentine, combining the shoegaze of the UK’s indie scene with the lo-fi of the American indie scene. Carmody and Tutunjian met in 1990 when a local Boston zine publisher asked them both to form a Go-Go’s cover band with him. The Go-Go’s were an all-female American new wave group during the transition from punk in the late 70s and early 80s with hits like “Our Lips Are Sealed” and “We Got the Beat”. Lead singer Belinda Carlisle was briefly a member of the Germs. Carmody and Tutunjian quickly decided to form their own band that wrote its own songs, bringing in Tutunjian’s high school friend Bernick on bass and MIT student Drucker on drums. They used effects pedals, alternative guitar tunings, and AM radio static to develop their noise pop sound. They released a home recorded debut single in 1991, followed by a mini-album *What To Do About Them* in 1992.

While recording their first full-length LP, Drucker and Bernick both grew apart from the band, with session drummers largely replacing the former and friend Morgan Andrews replacing the latter. Swirlies’ debut album, 1993’s *Blonder Tongue Audio Baton*, employed a shoegaze sound featuring many vintage analogue musical instruments like the mellotron and moog synthesizer. The album’s title refers to a vintage piece of audio equipment for audio equalization made in 1959 that the group used while making the album. The song “Park the Car (by the Side of the Road)” is a reference to lyric from “That Joke Isn’t Funny Anymore” by the Smiths. The album was an underground success, and is now considered one of the greatest shoegaze albums of all time (placed at #11 by Pitchfork).

After the album’s release, Swirlies toured its original lineup. However, by 1994, every original member had left the band but Tutunjian. Since then, the band has released four more albums with Tutunjian as the only permanent member. Their later sound has frequently been compared to Stereolab. Carmody went on to found Syrup USA and maintains a good relationship with Tutunjian, often joining Swirlies for concerts. Swirlies still tours occasionally to this day, adding former War on Drugs guitarist Kurt Vile to the lineup briefly in 2013.

# Hargrove's review

We are back and right off the bat there’s a pretty good album! I had never heard of Swirlies at all, and was not much impressed at all with my first listen. I already know enough lo-fi slacker indie rock bands from the 90s, I really don’t need to know another! But on my second listen, my ears really perked up starting with “Pancake” around halfway through. From that point on, the songs are all really well-written. This album’s first half is fine, but it really struggles to get into any kind of groove. There are just too many tempo swings and dynamic changes without the requisite riffs or hooks to create a through line. The second half, on the other hand, delivers non-stop blissed out noise pop that doesn’t skimp on either the heaviness of the noise or the catchiness of the pop. Then, on third listen, it really hit me how similar Swirlies’ sound is to pre-*loveless* my bloody valentine and *Daydream Nation* era Sonic Youth. They barely even try to hide it. Does that make me like it any less? Not really, no. Sure, I can find a better, purer version of the overall sound with those bands. But I can’t hear the Swirlies songs. And some of the songwriting on this album is really quite good. It’s not the most impressive record I’ve ever heard in my life, but I’ve come away from it quite pleased. Compared to my expectations after listen 1, this feels like a triumph.

“Untitled” is barely even a track at under 15 seconds, but I gotta admit that I do like that tearing tape sound. “Bell” sounds pretty nice, easily taking the top spot of songs on the first half of the record. I like some of the particulars of Tutunjian’s vocal delivery, particularly the stuttering on “Now, again/He's almost done/He's not allowed/But he can't stop now” and the way he says “It’s fine”. The tempo changes that shift the song from dream pop to pop punk are interesting at first, but things started to get really muddled in the extended outro. Still, it’s a decent track. “Vigilant Always” continues the whiplash inconsistency in song pacing. It’s just too disjointed for me. “His Love Just Washed Away” takes us to an early nadir, “solving” the erratic shifts between the mid slow sections and better fast sections by just playing the slow section throughout. “His Life of Academic Freedom” is weird, but honestly I’ll take the new flavor. With “Pancake”, the album goes from middling to stellar almost immediately. I like Carmody’s voice quite a lot, as well as the cool horn things. The outro is pure sex, perhaps the best part of the entire album. I her oohs and the weird textures floating around the drums. “Jeremy Parker” matches that energy, with Tutunjian’s voice every bit as good as Carmody’s. The guitar is at its absolute fuzziest, covering everything in feedback like a warm blanket. The drums are hyperactive and exciting, combining with the guitar riff for these awesome stop/start sections. And to top it all off, it has a great outro as well. “Park the Car by the Side of the Road” plays to Swirlies’ strengths by being the fastest song by a good bit. Yeah, it sounds a hell of a lot like “you made me realise”, but whatever, the song is good enough that I’ll let it slide. The cymbal crashes and rapid fills rule, as do Carmody’s oohs and the traded vocals in the verses. A pretty great guitar riff carries the next track, “Tree Chopped Down”. The guitar stays fuzzy, the drums stay splashy, and the momentum maintains. The hits keep coming with “Wrong Tube”, which I’m pretty sure is about anal sex. I adore the way the noisy opening transitions into calm for Carmody’s angelic vocals. There are also really nice sections where the guitar gets super punchy. Unfortunately, after five bangers in a row, the album ends on its lowest note with “Wait Forever”. I wouldn’t mind the minute of silence plus the weird sampled outro so much if the actual song part were better. The monologue doesn’t even feel thematically satisfying. Whatever.

I feel pretty comfortable putting this around the smack dab middle of B Tier. Half of the album is the mid that I expected, while the other half genuinely surprised me with how good it was. The end result is pretty much the same as how I felt about *The Stone Roses* despite opposite expectations.

Favorite tracks: Bell, Pancake, Jeremy Parker, Park the Car by the Side of the Road, Tree Chopped Down, Wrong Tube

Least favorite track: Wait Forever

# Aidan's review

This and mbv's Isn't Anything are probably the two most similar albums on this list. I'm not much for recognizing guitar lines and it took me 10 seconds to find ones on this album that were completely ripped. There's some originality here, with more of an American slacker rock sound beyond the "swirlie" guitars. When the melody wasn't buried in mountains of effects, there were some cool songs like Tree Chopped Down and Wrong Tube. Still, the fact that I'm not confident in my ability to identify songs from this album vs Isn't Anything makes me hesitant to rate it much higher.
