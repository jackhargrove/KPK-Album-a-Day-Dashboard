# Write-up

System of a Down (SOAD) is an alternative metal band formed in Glendale, a heavily Armenian suburb of Los Angeles, in 1994. Since 1997, the band has consisted of vocalist, lyricist, and keyboardist Serj Tankian, guitarist and composer Daron Malakian, bassist Shavo Odadjian, and drummer John Dolmayan. All four members are ethnically Armenian, and this heritage plays a major role in the band’s image. Though the band is often associated with the nu metal style that dominated the late 90s and early 2000s, their critical reputation is much higher most other members of that often-derided genre (I’ll get into it more later, but Deftones and Faith No More/Mr. Bungle are other notable exceptions here). Tankian and Malakian, the two creative leaders of the group, met in 1992 while working at a recording studio. They had both attended the same K-12 school for Armenian children in LA, but Tankian is eight years older than Malakian. They formed SOAD in 1994, basing the name off a poem written by Malakian and intentionally choosing a name that started with “S” so their records would be placed next to Slayer’s, their favorite band, at record stores. They began playing the local LA circuit, and released their first song in 1997 as part of a compilation album commemorating the Armenian genocide. They were discovered in the late 90s by legendary producer Rick Rubin (more on him later), who produced their self-titled debut album in 1998. It produced two singles that got play on MTV and a spot on South Park’s first soundtrack album.

SOAD released their second album, *Toxicity*, on September 3, 2001. Their release party concert drew an unexpectedly large crowd and resulted in a 6 hour riot. The album debuted at number 1 on the Billboard Hot 100, reflecting the popularity of nu metal at the time. The lead single, “Chop Suey!”, had a promising first week on the charts, starting at 76 and rising. The song was originally titled “Suicide”, but the label forced them to change it; “Chopped suey” is a Chinese dish, but also if you “chop” the word “suicide” in half you get “sui”. Unfortunately, just over a week after the album’s release, 19 men affiliated with the terrorist group al-Qaeda hijacked four commercial airplanes and used them to destroy the Twin Towers in New York, killing over 3,000 people. This put kind of a damper on the national mood, and many radio stations deemed “I don’t think you trust in my self-righteous suicide” inappropriate in light of an ideologically-motivated suicide attack. Many pulled “Chop Suey!” from all airplay in its second week, killing its momentum. Still, it received a Grammy nomination in 2002, and the album was a smash hit. It’s sold over 6,000,000 copies and is critically regarded as one of the masterpieces of nu and alternative metal. Malakian, who composed all the music, said he was inspired by The Beatles to include more harmony, pop melodies, and soft sections in *Toxicity* than he did in their debut. Tankian’s lyrics have also gained notoriety for their leftist and anti-establishment politics.

SOAD released their third album, *Steal This Album!*, in 2002 to modest success. They released their fourth and fifth albums, *Mezmerize* and *Hypnotize*, back-to-back in 2005. The track “B.Y.O.B.” won a Grammy in 2006. On these albums, Malakian seized creative control away from Tankian, whereas previous albums had an even collaboration. As a result of these creative differences, SOAD went on indefinite hiatus in 2006. They reunited in 2010 and have consistently toured since, but they Tankian refused to record any new music for the entire 2010s decade. In 2020, SOAD released two singles concerning the conflict between Armenia and Azerbaijan, but since then have gone back to only touring. Though fans are still holding out hope for a sixth album, the band has already cemented their place as one of the most successful metal bands of the 21st century.

**Rick Rubin Write-Up**

This is the second of three records we’ve listened to that was produced by Rick Rubin, perhaps the most important music producer of the end of the 20th century. The first was Kanye West’s *Yeezus*. Rubin was born and raised on Long Island before moving to Manhattan to attend NYU. Rubin was initially involved in the hardcore punk scene, befriending artists like Ian MacKaye of Minor Threat/Fugazi and bands like Hüsker Dü. Rubin founded Def Jam Recordings out of his dorm room in 1984 at age 21 to release punk records. However, Rubin soon met Russell Simmons, an aspiring record executive from Queens who had been involved in hip hop since 1977. He convinced Rubin to shift his focus from punk to hip hop; at the time, hip hop was a novelty party genre that had only gotten popular five years prior and it was not clear that it would have staying power. Simmons joined Def Jam to sign artists and Rubin would produce their records. Def Jam’s first release was a single by LL Cool J, a then-unknown MC. Rubin would produce LL Cool J’s debut studio album *Radio* in 1985, catapulting LL Cool J to national fame and starting what was at the time referred to as “new school hip hop” (online music nerds now call it “mid-school”). Def Jam followed this up by recording Run DMC’s (MC Run was Simmons’ brother) third album *Raising Hell* and the Beastie Boys’ (who were originally a hardcore punk band) debut *Licensed to Ill* in 1986. These three albums would all become wildly successful and essentially defined the form of modern hip hop. Def Jam also signed Public Enemy, another seminal rap group of the 1980s.

After revolutionizing hip hop, Rubin grew bored with the style and instead took interest in the burgeoning thrash metal scene. He signed Big Four band Slayer to Def Jam and produced their masterpiece *Reign in Blood* in 1986. Rubin was forced out of Def Jam in 1988, prompting him to leave NYC and move to LA. There, he continued to produce records for metal artists and also for burgeoning alternative rock bands like The Jesus and Mary Chain. As thrash became less popular, Rubin continued to work on the forefront of the metal scene through his production for Red Hot Chili Peppers, whose funk metal style exploded in 1991. As metal moved toward nu metal and alternative metal, Rubin became the producer for System of a Down. In one famous anecdote from the recording of *Toxicity*, Tankian came to Rubin complaining that he couldn’t write a bridge for “Chop Suey!”. Rubin told Tankian to open a random book to a random page, and just use those words. This is where the “Why have you forsaken me?” section of the song comes from (NOTE: that “book” just so happens to be the Bible, which makes me think this story is bunk. Rubin loves to tell it though).

In the 21st century, Rubin has become one of the most sought after (and most expensive) music producers in the world. He has produced music for artists as massive as Shakira, Justin Timberlake, Jay-Z, U2, Lady Gaga, and Adele. He’s also known for his collaborations with Kanye West in the 2010s. As the story goes, West sent a version of *Yeezus* to Rubin that was over 3 hours long and “only 30% complete” three weeks before the scheduled release date. Rubin cut it down and polished it into the album we know and love today. This kind of process is what Rubin is most known for; he believes strongly in minimalism and often tells artists he works with to cut out around half of the elements in their music. As a result of his influential work in hip hop, metal, and pop, and also thanks to his ridiculous beard, Rubin is still the most recognizable music producer alive today.

**Alternative Metal and Nu Metal Write-Up**

SOAD is associated with two major and related metal styles of the 1990s: alternative metal and nu metal. After British groups Black Sabbath pioneered heavy metal in the early 1970s and Iron Maiden reinvigorated it in the early 80s, different forms of metal would see mainstream success continuously for the next 30 years. First, hair metal groups like Mötley Crüe and Def Leppard dominated the charts for much of the mid-to-late 80s with their fusion of heavy metal sounds and glam rock theatrics. However, hair metal was more or less an artistic dead end (truly one of my least favorite genres ever), and they were supplanted in the late 80s by the unexpected success of underground thrash groups like Metallica, Megadeth, and Slayer. Though thrash became popular, it was never intended to be a pop genre like hair metal, so a vacuum opened for metal groups with more pop-friendly sounds. This sets the stage for the genres that will become nu metal.

In the late 80s, the term “alternative metal” was coined to describe weirdo, independent, and often punk-influenced metal groups as an analogue to “alternative rock”. Early examples include bassist Les Claypool (who sings the *South Park* theme song) and his quasi-comedy rock group Primus, college radio favorites Jane Addiction, and Seattle grunge pioneers Soundgarden. Despite the variance in style, most alternative metal groups differentiated themselves from thrash through slower tempos and melodic hooks. The first style within alternative metal to hit it big was funk metal, initially developed by Primus and Faith No More and then made famous by the Red Hot Chili Peppers in 1991 just as hair metal was dying. It combined the intensity and riffs of metal with the bouncy, danceable beats and bass lines from funk. One popular group to come out of this scene was Rage Against the Machine, who combined metal with another black genre: hip hop, specifically by rapping the vocals. Rage Against the Machine and Faith No More would heavily influence metal group Korn, whose 1994 self-titled debut record invented a style that would come to be known as “nu metal” (for whatever reason in the late 90s, they loved to call “modern” takes on genres “nu”. See also: nu jazz). Nu metal was instrumentally similar to funk metal but faster and more aggressive, the vocals were rapped, and the musicians dressed like white boys who were way too into hip hop. Nu metal would explode in popularity in the late 90s, with bands like Limp Bizkit and Linkin Park becoming some of the biggest in the world. SOAD are certainly considered a part of alternative metal, but whether they are also nu metal is debated.

By the early 2000s, the hangover from the 90s was starting to end. Nu metal and post-grunge (also called “butt rock”, the style of rock that was popular alongside nu metal, think Nickelback) were abandoned in the same way hair metal was when the hangover from the 80s ended in 1991. The styles and bands became the butt of jokes, the dying gasp of 90s alt rock culture that stuck around way too long. As a result, nu metal was often considered one of the worst genres of all-time; this is largely the reason why fans of bands like SOAD and Deftones, who are good, don’t like them being classed with nu metal. Metal remained popular through the 2000s, particularly a style called “metalcore” that fused metal with hardcore punk (Bring Me the Horizon, Bullet for My Valentine). In the 2010s, however, metal began to decline in popularity for the first time in decades. However, the early 2020s saw a short-lived nu metal revival movement bizarrely localized among female pop artists; Grimes, Rina Sawayama, and Poppy all released nu metal records that received some attention.

# Hargrove's review

*Toxicity* continues this week’s string of stellar albums. My original introduction to System of a Down was when I was 8 years old and playing *Rock Band 2*. “Chop Suey!” was one of the 90 or so songs from the main game library, and it was one of the ones that struck me the most. I remember the line “I cry when angels deserve to die” freaking me the fuck out and the entire “Why have you forsaken me?” section standing out. I found the quiet section to be so beautiful. My dad, however, thought it was stupid pop metal and, since I was very impressionable at the time, I just conformed to his opinion. However, I gave a listen through to this album back in March when we were trying to choose our first metal album and I was reminded of just how good “Chop Suey!” is. I’ve been listening to it regularly all year. Strangely, this album continues the ideas of up and down, fast and slow, quiet and loud that we heard prominently on both *Doolittle* and *The Black Saint and the Sinner Lady*. That’s a weird coincidence to me, but I’ve loved that theme we’ve had for the beginning of this week. Here, SOAD intertwines pop sweetness with relentless power and adds a lead singer who doesn’t sound like anyone else in history on top of it all. They really just don’t sound like anyone else before or since. Who could sound like them? Tankian is Tankian. Tankian’s politics are stupid and conspiratorial, but I have to respect the passion and sincerity he puts into singing about them. Malakian’s guitar is similarly expressive. He finds lots of guitar riffs that don’t sound like what I would expect in this style of music. The drums also consistently stand out as a highlight. The similarities with *Doolittle* are honestly striking: tight songwriting, catchy melodies, expressive vocals, independent guitar. Good album.

I want to hate “Prison Song” because it’s so ridiculous. It sounds like self-parody. But man, I have to respect putting something like this out there, especially as an album opener. Tankian sounds like he’s reading an essay. It’s insane. Honestly, it’s pretty good. It’s pretty good. I hate to say it, but it’s pretty good. “Needles” is a really fun song. “Pull the tapeworm out of your ass” is just such a funny line. I love the pre-chorus, and the whole thing is very catchy. Great song. “Deer Dance” is a little disjointed for my preference, but it makes up for it with its catchiness. “Jet Pilot” is the first miss, it’s a little forgettable. “X” is almost a favorite but not quite. It goes pretty hard but there’s just not enough to it. “Chop Suey!” is an absolutely GOATed song. One of the best of all time. The quiet sections are insanely beautiful and the primary guitar riff is one of my favorites. Pure, unadulterated bliss. “Bounce” as a follow-up kinda sucks though, definitely the low point of the album. “Forest” brings things back HARD though. What a good song. I *love* the way that the guitar line underscores the vocals in the chorus. “ATWA” is almost a favorite, I like the quiet verses a lot but I’m not crazy about the chorus. The lyrics on “Science” are really stupid but the guitar line is so epic it makes up for it. “Shimmy” also almost makes the favorites list, the chorus is quite fun, but it’s too underdeveloped for me. “Toxicity” is fantastic, the guitar glimmers and shimmers in a way that reminds me of Johnny Marr. It’s so wistful and I love the chorus, especially the drums. “Psycho” is another banger with a catchy chorus and one of the best quiet sections. I love this track on its own, and I love how seamlessly it connects to the tracks before and after it. “Aerials” is just unbelievable, it’s nearly as good as “Chop Suey!”. I love the vocal melody in the verses, the restrained guitar, the harmony on the chorus, the catchiness of the melody, all of it is so good. “Arto” is an interesting closer. It’s certainly unique, but I don’t feel much about it one way or the other.

Really fun metal album. It just takes all the best qualities of alternative rock and put them into metal. And there is no substitute. No one else sounds like this. Great, great listen.

Favorite tracks: Prison Song, Needles, Deer Dance, Chop Suey, Forest, Science, Toxicity, Psycho, Aerials

Least favorite track: Bounce

# Aidan's review

I really like how stupid this album can be at times. In some other hands, stuff like Prison Song could come off as preachy (I can never take mr "Harvard Poly Sci Grad" Tom Morello seriously when he's singing songs for Shining Path and Chairman Gonzalo), but Tankian is too earnest to heap scorn upon. He's like your dumb stoner friend that is too much fun to pay attention to his stupid political takes. There's hilariously dumb songs on here like Parasite and Bounce, there's songs held together by their surprisingly soothing soft sections like Chop Suey and Aerials, and there's the rest of the album that just radiates energy. The songs are a tad formulaic with their quiet/loud dynamic (but points for full choruses), and nothing here is incredibly creative, but it just works, and I gotta enjoy any metal-adjacent album that doesn't have a fucking double bass drum at all. It's just good fun, nothing revolutionary, but they hit their marks and it's an enjoyable listen. The stupidity does get a little grating if you leave it going for too long, but in 45 minute doses, it's great.
