# Write-up

We left off with Talk Talk after they had released Spirit of Eden, the groundbreaking yet profoundly uncommercial album that, along with Slint's Spiderland, established the genre of post-rock. The story of Laughing Stock, the follow up and final album, is more of the same. The band was down to a duo both officially and unofficially. Officially, the band consisted of Mark Hollis, the lead singer and multi instrumentalist and Lee Harris on drums. Unofficially, while Harris was still playing in the band, Hollis and producer Tim Friese-Greene exercised total control, while Harris was for all purposes a studio musician. Talk Talk's relationship with their label, EMI, was also breaking down. EMI was rereleasing old songs to eke out some semblance of a profit from the band, which made the band unhappy, and Hollis insisted on making music that was impossible to sell, which made the label unhappy. After a few lawsuits, the relationship ended and Talk Talk signed with Verve Records, a jazz label who gave them a blank check and creative control for their next album.

The story now takes on a familiar tone. Freed of any sort of oversight, Hollis and Friese-Greene's methods began to resemble madness. Musicians were encouraged to just improvise with little idea of what the song would sound like, and the two would select the few takes they liked the best from each instrument. Hollis worked in darkness with only a strobe light, installed his own paintings in the studio. They put mics 30 feet away from the drums, which caused problems because the speed of sound would cause the drums to lag the rest of the tracks, which had to be fixed in production.

Shockingly, the resulting album was not a big seller. Critical opinion was mixed at the time, and the label despaired. The record miraculously managed to chart for two weeks in the UK, but it never came close to making a profit. Talk Talk soon broke up as its members pursued other projects and Hollis wanted to spend more time with his family. Post breakup, their two post-rock albums became more and more acclaimed to the point where among internet music nerds, they are far more famous for Spirit of Eden and Laughing Stock than for anything that made it to radio. Hollis died from cancer in 2019.

# Hargrove's review

When this list finally made me listen to *Spirit of Eden* after having it recommended to me for years, I was not impressed. I was at kind of a low-point for my appreciation of post-rock anyway, but the mumbled vocals, sparse-to-the-point-of-empty arrangements, and stop-start improvised structures did not work for me at all. So imagine my shock during my second listen of *Laughing Stock*, an album that really is quite similar to its predecessor, when I realized it wasn’t half-bad! Don’t get me wrong, it’s not like its my favorite album or anything, but it made some minor tweaks that improve the formula in a major way. The biggest thing that stands out to me is the primacy of the drumming. Three tracks on this album, including the two longest, have a consistent drum line throughout. This helps prevent things from getting too sparse or static and lets the schizophrenic instrumentation grow around a nucleus. But beyond just the structure they add, I have to give major props to Lee Harris for the quality of his drumming here. The obvious reference point is jazz, and the way he rides those cymbals is certainly jazzy. However, there’s seemingly a lot of funk and, dare I say, hip hop in there too. The drums to “New Grass” would later be sampled by UNKLE, but like the drums in “After the Flood” could straight up be a breakbeat sample. In addition to the drums, the overall jazzy nature makes the improvisations feel more natural to me. Everything flows well, making the little bursts of new instruments exciting rather than exhausting. The climaxes were also more gratifying, from the guitar freakouts and Variophon solo of “After the Flood”, to the genuinely shocking trumpet stab around 6:10 in “Taphead”, and most especially to the drums in the final 30 seconds of “Ascension Day”. This album very much still has its issues, particularly the static songwriting of both the opening and closing track. I also still find Hollis’ voice more irritating than it is expressive, and the overall style is not one that I care to seek out often. But, man, did I like this more than I expected to. It even made me revisit *Spirit of Eden*, which is definitely not as good but I like it more now than I initially did.

The timbres on “Myrrhman” are immediately reminiscent of *Spirit of Eden*, as is the sparseness. I’m not sold on Hollis’ style of minimalism so I find that emptiness works against the track significantly, but it does set the tone for the album well. I also like the double bass quite a bit. “Ascension Day” is far and away the best track on either Talk Talk album we’ve listened to. I *really* like it, and not just relatively but in an absolute sense. The drums stand out immediately after the barebones opener. The driving ride cymbal quickly gives way to loud crashes and a wonderfully syncopated rhythm. I love the way that instruments come in and out, specifically the organ and the searing guitar. There’s a loud/quiet dynamic that rivals the best Pixies or Nirvana songs. The little one bar guitar explosions form raging, intense tempests in teapots. Then there’s the release of the final 30 seconds, an orgasmic drum line that stops dead in its tracks in this album’s finest moment. “After the Flood” is not quite so good as the previous track, but it’s the second best Talk Talk song on either album. The atmospheric instrumental of the first two minutes is gorgeous, and the drums generate a propulsive hip hop rhythm that reminds me a bit of the Stone Roses’ “Fool’s Gold”. There’s also a very low bass which adds significant heft. The Variophon solo in the midsection sounds apocalyptic, befitting the diluvian title and lyrics. While the guitar line at the beginning of “Taphead” sounds bluesy and promising, the aimlessness of its midsection threatens to doom the song to the irrelevance of this album’s bookends. However, the short stab of trumpet sounds around 6:10 is so shocking that I have to make this a favorite track. It’s a great moment. I’m a little conflicted on “New Grass”; the drum rhythm is easily the worst of the three songs driven by the drums because it lacks that funkiness, but at the same time the rest of the instrumental sounds gorgeous. It’s just so delicate, particularly the impressionism of the piano starting around 7:00. Both “New Grass” and “Taphead” are flawed and worse than either “Ascension Day” or “After the Flood”, but I’m including them in my favorite tracks because their highs are quite high. “Runeii” struggles from the same problems as the opener, but at least the opener sets the tone well.

This was one of the most surprising listens I’ve had in a while, maybe since Yes’ *Close to the Edge*. I foresee myself listening to “Ascension Day” semi-regularly, and revisiting this album in full on occasion.

Favorite tracks: Ascension Day, After the Flood, Taphead, New Grass

Least favorite track: Runeii

# Aidan's review

I had a lot of enthusiasm for Spirit of Eden based off how much I liked this album back in freshman year and while Spirit of Eden wasn't that good, listening to this again reminded me that the hope was well earned. The obvious highlight is "Ascension Day", the cutoff ending is just magical. The drumming throughout is also amazing, I feel like most studio gimmicks are just to make the band sound cool, but moving the drums far from the recording mics makes the snare drum sound delightfully subdued. Hollis's sporadic vocals work, and the usage of chosen instrumental parts creates a feeling of flow and structureless, there's a buildup at the beginning and a drawdown at the end (except for "Ascension Day"), but in between there's no strong feeling of direction. It's a very zen album, one that you can (and I did) listen to on loop without really noticing where one song ends and the next begins. It doesn't feel like music made by humans, and it's all the better because of it.
