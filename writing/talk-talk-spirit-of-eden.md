# Write-up

Talk Talk was born as a synth pop band, with Mark Hollis singing, Lee Harris on drums, Paul Webb on guitar and Simon Brenner on keyboard, who left after the first album. Tim Friese-Greene is often cited as an uncredited 4th member. Although he didn't tour with the band, he worked with Hollis on every album after the first, producing, playing keyboard and writing songs with Hollis. They were originally categorized as part of the larger New Romantic movement, which drew inspiration from glam rock icons such as David Bowie, Marc Bolan (of T. rex) and Roxy Music (Eno's original band), as well as the Romantic poets. Duran Duran is probably the biggest band to come out of the movement and the two were often compared, due to sharing a producer (Colin Thurston), label (EMI) and a style of name (Word Word). Their first album, "The Party's Over", achieved mild commercial success, but it was their second studio album, "It's My Life", that catapulted them into stardom, two years after Duran Duran made the same leap with "Rio". The band was already showing signs of chafing at their success before their third album came out, making a mockery of their music video for "It's My Life". They followed that up with a style shift for their third album, "The Color of Spring", which combined jazz influences with art pop. While it was more successful than its predecessor in their native UK, it failed to reach the same heights as "It's My Life" internationally.

The band had finally reached the point in their careers where commercial success had allowed them the freedom from their label, as they were given a large budget and creative freedom for their fourth album, which would become Spirit of Eden. The album was a large shift for the band. Not only did they move from EMI to the imprint Parlophone (formerly run by none other than George Martin), but they brought in extra musicians as Hollis and Freise-Greene plumbed unexplored music territory. In a familiar pattern for this list, the resulting album, which incorporated elements of jazz, ambient, classical, blues and dub into what would be dubbed "post rock", was not popular with the label. Due to its heavily produced nature, the band refused to play the album live. It also lacked any sort of a single and the band would not do a music video. It took a lawsuit by the band to end the record contract, and the album itself was not commercially successful, driving the band to rerelease their old hits as a way to fund their future projects. The band, now officially a duo after Webb left, doubled down on this groundbreaking style with their final album, "Laughing Stock", which is also on the list.

While Talk Talk had always claimed to be influenced by jazz, it wasn't obvious until their third album. Spirit of Eden took 8 months of 65 hour weeks to produce in a studio lit only by strobe lights and projectors (not unlike what the Beatles did for Sgt Pepper). While lacking a traditional single, "I Believe in You", an anti-heroin song, was released as a radio edit by EMI, who was desperate to salvage what was described as "the kind of record which encourages marketing men to commit suicide". The band didn't see any problem with it, and were actually surprised at the lack of commercial success when the album only sold half a million copies. In retrospect, both it and its successor, "Laughing Stock" have gone on to become critically beloved and alone with Spiderland, seen as the two founding albums of post-rock.

# Hargrove's review

I’m pretty sure I tried this album before at some point when I was 18 or so, around the time I was getting into Godspeed. Similar to Dan, I saw this was another big post-rock album everyone loves, and I gave it a shot; it was just too sparse, a little too out of reach. However, I had a similar reaction the first time I listened to *American Football* when I was 16, shortly after getting intoThe Dismemberment Plan, and when I revisited that album later I absolutely fell in love. So I was hopeful going into this. On the flip side, I haven’t loved post-rock that much recently for whatever reason. I used to think of myself as a big fan, but I was surprisingly underwhelmed when re-listening to both Godspeed albums again after a couple years, and I really wasn’t impressed by the Stereolab album. Yes, I still loved *Spiderland*, thought *Soundtracks* was super cool, and was quite impressed by *Flood* as well, but those all have a certain punk edge to them that made everything work for me. Talk Talk does not have this same punk edge. Nevertheless, I was determined to like this album. And the results were…ehhhhhh? It’s not bad by any means. There are a lot of cool parts to the record. I particularly like “Desire”, where all the tension built by the first two tracks finally comes to a climax. The guitar on that song is great, the vocals work really well, and the percussion is executed masterfully. There are also some really beautiful parts near the end of the record. “I Believe in You” is the only track where I found the lyrics interesting, but man were they heart-rending. And the backing vocals are just angelic. Really pretty track. And the closer, “Wealth”, uses that repetitive organ bit really well. It sounds like I’m in church, and though it’s a bit static, it creates a strong atmosphere. Overall though, I think things are unfortunately middling. This album is extremely ephemeral, and that brings both good and bad. There are so many small moments of beauty, small moments of release, fleeting wisps of interesting ideas, but they’re abandoned too quickly to become transcendent. In a way, I respect that. It makes me think about impermanence, and, for a record with such Christian lyrical themes, that makes the temporary nature of life hit hard. At the same time, though, let something develop! We don’t need a Godspeed crescendo, but just throw something to hold onto, something to take with me and replay in my head once the album’s over. “The Rainbow” and “Eden”, the two opening tracks, function well enough as one big lead-in to the explosions of “Desire” that I can forgive their more aimless, formless qualities. “Inheritance”, on the other hand, doesn’t justify its meandering, and it’s my least favorite track because of it. As an overall thing, I also don’t love Mark Hollis’ voice. He sounds mumbly at times, and is just so nasally. Not a huge deal, but it gets grating over the course of a record. Not a bad album, and I liked it much better than Dan and my dad do, but not entirely deserving of its stature. I do see myself returning to it in the future, though, for sure.

Favorite tracks: Desire, I Believe in You, Wealth

Least favorite track: Inheritance

# Aidan's review

One of the original post rock albums, this is the progenitor of the more ambient side of the genre, with heirs like Tortoise and Mogwai. I found this extremely similar to Hex by Bark Psychosis, an album I listened to earlier because it was listed as similar to Godspeed. Both have a very non committal style, avoiding the big crescendos and sampling that would come to be more common in the genre in favor of a meandering instrumentation. There’s still moment of stress and distressing and I really thought that the harshness of the instruments was used well to create that contrast, with the blaring of the sax on the opener standing out as one of the best moments. Lyrically, it’s not great, although I thought the vocal style was well suited, I never found lyrics interesting. More broadly, the album felt rather ambient, there’s no enough to latch onto, and while I thought it created a great atmosphere, it never went anywhere. Listening to other post rock stuff in comparison, I found that the genre really benefited from the usage of audio sampling and krautrock influences. I still enjoyed it because I’m a sucker for post rock, but it’s more influential than great on its own.
