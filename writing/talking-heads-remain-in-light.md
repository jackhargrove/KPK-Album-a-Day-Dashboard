# Write-up

Story/Fun Facts: There aren’t too many interesting stories about the creation of this album like there were about the last three, so I’ll just give some color on the people involved. Talking Heads formed in the mid 70s at the Rhode Island School of Design (RISD) in Providence, though they quickly moved to New York. David Byrne was the lead singer and songwriter, Tina Weymouth played bass, Chris Frantz played drums, and Jerry Harrison played keyboard and guitar.

*Remain in Light* was Talking Heads’ fourth album, and the third and final produced by the legendary Brian Eno (more on him later). Their first three albums had a few hits that you’ve all probably heard, in particular “Psycho Killer” off their first album. They existed in the world of punk rock, but their subversion of punk tropes and fusion of diverse musical styles earned them the label “post-punk”, which eventually became “New Wave” when punk went commercial in the 80s. The studio album that followed *Remain in Light*, *Speaking in Tongues*, is also on this list.

Brian Eno, the producer, was already at this time a very accomplished experimental musician. In 1978, his album *Ambient 1: Music for Airports*, which is on this list, started the ambient genre. His other album on this list was a collaboration with David Byrne recorded right before the sessions for *Remain in Light*.

# Hargrove's review

As someone who played the tuba for 10 years, I have always been partial to the rhythm section. This is the first album on the list that I would consider primarily rhythmically-driven, and it ROCKS. I love the African drum beats, I love the staccato down-up strumming of the rhythm guitars that turns them into percussion, I love the funky bass, I love how David Byrne’s repetitive vocals function rhythmically more than melodically. it’s chaotic, it’s weird, it’s frantic, but despite all of that, you can still dance to it! Put simply, this album is my shit. I was plenty familiar with the Talking Heads before this, I had heard many of their singles including “Once in a Lifetime”, but I had never listened to this album. What a shame that it took me this long.

The first half of this album is just so driving. You don’t get a second to breathe from the opening second of “Born Under Punches (The Heat Goes On)” through to the end of “Once in a Lifetime”. The tempo is high, David Byrne sounds paranoid and out of his mind, the bass is relentless, and the percussion is never for a second normal. I’ll talk about “Once in a Lifetime” individually, but consider this my review for tracks 1-3: all bangers, all the time.

“Once in a Lifetime” is kind of a bridge between the first half of the album and the second half of the album. It’s manic like the first half, but takes a contemplative tone in both its instrumental and lyrics like the second half. Musically, it takes everything that makes this album interesting, but it puts it into a more palatable pop package. The vocal melody is just so catchy. The lyrics describe a man who has been living his life on autopilot suddenly waking up, going “how the hell did the last 20 years go by and I don’t know what the fuck happened?” Make sure you’re living in the moment, they’re finite.

The last four tracks are a lot more low-key. While I do prefer the first half, I like these songs as well. “Houses in Motion” turns its string instruments percussive, with the frantic strumming of the guitar being just as important as the notes being strummed. “Seen and Not Seen” is very good but probably my least favorite just because it’s a little too mellow musically for me. “Listening Wind” is also mellow, but I like how seedy it feels. It sounds like being in a gross basement bar at 3 am, especially the low-pitched undulating synth. “The Overload” is a cool closer, those sustained guitar reverb sounds in the instrumental are almost like drone, sounds dark and ominous.

Many artists before, and many artists since, have tried to combine rock and hip-hop to varying degrees of success. In my opinion, this record is one of the most successful; it incorporated the spirit of hip-hop, the beat, the grooves, the loops, and incorporated them seamlessly into the chaotic sound of punk.

Fantastic album, going into my direct rotation and somewhere in my top 25 albums of all time list. Makes yuo dance, but also makes yuo think?????? I am biased because I am a government man.

9.5/10

Favorite tracks: Born Under Punches (The Heat Goes On), Crosseyed and Painless, The Great Curve, Once in a Lifetime, Houses in Motion, Listening Wind

Least favorite track: Seen and Not Seen

# Aidan's review

Borderline perfect first 5 songs, loses momentum with Seen and Not Seen and The Overload. Eno’s production is so good, the guitar and drums are incredibly crisp. The lack of emphasis on Byrne’s vocals and sense of humor is probably why there is only one big single from this album, but the overall vibe is great.
