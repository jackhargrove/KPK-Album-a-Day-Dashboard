# Write-up

Television is as New York as they come, but the band's genesis was in Hockessin, Delaware, where Thomas Miller met Richard Meyers while they were both attending the Sanford School, a private K-12 school. The two ran away together to Alabama before being arrested and returned to their parents. Both were heavily interested in poetry and music. After Miller graduated and Meyers flunked out, both moved to New York City and took new names. Thomas Miller became Tom Verlaine while Richard Meyers became Richard Hell. In 1972, the two decided to form a band. Verlaine played lead guitar while Hell played bass and the two got Verlaine's friend Billy Vicca to play drum, calling their first group the Neon Boys. In 1973, the band changed its name to Television and added Richard Lloyd on guitar, landing recurring gigs at influential punk clubs CBGB and Max's Kansas City. The band's reputation grew among the New York scene, and the band grew more professional, with the exception of Hell, who never grew out of his teenage rebelliousness. Combined with Verlaine's increasing creative control over the songwriting duties he and Hell had initially shared, tensions grew until Hell was forced out of the group. Hell would go on to have his own successful career both solo and headlining the Vocaloids. To replace him on bass, the band snagged Fred Smith, who at the time was playing for another local band without any releases, Blondie.

The band had their pick of record deals, and did some demo tapes with Brian Eno, including several songs that would end up on Marquee Moon, but Verlaine called Eno's production "very cold and brittle". The band flirted with other labels before finally landing with Elektra Records in 1976 with a blank check and near complete creative freedom. The band was well rehearsed, putting down the title track in a single take that was left completely untouched on the final product. All but the final two songs on the album were staples of their live shows, with the remaining two being written in the studio. The album was released to glowing critical reception in 1977, which managed to drive sales in the UK but not the US. The band had a very successful tour of the UK and were reportedly massive jerks to Blondie, who were supporting them. The album, and its follow-up, Adventure, failed to sell in the US, which prompted the band to break up in 1978. The band reunited to put out a self titled album in the mid 90s and played a few live shows after that. There were movements towards a fourth album in the 2010s before Verlaine died in 2023. By then, Marquee Moon had risen to the top of the rock pantheon and is often listed among the greatest albums of all time.

# Hargrove's review

I had heard of Television and was vaguely familiar with their place and time as well as their influence, but I had never actually listened to the music. This has to be the start of what I think of as the post-punk atmosphere, sound, and feel. Those interlocking guitars create so many fascinating possibilities, and yet are still so distinctive to one particular sound. This was a much-needed bridge, a missing evolutionary link I needed in the map of music history I have in my head. Songs like “Marquee Moon” and “Torn Curtain” are roadmaps for post-punk. Alternatively, songs like “Friction” and “Prove It” sound like classic rock songs played by schizos. As a historical document, it’s an amazing Rosetta Stone for me. Unfortunately, that strength is also somewhat of a weakness, as I don’t love some of the songs like “Prove it” that reveal the basic punk origins. They are by far the weakest tracks. When these guys are unleashed, though, there is no ceiling to how good the song can be. There’s an atmosphere that is both consistent and unique, driven by the dual guitars and Verlaine’s crazed vocals. The album doesn’t quite overstay its welcome, though there are a couple songs I don’t care for. I was not particularly moved by the lyrics; I’m surprised to see that the lyrics are so highly regarded by some. I think “Marquee Moon” and “See No Evil” have consistently interesting lyrics, and there are some good lines elsewhere (“I fell right into the arms of the Venus de Milo” is quite good), but overall I was not crazy for them. The lyrics are largely unneeded though. The atmosphere is a weapon of such focused energy that the music beams the images of gritty but colorful New York City life straight into my brain.

To get the elephant out of the room, “Marquee Moon” is a behemoth of a post-punk track that stands on its own. It’s honestly just so cool. The interplay between the two guitars is unlike anything else I’ve ever heard. I particularly love how the rhythm between the two shifts somewhat after the first minute or so. The song’s structure is novel and very well done, and there’s not a second that I would cut. It’s very deserving of its standing. I thought the closer “Torn Curtain” was also very impressive. The guitar sounds amazingly good right off the bat. I don’t even really know how to describe it. The main riff sounds wrong in a way that is so right. And then on the chorus you get a moment of pure, unadulterated melodrama, which I wasn’t expecting from the closer. It strikes me every time. Speaking of good choruses, I am certainly a fan of the one on “Elevation”. It has a stellar vocal line and guitars that clash and clang. What more is there to ask for? “See No Evil” is an interesting opener because it’s the tightest pop packaging of the ideas on this album. Instead of introducing these new sounds and slowly pulling them together into something recognizable, Television instead starts with that recognizable product and then unravels it. “Venus” is a tad slow for my tastes but I like the guitar riff after the chorus enough to name it a favorite track. It’s very baroque.

The back half of this album is much worse than the front because of the songs “Guiding Light” and “Prove It”. These are two pretty low lows. Both songs are over five minutes and a complete slog to get through, worse with the former than the latter because of how sentimental and sappy it feels. “Friction” is much better than those two, but I’m still not crazy about it. The atonal descending guitar riff is the best part, but otherwise the structure sounds too much like classic rock.

This album is more uneven than I think many people would care to admit, but it certainly has the swagger to make up for it. Cool album with a couple transcendent tracks.

Favorite tracks: See No Evil, Venus, Marquee Moon, Elevation, Torn Curtain

Least favorite track: Guiding Light

# Aidan's review

This is one of the few albums that exist in my mind not as a collection of songs that accumulate to form an album but rather one coherent whole, as if the first time I listened to it, I heard every song at once. At this point I've listened to Marquee Moon enough to not remember the first time I've listened to it. Everything just sounds right, it's the soundtrack to the era of New York City that I remember through the borrowed nostalgia of Martin Scorsese and Paul Auster. The title track is an all time favorite but the entire album is just so well put together, so sleek, so cool. The intertwining guitars are so tight, the album never loses its momentum, it's just a great listening experience. My one gripe is that after the ecstasy that is the title track, Prove It and Torn Curtain feel like an extended epilogue but that's just suffering from success. Great, great album.
