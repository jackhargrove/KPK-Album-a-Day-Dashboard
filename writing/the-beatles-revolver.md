# Write-up

The Beatles were a British pop/rock outfit formed in Liverpool in 1960 consisting of rhythm guitarist John Lennon, bassist Paul McCartney, lead guitarist George Harrison, and drummer Ringo Starr (real name Richard Starkey). Lead vocals on any given song were typically delivered by whoever wrote the song, with the exception that Ringo was generally given one song to sing per album despite the fact that he wasn’t much of a songwriter. Beatles history crash course: 16 year-old John forms skiffle (British version of rock n roll) group called The Quarrymen in 1956. 15 year-old Paul joins in 1957. 15 year-old George joins in 1958 as Paul’s friend despite John’s objections. Everyone else leaves the group, and our 3 boys look for new members. John’s friend Stu Sutcliffe joins in 1960 and suggests the name “Beatals” as a tribute to Buddy Holly’s band The Crickets. After a few minor changes, they land on The Beatles in 1960. Drummer Pete Best joins the band in 1960 and they leave Liverpool for a residency at a club in Hamburg, Germany, where they all get addicted to meth so they can play 6 hours a day, 7 days a week. They stay in Hamburg on-and-off for almost 2 years. In late 1961, the Beatles return to Liverpool, but Stu stays in Hamburg for a girl; he’ll be dead within 6 months. Now expert live performers, the 4 Beatles become regulars at the Cavern Club in Liverpool. There, music manager Brian Epstein discovers them and becomes their manager. Epstein gets them auditions with various labels; after getting rejected by Decca (shame they will never live down), they catch the eye of producer George Martin at EMI Parlophone. The Beatles sign with EMI and record for the first time in June 1962. Martin thinks Pete Best is a terrible drummer and convinces The Beatles to fire him. They recruit well-known local Liverpool drummer Ringo Starr as his replacement, and the final lineup is set.

The Beatles released their first UK album (at the time, different countries would get different albums), *Please Please Me*, in early 1963. The style was Merseybeat, a melodic form of guitar pop played in Liverpool in the early 60s. John and Paul, the group’s primary songwriters, begin the extremely fruitful Lennon-McCartney partnership: every song that either of them writes, even if done completely alone (as was usually the case), would be credited to both as Lennon-McCartney. Bolstered by lead single “Love Me Do”, *Please Please Me* reached #1 on the UK charts. Their non-album single “She Loves You” became the fastest selling single of all time in the UK. They became a sensation in Britain that was dubbed “Beatlemania” by the end of 1963, intensified by another smash hit non-album single “I Want to hold Your Hand”. Before the year was out, they released their second UK album, *With The Beatles*, which contained the first song ever written by George. In early 1964, Beatlemania finally made it to America: their first North American album (these are now considered non-canon, as it were; the UK releases are considered the real albums) released in January, and in February they flew to NYC to play the popular TV program *The Ed Sullivan Show*. They were now as massive in the US as they were in the UK. In July 1964, they released their third album, *A Hard Day’s Night*, which accompanied a critically acclaimed film of the same name. It was their first album consisting entirely of original songs. In August, they met Bob Dylan, who introduced them to three things: the American folk movement, what it was like to be critically acclaimed, and, most importantly, marijuana. They quickly put out their fourth (and worst) album *Beatles for Sale* at the end of 1964, but they became determined to make music that was critically acclaimed, not just commercially successful. Around the same time, in early 1965, George’s dentist secretly dosed him and John with LSD; the pair soon after became obsessed with acid and introduced Ringo to the stuff as well, but Paul refused to take it. They released their 5th album, *Help!*, in summer 1965, which was a good album but a little too obvious in its Dylan imitation. The Beatles were finally successful in being taken serious critically with their 6th album *Rubber Soul*, released in December 1965. *Rubber Soul* was the first great Beatles album from front-to-back, and one of the first albums to be more than just a random group of songs put together for commercial release. It marked the end of their boy band period, and the beginning of their shift into weirder, more interesting stuff. It also lit a fire under Brian Wilson’s ass, inspiring him to write *Pet Sounds* for The Beach Boys as a response.

As the most famous band in the world, and with their profile growing as a “serious” group, the Beatles began to face controversies and backlash in the mid 60s. The most famous of these was when John claimed they were “bigger than Jesus”, which got them heavily protested in the US. The Beatles also grew very tired of touring; they had been playing concerts nonstop since 1962, and their popularity was such that the crowds at their concerts were so loud that no one could hear the music. In summer of 1966 they released *Revolver*, a common pick for the best Beatles album, containing classics like Paul’s “Eleanor Rigby” and their most experimental song to date, John’s “Tomorrow Never Knows”. The tour they did after *Revolver* would prove to be their last; after playing the final date in San Francisco, the Beatles retired from live performance, becoming a studio band full time. Their newfound free time allowed them to spend more time writing, but it also imposed high pressure on them to release more music. Their first sessions for a new album yielded the songs “Strawberry Fields Forever” (John), “Penny Lane”, and “When I’m 64” (both Paul). The studio forced them to release the former two as singles, meaning they couldn’t be on the album, but they were extremely critically acclaimed and created much anticipation for the forthcoming album. Additionally, Paul finally decided to try to LSD with the rest of the band, and he became the first to talk about his drug use with the press not long after. And finally, *Pet Sounds*, which itself had been designed to compete with *Rubber Soul*, became the new best album to beat in the competition between The Beatles and the Beach Boys. *Sgt. Pepper’s Lonely Hearts Club Band* came out in May 1967 to unprecedented critical and commercial success. They became hippie icons, and helped to kick off the Summer of Love in 1967. They were quickly disillusioned with the hippie movement, however, and sought some deeper meaning in life. In August, they met the Maharishi Mahesh Yogi, an Indian spiritual leader (huge grifter) and became enamored with India. A few days later, their manager Brian Epstein died of a possibly intentional drug overdose. Brian Epstein had kept the Beatles together with an iron fist for 5 years, and they were completely rudderless without him. For the first time, the Beatles had no direction, no idea what to do next.

Paul had dominated the sessions for *Sgt. Pepper’s* and saw himself as the natural leader in Epstein’s absence. The first thing they did without Epstein was the psychedelic TV movie *Magical Mystery Tour*, a complete and total mess that was ridiculed and was the first real misstep the Beatles ever took. That being said, the resulting LP contained new great songs like John’s “I Am the Walrus” and Paul’s “The Fool on the Hill”. Hoping to shake off the failure and find themselves, all four Beatles went on a now legendary trip to India in early 1968. They went to an ashram led by the Maharishi Mahesh Yogi with a group of other celebrities including members of The Beach Boys and actress Mia Farrow. They primarily went at the behest of George, who was somewhat of a weeaboo for India. John was the second most enthusiastic, as he had just begun cheating on his wife with Japanese artist Yoko Ono and genuinely sought spiritual clarity. Paul went because he thought the whole India thing would be good for his image. Ringo went because everyone else was going, but he went home after less than 2 weeks because he was a very picky eater. Paul left a month later when he got bored. John began to grow disillusioned as he didn’t find the clarity that he sought, and he became convinced that the Maharishi was a fraud. John convinced George, who was a true believer, that the Maharishi was trying to sleep with women on the trip, and they left after 2 months. The Beatles reconvened in London in May of 1968 to begin work on their ninth studio album. These sessions were extremely tense: Paul was becoming more and more controlling and his relationship with John was strained, John began a heroin addiction and started bringing Yoko Ono to rehearsals, George was becoming a prolific songwriter but neither John nor Paul would take him seriously, and Ringo hated how negative the atmosphere was that he quit the band for 2 weeks. They had also just started their own business venture, Apple Corps, which was a ticking time bomb disaster. The sessions yielded the single “Hey Jude” (Paul) and their first ever double album, *The Beatles* (typically referred to as “The White Album”). This record was extremely scattershot, as it became abundantly clear that there was almost no collaboration between the members on the songwriting. There were also some highly experimental pieces, severe genre shifts, and the first ever song written by Ringo (“Don’t Pass Me By”)! It was released in late 1968 as the first release on Apple Records.

With The Beatles on the verge of collapse, Paul took it upon himself to orchestrate a brand new project in early 1969 with the hopes of bringing everyone back together. So began the *Get Back* sessions, named after the first song they (Paul) wrote as part of the project. The idea was to make a back-to-basics blues rock record and film the entire recording process. Unfortunately, many of the problems from the White Album sessions persisted: John hated being managed by Paul and they were barely on speaking terms, John missed half of the sessions because he was high on smack with Yoko, and George began to hate Paul because he kept shooting down his songs, causing George to quit the band for a weekend. A fun added element to the drama was that John, George, and Ringo, in an effort to subvert Paul’s new authority, voted to make Allen Klein their new manager. Klein was a sleazy crook who had previously managed The Rolling Stones, and those three bozos put him in charge of all of their money just to spite Paul. Probably the biggest mistake made at any point in The Beatles career, and the resultant money problems stretched tensions to their breaking point. Nevertheless, the *Get Back* sessions were fruitful, and The Beatles played their final ever live performance on January 30, 1969: a surprise performance of their new songs on the roof of their building in central London at lunch time. People on their lunch break from work were treated to the first Beatles concert in 3 years, and this performance has become legendary. Afterward, The Beatles gave their recordings from the *Get Back* sessions to engineer Glyn Johns to fashion into an album, and then they kinda forgot about it for a while.

By summer of 1969, Paul hated John, John hated Paul, George hated Paul, Paul was sad that George hated him, John was ambivalent toward George, George was desperate for John’s approval, and Ringo felt like the child of a divorce (even though he was the oldest). The end was near. Thankfully, they had the presence of mind to realize that they were the fucking Beatles, and they decided to make one last album before calling it quits for good. They began recording at EMI Recording Studio on Abbey Road in London. Producer George Martin wanted the album to be one continuous composition, but John refused because he didn’t want to have to collaborate with Paul at all; they compromised, and side 1 was made up of solo contributions from all four Beatles (including Ringo!), and side 2 being a medley. The last time all four were in a studio together was August 20, 1969. Exactly a month later, John told the rest that he was leaving The Beatles, but he agreed to keep that private as they were still releasing their remaining records. *Abbey Road*, the final album recorded by The Beatles, was released in September 1969. And that was that…except, can’t forget those *Get Back* sessions, which still hadn’t been turned into an album. They somehow found their way into the hands of famous producer Phil Spector, who added his Wall of Sound technique to the songs, including entire orchestras for some tracks. This pissed off Paul, who thought Spector had ruined the album. Klein ignored Paul’s objections and Paul was so pissed off that he stole John’s thunder and publicly announced that he was leaving The Beatles in April 1970. One month later, they released *Let It Be*, the final Beatles album, although not the last one recorded. After 10 very productive years, the Beatles were officially finished. George was 26, Paul was 27, John was 28, and Ringo was 29.

Where are they now? After the breakup, Paul, John, and George raced each other to set up solo careers (Ringo also went solo, but he was less concerned with the competition aspect). John had a head start, having already released material with his now-wife Yoko Ono. He had come to fashion himself a left-wing political icon, releasing preachy songs about world peace and prejudice. His most notable solo album was 1971’s *Imagine*, featuring the eponymous hit song. Paul began to record with his wife Linda McCartney, releasing *Ram* (my personal favorite Beatles solo album) together in 1971. It was recorded on Paul’s farm and contained a lo-fi but melodic sound that would become very influential on the development of indie pop, with some going so far as calling it the first indie pop album (which is, to be fair, ridiculous). Paul and Linda would recruit a few more musicians and formed the band Wings, which became quite successful. George was sitting on a tremendous backlog of songs he had written while with The Beatles but that John and Paul had rejected. He turned these songs into a triple (!) album called *All Things Must Pass* in 1970, which is probably the consensus best Beatles solo album. Ringo played drums for some of John’s solo work, and also released his only two solo hits in the early 70s: “Photograph” in 1973 and “You’re Sixteen” (ew) in 1974. There were still major tensions between the members even after the breakup. The opening song on Paul’s *Ram* included some veiled insults toward John; in response, he wrote the song “How Do You Sleep?” that was a far more direct attack on Paul (more or less a diss track), and that included George on slide guitar and Ringo on drums. Brutal. They never forgave him for trying to save The Beatles. All four Beatles continued their solo careers through the rest of their lives but, as time went on, their new music became less and less relevant. John was the first to die when he was murdered by a crazed fan outside of his UWS apartment in New York in December 1980. George was the next to go, dying from lung cancer in 2001. Paul and Ringo are both still kicking, but both are by now well into their 80s. In the not-too-distant future, there won’t be any Beatles left, and that’s sad. But what a legacy they’ve left behind.

*Revolver* was the Beatles’ seventh album in the UK, releasing in August 1966. Coming shortly after the Beach Boys’ *Pet Sounds* in May and Bob Dylan’s *Blonde on Blonde* in June, it helped solidify the beginning of the album era and kickstart modern popular music as we know it. After *Rubber Soul* came out at the end of 1965 and was widely celebrated for the novel idea in rock of “make every song on the album actually good” (of course, this idea was stolen from Dylan’s *Highway 61 Revisited*), the members of the Beatles decided to spend the first three months of 1966 taking their first break since being signed by EMI Parlophone in 1962. This break coincided with new interests for the Beatles. John and George first tried LSD in 1965 and were now regularly tripping. They also got Ringo to try acid, but not Paul, whose drug of choice was still marijuana. George had first learned to play the sitar in 1965, as demonstrated on *Rubber Soul*’s “Norwegian Wood (This Bird Has Flown)”, but 1966 was the year he became fully obsessed with Indian culture and began composing Hindustani classical music. Paul started hanging around London’s underground art scene, where bands like Pink Floyd, Tyrannosaurus Rex, and other future progenitors of psychedelic and progressive rock were just getting their starts.

These bohemian interests inspired the Beatles to take a more expansive, eclectic approach when the band returned to the studio in April 1966. While *Rubber Soul* had been an unfathomable leap forward in quality and scope, *Revolver* would set itself even further apart from the sound of the Beatles’ early material. During the *Revolver* sessions, the Beatles began experimenting with studio recording technology to give the tracks effects and sounds that could not be created live. Many of these effects had the intent of recreating the sensations that John, George, and Ringo had felt while tripping on acid, combined with the electronic techniques being developed by avant-garde classical composers with whom Paul had become familiar during his adventures in London’s art scene. Also influential was music from India, even beyond George’s sitar composition “Love You To”. Many tracks feature fewer chord changes than is typical of pop rock, inspired by the drones of Hindustani classical music. “Raga rock”, as it was called, was one of the first crazes of the psychedelic rock era.

The album starts with George’s “Taxman”, a scorching rocker written in protest of the UK’s 95% top marginal tax rate that prevented the band from becoming as fabulously wealthy as you would expect the members of the most successful band in the history of the world to become. Though George wrote the song and plays lead guitar, Paul plays the track’s iconic guitar solo. Paul’s “Eleanor Rigby” expands on his earlier “Yesterday” with a full string octet, helping launch the genre of baroque pop alongside the Beach Boys’ Brian Wilson. Next is John’s “I’m Only Sleeping”, which uses tempo manipulation and a reversed, distorted, dual-tracked guitar solo from George to create a hazy atmosphere. “Love You To” starts George’s largely solo trilogy of Hindustani classical songs, followed by “Within You, Without You” on *Sgt. Pepper’s* in 1967 and the non-album single “The Inner Light” in 1968. Paul responded to the Beach Boys’ recently released “God Only Knows”, which he would later name as his favorite song, with the grand “Here, There and Everywhere”. The album’s obligatory Ringo track comes in the form of “Yellow Submarine”, a novelty track that John and Paul wrote collaboratively for Ringo to sing. Perhaps surprisingly, it has become one of the most enduringly popular Beatles songs thanks to its appeal with children and use as the basis of a 1968 animated film of the same name. Side one concludes with John’s “She Said She Said”, which takes inspiration from an acid trip John, George, and Ringo had with actor Peter Fonda and the Byrds.

Paul’s “Good Day Sunshine” opens side two with a sound that presaged his interest in theatrical music. Though “And Your Bird Can Sing” is a John track, it’s best known for the dueling guitar riffs played by George and Paul. Paul wrote “For No One” about his breakup with longtime girlfriend Jane Asher; Alan Civil, principal horn of the BBC Symphony Orchestra, plays the track’s French horn solo. Paul demanded he play a note too high for the range of the French horn, resulting in a solo Civil himself disliked but that has become his most celebrated performance. John’s “Doctor Robert” describes a doctor who prescribes his patients recreational drugs, reminiscent of the dentist who first introduced him and George to LSD. “I Want to Tell You” is George’s third and final contribution to this album, the most he would have on a Beatles record outside of *The White Album* (which is cheating because that’s a double LP). Paul’s last track “Got to Get You Into My Life” features a soul-style brass section inspired by Stevie Wonder. *Revolver* ends with John’s “Tomorrow Never Knows”, the most experimental, progressive, and overtly psychedelic Beatles song up to that point. In addition to John’s compositional vision, Paul’s tape loops, George’s Indian instrumentation, and Ringo’s astonishingly robotic, proto-motorik drumming combined to make this one of the Beatles’ most acclaimed tracks to this day. The Beatles also recorded non-album single “Paperback Writer” during the *Revolver* sessions, a track which the band chose to record a promotional video alongside in lieu of a live television performance; it was the first music video in the modern sense.

Despite the radical departure in sound from previous albums, *Revolver* was an instant hit in the UK. In the US, however, the album released amid a flurry of controversy resulting from a misinterpretation of John claiming the Beatles were “more popular than Jesus”. It still sold well in the US, but it created an unpleasant environment during the Beatles’ North American tour immediately upon album release. On August 29, 1966, the Beatles finished this tour with a concert at Candlestick Park in San Francisco; the band would not perform live in concert again until the legendary rooftop show in London on January 30, 1969. Contemporary critics raved over the album’s experimentalism and songwriting, helping inspire a serious artistic critical ecosystem for pop and rock music. Retrospectively, *Revolver* is one of the most common picks for the best Beatles album. *Sgt. Pepper’s* was the most acclaimed Beatles record upon release in 1967 and it retained that status through the 70s and 80s.*Revolver* dethroned it for the top spot sometime in the 90s thanks to a US re-issue in 1987 that was the first time the UK track list had been released stateside as well as the influence *Revolver*’s sound had on the Britpop that dominated the UK in the 90s. Sometime in the 2010s, *Abbey Road* came out of nowhere on the internet to take the consensus best Beatles album spot, probably due to the increased popularity of George. Anyway, all three are decent, but *The White Album* is better. Cooper knows.

# Hargrove's review

I, of course, love this album. Among all the other bangers, “For No One” is my favorite song by my favorite Beatle (love you Paul). It’s solidly at least A Tier for me. Unfortunately, I also think it’s the most overrated Beatles album. It’s my second least favorite of theirs from *Rubber Soul* onward (counting *Magical Mystery Tour* but not counting the *Yellow Submarine* soundtrack), only ahead of *Let It Be*. There are just too many damn misses, too many songs that don’t work or are completely forgettable for a Beatles album. And yes, I acknowledge the irony in saying that when I’m a fanboy of *The White Album*. But when “Wild Honey Pie” or “Revolution 9” or “Don’t Pass Me By” or “Why Don’t We Do It in the Road” comes on, you can’t help but smile. They’re bad in an interesting, fun way. They bring that album to life in a way that keeps you entertained straight through for an hour and a half. By contrast, “Doctor Robert” and “Good Day Sunshine” halt all momentum on an album that’s already barely more than half an hour long. No Beatles album is perfect, but come on. *Rubber Soul* gets “Run For Your Life”. *Sgt. Pepper’s* gets “Good Morning, Good Morning”. *Abbey Road* gets uhhhhhhhhhhhhhhhh…okay *Abbey Road* might actually be perfect (*The White Album* still better because it’s pluperfect). But *Revolver* gets “Yellow Submarine” and “Good Day Sunshine” and “Doctor Robert”, plus “Here, There and Everywhere” and “I Want to Tell You” and “Got to Get You into My Life” are all kind of mid too. Of those six songs I listed, you might notice that Paul is responsible for 3.5 of them. I think that specifically is kind of what ends up bugging me about *Revolver*: it’s my favorite Beatle’s most inconsistent album. “Eleanor Rigby” and especially “For No One” are two of his greatest accomplishments, but everything else here just doesn’t resonate with me. By contrast, John has a remarkably consistent outing with the exception of “Doctor Robert”. And George contributes two great tracks and one that’s decent. It just kind of sucks that my boy is the limiting factor. Of course, I’m focusing on the negatives because you all already know the positives. There are eight absolute Goddamn bangers on this thing. Not just any bangers, Beatles bangers. Some of the greatest songs ever. But when you’re reviewing a Beatles record, you have to keep in mind your relative opinions on each album. And this one has never lived up to its stature for me.

The album starts out with pure heat thanks to George’s first truly great song, “Taxman”. It’s a straight rocker on an album that otherwise shies away from straight rockers, and a fiery one at that. Paul absolutely shreds on that solo. I also find the lyrics clever and insightful, particularly “Now my advice for those who die/Declare the pennies on your eyes”. Paul follows that with “Eleanor Rigby”, which remains the high watermark of classical instrumentation in pop music. God bless George Martin for those strings. The lyrics have become somewhat of a cliché but, when you give them a fresh listen, they’re actually quite moving. “Eleanor Rigby/Died in the church and was buried along with her name/Nobody came/Father McKenzie/Wiping the dirt from his hands as he walks from the grave/No one was saved“ is sad! John’s first contribution “I’m Only Sleeping” rounds out an elite opening run to the album. It’s a masterclass in using atmosphere to capture the sentiment of lyrics, so hazy and lethargic. And I have to give some credit to the reversed solo. I’m a fan of all of George’s Indian experiments, and “Love You To” might have the strongest melody of any of them. I prefer “Within You Without You” on the whole, but this one sounds great too. While I’ve been enjoying “Here, There and Everywhere” more than usual this week, I just find it too sappy and syrupy and sentimental, particularly the backing vocals. On top of that, the melody isn’t strong enough to justify the weight of the arrangement. The only thing that really works for me is the chord progression. I find it odd how people often point to “She’s Leaving Home” as Paul going too far with the orchestral melodrama, but I love that song. Instead, “Here, There and Everywhere” fills that role for me. “Yellow Submarine” is another one that I’ve found less annoying than usual this week, but it’s still pretty annoying. The chorus is just incessant. It sticks out like a sore thumb in the middle of this record. “She Said She Said” rights the ship just in time to finish side 1. John’s steady, solid, consistent presence gives this album a backbone. This track also has some of Ringo’s flashiest drumming.

Sadly, side 2 opens with “Good Day Sunshine”, which might be my least favorite Paul song ever. You can hear hints of his later music hall stuff that I really do enjoy (“Your Mother Should Know” might be the most underrated Beatles track) but this just annoys me. There’s little substance to it. The bounce of the piano feels so plastic and contrived. John saves the day with yet another banger in “And Your Bird Can Sing”. There’s not much to it, just some interlocking guitars, but they absolutely fuck. I love it. Paul redeems himself with the absolute heartbreaker that is “For No One”. These have to be the best lyrics he ever wrote. Each and every verse is a dagger through the heart in a different way. “Your day breaks, your mind aches/You find that all her words/Of kindness linger on/When she no longer needs you”? You stay home, she goes out/She says that long ago/She knew someone, but now he's gone/She doesn't need him”? And in her eyes, you see nothing/No sign of love behind the tears/Cried for no one/A love that should have lasted years”? Holy FUCK. On top of that, the french horn sounds morose yet regal. It’s my favorite track on the album. “Doctor Robert”, which follows, is my least favorite. I don’t know what it is that bugs me so much; on the surface, it’s just kind of an inoffensive John blues rock track. For some reason, though, it irritates me to no end. Like, how many times can you say “Doctor Robert”? “I Want to Tell You” is the worst of George’s three contributions but, you know what, it’s still pretty decent. It’s better than multiple Paul tracks and a John track. I particularly enjoy the little dissonant piano bit. “Got to Get You Into My Life” is a solid Paul track. The horns go a little over the top, though, and the melody is just okay. As much as I want to be contrarian (and I kind of am because I will reiterate that “For No One” edges it out as my favorite on the album), “Tomorrow Never Knows” is just brilliant. It deserves all the praise it gets and more. John sounds like the Wizard of Oz behind the curtain. Paul’s tape loops are trippy as fuck. George provides the perfect little accoutrements and flourishes. For my money, though, the real MVP is Ringo. The drumming could straight up be on *Ege Bamyası*. It’s hypnotic.

Of the three Beatles albums on the list, *Abbey Road* > *Sgt. Pepper’s* > *Revolver*. All three are brilliant though. One thing I’ll say for *Revolver*: “For No One” should just be on every album. Not even just Beatles albums. *Relationship of Command* would be better if “For No One” was on it. *American Football* would be better if “For No One” was on it. *good kid, m.A.A.d. city* would be better if “For No One” was on it. Those are just facts.

Favorite tracks: Taxman, Eleanor Rigby, I’m Only Sleeping, Love You To, She Said She Said, And Your Bird Can Sing, For No One, Tomorrow Never Knows

Least favorite track: Doctor Robert

# Aidan's review

It's the Beatles, it's Revolver, it's a good album. Off the top of my head, it might be my least favorite Paul album, but that's a bit like picking the worst member of the '27 Yankees or the ’92 Dream Team. "Good Day Sunshine" and "Yellow Submarine" are a tad annoying (and more than a tad overplayed), and it's a crying shame that "Paperback Writer" was only released as a single. That's about the sum of my complaints with the album. I'm heavily partial to the earlier pop songwriting of the Beatles cumulating in Rubber Soul, but it's unmistakable how well they combine their songwriting chops with their many musical innovations, as they began to push the boundaries of the recording studio. "Love You To" is probably the best George song from the quartet and “For No One" is up there with the (many) great Paul songs. There's so many perfect instrumental moments. The guitar on "And Your Bird Can Sing", the horns on "Got To Get You Into My Life", the strings on "Eleanor Rigby". It's a minor miracle how well every single experimentation lands that you just take it for granted that George's guitar is backwards on "I'm Only Sleeping" or that this was the first album to close-mic drums. I have no problem with anyone preferring another Beatles album (it's no Rubber Soul), but if you think this album is not good you're insane.
