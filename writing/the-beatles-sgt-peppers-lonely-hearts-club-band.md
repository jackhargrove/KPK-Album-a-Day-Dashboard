# Write-up

The Beatles were a British pop/rock outfit formed in Liverpool in 1960 consisting of rhythm guitarist John Lennon, bassist Paul McCartney, lead George Harrison, and drummer Ringo Starr (real name Richard Starkey). Lead vocals on any given song were typically delivered by whoever wrote the song, with the exception that Ringo was generally given one song to sing per album despite the fact that he wasn’t much of a songwriter. Beatles history crash course: 16 year-old John forms skiffle (British version of rock n roll) group called The Quarrymen in 1956. 15 year-old Paul joins in 1957. 15 year-old George joins in 1958 as Paul’s friend despite John’s objections. Everyone else leaves the group, and our 3 boys look for new members. John’s friend Stu Sutcliffe joins in 1960 and suggests the name “Beatals” as a tribute to Buddy Holly’s band The Crickets. After a few minor changes, they land on The Beatles in 1960. Drummer Pete Best joins the band in 1960 and they leave Liverpool for a residency at a club in Hamburg, Germany, where they all get addicted to meth so they can play 6 hours a day, 7 days a week. They stay in Hamburg on-and-off for almost 2 years. In late 1961, the Beatles return to Liverpool, but Stu stays in Hamburg for a girl; he’ll be dead within 6 months. Now expert live performers, the 4 Beatles become regulars at the Cavern Club in Liverpool. There, music manager Brian Epstein discovers them and becomes their manager. Epstein gets them auditions with various labels; after getting rejected by Decca (shame they will never live down), they catch the eye of producer George Martin at EMI Parlophone. The Beatles sign with EMI and record for the first time in June 1962. Martin thinks Pete Best is a terrible drummer and convinces The Beatles to fire him. They recruit well-known local Liverpool drummer Ringo Starr as his replacement, and the final lineup is set.

The Beatles release their first UK album (at the time, different countries would get different albums), *Please Please Me*, in early 1963. The style was Merseybeat, a melodic form of guitar pop played in Liverpool in the early 60s. John and Paul, the group’s primary songwriters, begin the extremely fruitful Lennon-McCartney partnership: every song that either of them writes, even if done completely alone (as was usually the case), would be credited to both as Lennon-McCartney. Bolstered by lead single “Love Me Do”, *Please Please Me* reached #1 on the UK charts. Their non-album single “She Loves You” became the fastest selling single of all time in the UK. They became a sensation in Britain that was dubbed “Beatlemania” by the end of 1963, intensified by another smash hit non-album single “I Want to hold Your Hand”. Before the year was out, they released their second UK album, *With The Beatles*, which contained the first song ever written by George. In early 1964, Beatlemania finally made it to America: their first North American album (these are now considered non-canon, as it were; the UK releases are considered the real albums) released in January, and in February they flew to NYC to play the popular TV program *The Ed Sullivan Show*. They were now as massive in the US as they were in the UK. In July 1964, they released their third album, *A Hard Day’s Night*, which accompanied a critically acclaimed film of the same name. It was their first album consisting entirely of original songs. In August, they met Bob Dylan, who introduced them to three things: the American folk movement, what it was like to be critically acclaimed, and, most importantly, marijuana. They quickly put out their fourth (and worst) album *Beatles for Sale* at the end of 1964, but they became determined to make music that was critically acclaimed, not just commercially successful. Around the same time, in early 1965, George’s dentist secretly dosed him and John with LSD; the pair soon after became obsessed with acid and introduced Ringo to the stuff as well, but Paul refused to take it. They released their 5th album, *Help!*, in summer 1965, which was a good album but a little too obvious in its Dylan imitation. The Beatles were finally successful in being taken serious critically with their 6th album *Rubber Soul*, released in December 1965. *Rubber Soul* was the first great Beatles album from front-to-back, and one of the first albums to be more than just a random group of songs put together for commercial release. It marked the end of their boy band period, and the beginning of their shift into weirder, more interesting stuff. It also lit a fire under Brian Wilson’s ass, inspiring him to write *Pet Sounds* for The Beach Boys as a response.

As the most famous band in the world, and with their profile growing as a “serious” group, the Beatles began to face controversies and backlash in the mid 60s. The most famous of these was when John claimed they were “bigger than Jesus”, which got them heavily protested in the US. The Beatles also grew very tired of touring; they had been playing concerts nonstop since 1962, and their popularity was such that the crowds at their concerts were so loud that no one could hear the music. In summer of 1966 they released *Revolver*, a common pick for the best Beatles album, containing classics like Paul’s “Eleanor Rigby” and their most experimental song to date, John’s “Tomorrow Never Knows”. The tour they did after *Revolver* would prove to be their last; after playing the final date in San Francisco, the Beatles retired from live performance, becoming a studio band full time. Their newfound free time allowed them to spend more time writing, but it also imposed high pressure on them to release more music. Their first sessions for a new album yielded the songs “Strawberry Fields Forever” (John), “Penny Lane”, and “When I’m 64” (both Paul). The studio forced them to release the former two as singles, meaning they couldn’t be on the album, but they were extremely critically acclaimed and created much anticipation for the forthcoming album. Additionally, Paul finally decided to try to LSD with the rest of the band, and he became the first to talk about his drug use with the press not long after. And finally, *Pet Sounds*, which itself had been designed to compete with *Rubber Soul*, became the new best album to beat in the competition between The Beatles and the Beach Boys. *Sgt. Pepper’s Lonely Hearts Club Band* came out in May 1967 to unprecedented critical and commercial success. They became hippie icons, and helped to kick off the Summer of Love in 1967. They were quickly disillusioned with the hippie movement, however, and sought some deeper meaning in life. In August, they met the Maharishi Mahesh Yogi, an Indian spiritual leader (huge grifter) and became enamored with India. A few days later, their manager Brian Epstein died of a possibly intentional drug overdose. Brian Epstein had kept the Beatles together with an iron fist for 5 years, and they were completely rudderless without him. For the first time, the Beatles had no direction, no idea what to do next.

Paul had dominated the sessions for *Sgt. Pepper’s* and saw himself as the natural leader in Epstein’s absence. The first thing they did without Epstein was the psychedelic TV movie *Magical Mystery Tour*, a complete and total mess that was ridiculed and was the first real misstep the Beatles ever took. That being said, the resulting LP contained new great songs like John’s “I Am the Walrus” and Paul’s “The Fool on the Hill”. Hoping to shake off the failure and find themselves, all four Beatles went on a now legendary trip to India in early 1968. They went to an ashram led by the Maharishi Mahesh Yogi with a group of other celebrities including members of The Beach Boys and actress Mia Farrow. They primarily went at the behest of George, who was somewhat of a weeaboo for India. John was the second most enthusiastic, as he had just begun cheating on his wife with Japanese artist Yoko Ono and genuinely sought spiritual clarity. Paul went because he thought the whole India thing would be good for his image. Ringo went because everyone else was going, but he went home after less than 2 weeks because he was a very picky eater. Paul left a month later when he got bored. John began to grow disillusioned as he didn’t find the clarity that he sought, and he became convinced that the Maharishi was a fraud. John convinced George, who was a true believer, that the Maharishi was trying to sleep with women on the trip, and they left after 2 months. The Beatles reconvened in London in May of 1968 to begin work on their ninth studio album. These sessions were extremely tense: Paul was becoming more and more controlling and his relationship with John was strained, John began a heroin addiction and started bringing Yoko Ono to rehearsals, George was becoming a prolific songwriter but neither John nor Paul would take him seriously, and Ringo hated how negative the atmosphere was that he quit the band for 2 weeks. They had also just started their own business venture, Apple Corps, which was a ticking time bomb disaster. The sessions yielded the single “Hey Jude” (Paul) and their first ever double album, *The Beatles* (typically referred to as “The White Album”). This record was extremely scattershot, as it became abundantly clear that there was almost no collaboration between the members on the songwriting. There were also some highly experimental pieces, severe genre shifts, and the first ever song written by Ringo (“Don’t Pass Me By”)! It was released in late 1968 as the first release on Apple Records.

With The Beatles on the verge of collapse, Paul took it upon himself to orchestrate a brand new project in early 1969 with the hopes of bringing everyone back together. So began the *Get Back* sessions, named after the first song they (Paul) wrote as part of the project. The idea was to make a back-to-basics blues rock record and film the entire recording process. Unfortunately, many of the problems from the White Album sessions persisted: John hated being managed by Paul and they were barely on speaking terms, John missed half of the sessions because he was high on smack with Yoko, and George began to hate Paul because he kept shooting down his songs, causing George to quit the band for a weekend. A fun added element to the drama was that John, George, and Ringo, in an effort to subvert Paul’s new authority, voted to make Allen Klein their new manager. Klein was a sleazy crook who had previously managed The Rolling Stones, and those three bozos put him in charge of all of their money just to spite Paul. Probably the biggest mistake made at any point in The Beatles career, and the resultant money problems stretched tensions to their breaking point. Nevertheless, the *Get Back* sessions were fruitful, and The Beatles played their final ever live performance on January 30, 1969: a surprise performance of their new songs on the roof of their building in central London at lunch time. People on their lunch break from work were treated to the first Beatles concert in 3 years, and this performance has become legendary. Afterward, The Beatles gave their recordings from the *Get Back* sessions to engineer Glyn Johns to fashion into an album, and then they kinda forgot about it for a while.

By summer of 1969, Paul hated John, John hated Paul, George hated Paul, Paul was sad that George hated him, John was ambivalent toward George, George was desperate for John’s approval, and Ringo felt like the child of a divorce (even though he was the oldest). The end was near. Thankfully, they had the presence of mind to realize that they were the fucking Beatles, and they decided to make one last album before calling it quits for good. They began recording at EMI Recording Studio on Abbey Road in London. Producer George Martin wanted the album to be one continuous composition, but John refused because he didn’t want to have to collaborate with Paul at all; they compromised, and side 1 was made up of solo contributions from all four Beatles (including Ringo!), and side 2 being a medley. The last time all four were in a studio together was August 20, 1969. Exactly a month later, John told the rest that he was leaving The Beatles, but he agreed to keep that private as they were still releasing their remaining records. *Abbey Road*, the final album recorded by The Beatles, was released in September 1969. And that was that…except, can’t forget those *Get Back* sessions, which still hadn’t been turned into an album. They somehow found their way into the hands of famous producer Phil Spector, who added his Wall of Sound technique to the songs, including entire orchestras for some tracks. This pissed off Paul, who thought Spector had ruined the album. Klein ignored Paul’s objections and Paul was so pissed off that he stole John’s thunder and publicly announced that he was leaving The Beatles in April 1970. One month later, they released *Let It Be*, the final Beatles album, although not the last one recorded. After 10 very productive years, the Beatles were officially finished. George was 26, Paul was 27, John was 28, and Ringo was 29.

Where are they now? After the breakup, Paul, John, and George raced each other to set up solo careers (Ringo also went solo, but he was less concerned with the competition aspect). John had a head start, having already released material with his now-wife Yoko Ono. He had come to fashion himself a left-wing political icon, releasing preachy songs about world peace and prejudice. His most notable solo album was 1971’s *Imagine*, featuring the eponymous hit song. Paul began to record with his wife Linda McCartney, releasing *Ram* (my personal favorite Beatles solo album) together in 1971. It was recorded on Paul’s farm and contained a lo-fi but melodic sound that would become very influential on the development of indie pop, with some going so far as calling it the first indie pop album (which is, to be fair, ridiculous). Paul and Linda would recruit a few more musicians and formed the band Wings, which became quite successful. George was sitting on a tremendous backlog of songs he had written while with The Beatles but that John and Paul had rejected. He turned these songs into a triple (!) album called *All Things Must Pass* in 1970, which is probably the consensus best Beatles solo album. Ringo played drums for some of John’s solo work, and also released his only two solo hits in the early 70s: “Photograph” in 1973 and “You’re Sixteen” (ew) in 1974. There were still major tensions between the members even after the breakup. The opening song on Paul’s *Ram* included some veiled insults toward John; in response, he wrote the song “How Do You Sleep?” that was a far more direct attack on Paul (more or less a diss track), and that included George on slide guitar and Ringo on drums. Brutal. They never forgave him for trying to save The Beatles. All four Beatles continued their solo careers through the rest of their lives but, as time went on, their new music became less and less relevant. John was the first to die when he was murdered by a crazed fan outside of his UWS apartment in New York in December 1980. George was the next to go, dying from lung cancer in 2001. Paul and Ringo are both still kicking, but both are by now well into their 80s. In the not-too-distant future, there won’t be any Beatles left, and that’s sad. But what a legacy they’ve left behind.

The Beatles were a British pop/rock outfit formed in Liverpool in 1960 consisting of rhythm guitarist John Lennon, bassist Paul McCartney, lead George Harrison, and drummer Ringo Starr (real name Richard Starkey). Lead vocals on any given song were typically delivered by whoever wrote the song, with the exception that Ringo was generally given one song to sing per album despite the fact that he wasn’t much of a songwriter. Beatles history crash course: 4 working class boys from Liverpool, became the biggest band in the world in 1964 playing guitar pop, started making more serious music and became critical darlings with their sixth album *Rubber Soul* in 1965, got really into weed and acid, released pop masterpiece *Revolver* in 1966 (on the list), stopped touring, released psychedelic masterpiece *Sgt. Pepper’s Lonely Hearts Club Band* in 1967 (today’s album), released one of the most bizarre albums of all time with 1968’s *The Beatles* aka The White Album (my personal favorite but not on the list), released their swan song and pop rock masterpiece *Abbey Road* in 1969 (on the list and the current consensus best Beatles album), broke up in 1970, pursued successful solo careers, two of them are now dead. Consult my longer piece above if you’d like more info. It is long, but I am a very dedicated Beatles historian, and my spin on events will be more entertaining (and accurate) than anything else you read. Please do consult me if you have questions about why they broke up, or if you’d like more detail on their trip to India, as those are the two subjects on which I consider myself one of the world’s foremost experts. But really, what else is there to say about The Beatles? I think RYM’s band page puts it better than I ever could: “*They're the Beatles man. Nothing new can be said anymore. If you go by popular opinion they will alway be the one group regarded as the greatest of all time. There'll be people who disagree with that result, but it's still an undeniably powerful statistic. In all likelihood, there will never be another band as popular and critically acclaimed. They win at music.*”

*Sgt. Pepper’s Lonely Hearts Club Band* was the first Beatles record they recorded after they stopped touring, and the first where they had complete and total creative freedom. By late 1966, the Beatles were absolutely exhausted. They had performed over 1,400 concerts since 1962, and many of those later concerts were miserable, grueling experiences where they couldn’t even hear themselves play over the roar of the crowd and they had to be protected by security at all times as girls chased them. They were also facing mounting pressure to innovate and mature, especially after the acclaim their rivals The Beach Boys received for their 1966 record *Pet Sounds*. In response to these pressures, Paul came up with an idea: what if they pretend they’re not The Beatles, and instead they’re an Edwardian era (1901-1910) military band called Sgt. Pepper’s Lonely Hearts Club Band? That way, they could dissociate somewhat, and feel free to experiment. The idea was pretty half-baked from the beginning, and they pretty much abandon the concept after track 2 of the album. It allowed Paul to dominate in the studio, however, especially because John was depressed and often absent. Of the 13 songs on the record, 7 were primarily written by Paul (“Sgt. Pepper’s Lonely Hearts Club Band”, “Getting Better”, “Fixing a Hole”, “She’s Leaving Home”, “When I’m Sixty-Four”, “Lovely Rita”, “Sgt. Pepper’s Lonely Hearts Club Band (Reprise)”) and 2 more were 50/50 collaborations with John (“With a Little Help from My Friends” was written by both for Ringo to sing, “A Day in the Life” was made by splicing together a John song and a Paul song). John contributed 3 songs on his own (“Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds”, “Being for the Benefit of Mr. Kite”, “Good Morning, Good Morning”) and George contributed only 1 (“Within You Without You), his fewest since 1964’s *Beatles for Sale*. “Within You Without You” was the second of three Indian classical songs George would write, after *Revolver*’s “Love You To” and before the single “The Inner Light”. Ringo was reportedly very bored by the Paul-dominated sessions, claiming the most memorable part was that he learned how to play chess. The album’s cover is extremely famous as well; it features the four Beatles all dressed up in front of photographs of 57 celebrities. John, Paul, and George all selected which celebrities they wanted represented; two of John’s suggestions (Adolf Hitler and Jesus Christ) were rejected by the label.

For a long time, *Sgt. Pepper*’s was considered the quintessential Beatles album. It was a smash hit upon release, and was their last record made before the death of their manager Brian Epstein sent the band out of control. It contains the track “A Day in the Life”, which is still to this day considered the best song the Beatles ever wrote, and is currently the number 1 song of all time on RYM. It was also one of the first ever concept albums, ushering in the album era where the album was the fundamental artistic unit of popular music. It was one of the first pieces of popular music ever to be evaluated critically as art, similar to classical music and jazz before it. It was a touchstone in the hippie music, with the Summer of Love in San Francisco starting immediately after its release. Starting with the rise of punk in the late 70s, critics began to turn on *Sgt. Pepper’s* somewhat, finding its artsy grandiosity at odds with the primal emotional power of rock. It became a popular contrarian opinion to shit on *Sgt. Pepper’s*. Still, it was largely thought of as the most essential Beatles album in the public consciousness until the 2010s, when *Abbey Road* seems to have taken the mantle.

# Hargrove's review

So hard reviewing Beatles albums because I don’t really know what I can say that hasn’t been said before. I’m a white knight for *Sgt. Pepper’s*; I think there was a point at which it was overrated, but the pendulum swung too far and now it’s underrated. I struggle with choosing between *Abbey Road* and *Sgt. Pepper’s* as my second favorite Beatles record behind The White Album (I find *Revolver* a tad bit overrated just because of a few Paul songs I don’t care for). Paul is my favorite Beatle, and he absolutely shines on *Sgt. Pepper’s*. He doesn’t have a bad track on here. Some people complain about his music hall (or as John called it, “granny music”) obsession that started around this time, but I think “When I’m Sixty-Four” and “Lovely Rita” are both killer tracks. Sugary sweet, beautiful melodies, lyrics that stick on your tongue and in your brain, a perfect touch of psychedelia to bridge the gap between the old and the new; Paul would continue to use music hall effectively with “Your Mother Should Know”, “Martha My Dear”, and “I Know” among others, but I don’t think he ever really did it better than he did it here. “She’s Leaving Home” is another song I hear get shit occasionally, with some calling it dreary or overdone. I couldn’t disagree more. It’s one of my favorite Beatles songs. The strings aren’t dreary or claustrophobic, they’re melodramatic, and I love it. I feel like I’m in a domestic drama every time. It’s awesome, aching, beautiful. “Getting Better” has one of the most ambiguous melodies I’ve ever heard, and it still sounds so cool every time I listen. I also think John’s “It can’t get no worse” in the chorus is so brilliant, his cynicism contrasts with Paul’s optimism so perfectly. “Fixing a Hole” I feel like often gets forgotten, but it really shouldn’t. The harpsichord adds a jarring new texture, and I love hoe unresolved the chorus feels. The lyrics have also always resonated with me for whatever reason. The title track and its reprise are a bit less substantial, but I love it when The Beatles have a little fun with things, and I love the character that they add to the record. And beyond just his songwriting, Paul’s bass goes INSANE on this album, oh my GOD. Just listen to it on “With A Little Help From My Friends”, “Lucy In the Sky With Diamonds”, “Getting Better”, “Lovely Rita”, “A Day in the Life”, it’s so high in the mix its almost a primary melody. Bro really went sicko mode all over this album.

I’ve always been a little bit less impressed with John’s contributions. “Good Morning Good Morning” is often cited as the worst track on this album, and I have to agree. The melody is just too off-balance, the horns don’t blend all that well, it never really finds a groove, and the animal noises are a bit annoying. It’s not a bad song by any means, but it just doesn’t work like it should. That’s not a hot take. What’s a bit more of a hot take is that I’ve never super loved “Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds”. There’s just something I’m missing. I’ve always found John’s voice a bit irritating, the lyrics not as evocative as they should be, and the melody a little weak. Again, still a good song, and better than “Good Morning Good Morning”, but I’ve never understood its stature. I do like “Being for the Benefit of Mr. Kite” quite a bit though. It always runs the risk of being annoying in the way that “Good Morning Good Morning” is, but it somehow supports its wacky premise really well. The atmosphere goes crazy, it’s all over the place, and it just doesn’t sound like anything else. “A Day in the Life” is technically a John/Paul collab, but I give more credit to John because his part forms the skeleton of the song. It’s obviously phenomenal. I would never in a million years call it the greatest song ever written, not even the best Beatles song, but I get it. The use of the orchestra is unlike any other chamber pop I’ve ever heard, just a giant, rumbling, dissonant wave. It’s super, super cool. And the chord at the end is massive. John’s lyrics are really well done, and Paul’s interlude feels like a brief snap back to reality. I’d never insult this song. Ringo does a great job singing “With a Little Help from My Friends”, one of my favorite vocal performances of his. It’s a nice song, written well, performed well, I like it a lot. George’s sole contribution “Within You Without You” I go back and forth on. Some days I love it, some days I can’t stand it. Today has been a day that I’ve loved it. When you’re in the mood, it can be hypnotic, completely sucking you in. I respect the risk it took putting this on the album.

Just a banger album, man. What more needs to be said?

Favorite tracks: Sgt. Pepper’s Lonely Hearts Club Band, With a Little Help from My Friends, Getting Better, Fixing a Hole, She’s Leaving Home, Being for the Benefit of Mr. Kite, Within You Without You, When I’m Sixty-Four, Lovely Rita, A Day in the Life

Least favorite track: Good Morning Good Morning
