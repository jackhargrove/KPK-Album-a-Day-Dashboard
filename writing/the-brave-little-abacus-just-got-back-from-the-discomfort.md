# Write-up

The Brave Little Abacus was an emo revival (also known as fourth wave emo) band formed in 2007 in Sandown, New Hampshire consisting of guitarist, vocalist, and guitarist Adam Demirjian, bassist Andrew Ryan, and keyboardist Zach Kelly-Onett. I’ve described the waves of emo before on previous emo albums, but as a quick refresher: after the first wave of punk in the late 70s, the American punk scene took the speed and aggression to the extreme with hardcore punk in the early 80s. The most important early hardcore scenes were in LA (Black Flag), San Francisco (Dead Kennedys), and DC (Minor Threat). DC in particular developed a very strong hardcore scene, but by 1985 it was experiencing serious problems and decline. Ian MacKaye of Minor Threat decided to rejuvenate the DC hardcore scene by declaring 1985 “Revolution Summer” and a new wave of hardcore bands sprung up. Among these was the band Rites of Spring, whose live performance style was intensely emotional and often featured the lead singer crying on stage. Rites of Spring and the bands that followed were originally referred to as “emotional hardcore”, which was then shortened to “emocore”. These emocore bands were still firmly within the hardcore punk umbrella but made up what we now call the first wave of emo. As post-hardcore developed in the late 80s, bands like Slint proved very influential on emo sound going forward. In the  mid-90s, bands like Cap’n Jazz and American Football formed in the midwest. They took the feelings of emocore but applied them to the indie rock sound popular at the time. This style became very important in the underground, and is now referred to as Midwest emo or second wave emo. As these bands got more influential, some bands in the late 90s introduced a pop sound into emo, and a few of those bands like Jimmy Eat World, Paramore, and My Chemical Romance became some of the biggest bands in the world in the early 2000s. This was the third wave of emo, and the first time that emo was genuinely successful in the mainstream. Third wave emo wore out its welcome by the mid-2000s, and emo as a style became very unfashionable. In the late 2000s and early 2010s, some small indie bands began trying to replicate the style of Midwest (second wave) emo, and it became an important movement in the early 2010s indie rock scene. This movement was fourth wave emo, or emo revival, and The Brave Little Abacus were one of the earliest bands of that scene.

The Brave Little Abacus released its first demo in 2008 and a full album in 2009, both self-released. Their second album, *Just Got Back From the Discomfort—We’re Alright* also proved to be their last. It was self-released to little fanfare outside of the emo revival scene, and the band broke up after playing their final show in Plaistow, New Hampshire in 2012. Midwest emo has always been known for its high-pitched and whiny vocals, and The Brave Little Abacus took that to an extreme. The album was recorded at Demirjian’s home rather than a studio. It contains three samples of dialogue from *Malcolm in the Middle*, one of my all-time favorite TV shows. The album got very little love at the time, but after their breakup it got really big on 4chan’s /mu/ board (where our list is from!) and in retrospect is considered one of the finest records of the fourth wave emo scene. Demirjian now plays in a Boston-area power pop band, and Kelly-Onett has become a classical composer.

# Hargrove's review

A Midwest emo album with unorthodox vocals that’s part of a small 2010s music revivalist scenes and uses multiple samples from *Malcolm in the Middle*, perhaps the most important TV show of my childhood? This album conceptually seems like it was made in a lab to appeal to me specifically. How did it measure up? There was a lot to like about this album, but unfortunately it has some serious flaws that prevent me from liking it as much as it feels like I should. I’ll start with the good: the vocals are passionate and dripping with emotion, the sonic palette is truly diverse (normal rock instrumentation but also horns, accordion, video game, more instruments that I can’t name), a few great climaxes that are pure release, and the samples are both well-picked and well-placed. On to the bad: I really couldn’t get into the lyrics at all outside of a couple tracks (more on that later), the constant shifts in tempo and style make it really hard to settle into a nice listen, missed opportunities in tension build and release, and, as much as I hate to say it, Demirjian’s voice is *really* hard to get into.

The lyrics specifically were a major roadblock for me. Midwest emo has a reputation for “bad” lyrics, but those lyrics aren’t actually bad; sure, they’re angsty, sophomoric, and melodramatic, but that’s exactly what this style of music calls for. I listen to emo as an outlet for my stupidest, most embarrassing emotions, and the best bands in the genre tailor everything in the music for the moment when you can scream out exactly what you’re feeling. On this album, the lyrics are indeed deeply personal and emotional, but it feels like Demirjian got too in his head about intellectualizing these feelings (with symbolism and other literary dreck) without giving the proper context to make that kind of thing work. I contrast this with *American Football*, where the lyrics are all direct excerpts from Mike Kinsella’s diary. I don’t have the context to know the particulars of his relationship or breakup, but the feelings are so straightforward that I can belt out “So let’s just pretend/Everything and anything/Between you and me/Was never meant” and feel what he was feeling. On the other hand, you have Brand New, where Jesse Lacey intellectualizes his feelings with proper, fleshed-out allegories. I think of “Limousine”, a story of a drunk driving accident not personal to Lacey at all, but that translates his own feelings of shame and guilt as effectively as any personal statement would. With The Brave Little Abacus, his attempts at artistic grandeur while protecting the context for himself leaves the lyrics in an unsatisfying middle ground. The prime example is “Please Don’t Cry, They Stopped Hours Ago”, where Demirjian gets lost in some kind of nonsensical metaphor about drowning in a glass of water. There are two songs that I really liked lyrically (“Can’t Run Away” and “It’s Not What You Think It Is”), but most of the songs left me without any kind of emotional catharsis. The music teed things up very well, but he just failed to deliver on the conveyance of emotion.

With that fundamental complaint out of the way, here are my thoughts on the tracks. “Pile! No Pile! Pile!” opens things off very well, punching you in the face immediately with metal-style drums and an opening scream. In addition to being a good song, the piano stands out to me, and the use of the *Malcolm in the Middle* (MitM from here on out) sample where Hal muses on the meaningless of his relationship with his dad after his funeral is perfectly repurposed for the breakup story. I do think it’s telling that the best lyrical uses on this album are samples rather than Demirjian’s own writing, but I digress. “Please Don’t Cry, They Stopped Hours Ago” does some cool stuff with video game noises, a great driving pace for most of it, and an awesome explosion at the end. I didn’t love the slow section though, and the lyrics push this over the edge of not being a favorite track for me. “Boy’s Theme” is a pretty insignificant interlude, although the insight into his life with the voicemail is cool. “A Highway Got Paved Over My Future, I Drive It Getting To School” is one of my favorites. I love how hard it is to get a handle on the rhythm, the fast sections are immaculate, and it’s the first appearance of those very Neutral Milk Hotel or American Football horns. The video game sounds on “The Blah Blah Blahs” have a Baroque feeling that’s nice and the horns are back. It’s fast and loud, but the songwriting isn’t there to really highlight those qualities. “Can’t Run Away” is almost a pure folk tune, with these bells that sound so bright as to be stark. I think the calm vocals really shine in this one, as does the interplay between the calm vocals and the occasional scream coming in on a different channel. This is one where I actually really like the lyrics; “When I say I’m sad, I mean it/There is no excuse, I need this” is direct and impactful enough to hit as hard as any other emo lyric. I’ve also been feeling homesick, so the stuff about reasons to stay in his small town (particularly his dad being funny) really hit home.

“Untitled (Cont.)” starts a trilogy of tracks that run right into the other. The intro uses the horns very well and the drums are great, but it doesn’t build in the way I want it to. I feel similarly about “Aubade (Morning Love Song)”, but now there’s a great bass line and a hard-hitting outro to take us into the trilogy’s conclusion. “It’s Not What You Think It Is” is a favorite of mine, and another one where I like the lyrics. This frustration with your fellow fashionable indieheads, a contrarian impulse against contrarianism, is something I’ve felt for a long time. Fiona Apple explores this on her song “Periphery”. “Cynics mock all surroundings/I just wish to live with the ones that/Instead of hating MTV/Beat on the floorboards to wake their tenants” really sums up a frustration I have sometimes with people who are to cool for everything. I also like the simmering guitar, and the climax is a perfect release to end the three-track suite. “Allston, Massachusetts December 2009-January 2010” is pretty, but another insignificant interlude. “Bug-Infested Floorboards—Can We Please Just Leave This Place, Now” is a banger and one of the best tracks on here. The section beginning around 2:00 is pure emo bliss that’s as soaring as emo can ever be. That horn, man. It uses two MitM samples masterfully, both a classic Dewey line with “Is your brain big enough to get your feelings hurt? Me neither”, originally said in the show to his pet hamster but repurposed here to deal with pain. And near the end, a quote that sums up everything about why I love MitM and why I found Malcolm to be among the most relatable characters in all of fiction: “Cynthia: You hate everyone. to you, everyone’s either a moron, or a creep, or a poseur. Why do you suddenly care about their opinion of you?/Malcolm: Because I’m shallow, okay? I want them to like me.” Great track. The closer, “Orange, Blue With Stripes” is quiet but ends on a big build, seemingly before it hits its climax. It has a great line from the pilot episode of MitM, “At some point it stops.” In the show, the full line is “The best thing about childhood is, at some point it stops.” If there’s one thing that needs to be said to emo artists (and those of us who love the music), it’s that eventually we need to realize that childhood has stopped.

Clearly, this album made me feel all sorts of ways given how much I just wrote about it. I really do like how uncompromisingly personal it is; the unabashedly strange voice and the sampling from Demirjian’s favorite TV shows and video games really drives that home. But I just don’t think he’s all that great at putting that personality into his music. This is a record that’s still puzzling and infuriating me, and I hope to make further progress.

Favorite tracks: Pile! No Pile! Pile!, A Highway Got Paved Over My Future I Drive It Getting To School, Can’t Run Away, It’s Not What You Think It Is, Bug-Infested Floorboards—Can We Please Just Leave This Place Now

Least favorite track: Boy’s Theme
