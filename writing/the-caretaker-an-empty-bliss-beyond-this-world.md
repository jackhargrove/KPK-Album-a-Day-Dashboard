# Write-up

The Caretaker is the most famous music project by James Leyland Kirby, an English musician. He started releasing music under the named V/Vm and his own record in 1996, releasing experimental and electronic music, including one release that was just the sounds of pigs. The majority of his early output was noise albums (very different from noise rock, Metal Machine Music is a noise album) sampled from pop music. When plunderphonics became popular, it was a natural genre for him to expand into. At the same time he was plundering pop records, he also got into ballroom music and released Selected Memories From The Haunted Ballroom, an album loosely inspired by several horror movies, including The Shining (specifically the ballroom scene). This was his first release under the Caretaker name (also sourced from the Shining), and he followed it up with two more similar albums, A Stairway to the Stars and We'll All Go Riding on a Rainbow. For his next release, Theoretically Pure Anterograde Amnesia, he explored memory by creating an album designed to be impossible to remember.

His next album would begin to narrow in on the subject matter the Caretaker would become best known for, Alzheimer's disease. Persistent Repetition of Phrases took its track names from symptoms of Alzheimer's such as "Lacunar Amnesia" and "False Memory Syndrome". The album was composed of short samples of ballroom music looping over and over again with white noise being played over them. Today's record, An Empty Bliss Beyond This World developed that idea further. Inspired by the fact that music is often the last thing that Alzheimer's patients remember, the album consists of ballroom records that Kirby purchased in Brooklyn in 2010 before transferring them across several recording devices as he moved from the US to Germany to Spain. Finally, he turned the records into an album, combining them with increasingly prominent clicks, hisses and audio alternating channels. While the Caretaker's prior albums had been well received by critics, An Empty Bliss Beyond This World was a breakthrough, bringing wider critical and popular attention to his work, ranking among the best albums of the year for Pitchfork. His next album, Everywhere at the End of Time, was supposed to be his last, and took the exploration of dementia to an extreme, spanning six albums, six and a half hours and released over 3 years. It slowly progressed from upbeat and slightly distorted big band samples to near complete white noise that barely evoked human voices. You may have heard of it from when it became a TikTok challenge to listen to the entire thing during the pandemic.

While all of the music released by the Caretaker was created by Kirby from samples, he saw the project as a collaborative one, with collaborators finding the samples and giving him ideas to create music from. His most famous collaborator was British philosopher Mark Fisher who wrote his linear notes. Fisher was the main figure who defined "hauntology" as a music genre, with the Caretaker being among his primary examples. The genre featured nostalgia and techniques which reflect the degradation of mechanical and biological memory in order to create a sense of loss. This, in turn, served Fisher's conception of the modern world as itself haunted by the failure of capitalism to produce a future that could be aspired to. Among the artists we have listened to, Burial, Broadcast, Boards of Canada, and Oneohtrix Point Never are known as hauntological artists. Fisher's most famous work, Capitalist Realism is based on (former Killingworth CT resident) Fredric Jameson's quote "It is easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of capitalism". Fisher committed suicide in 2017 after a lifelong struggle with depression. Afterwards, the Caretaker released Take Care, It's a Desert Out There, a single 48 minute song in order to honor his friend and raise money for a mental health charity.

# Hargrove's review

I remember back when the Caretaker’s *Everywhere at the end of time* was a TikTok trend. I had always kind of wanted to listen to it, but six and a half hours is a lot of time to set aside. I’ve been looking forward to *An empty bliss beyond this World* as it seemed like a similar concept but in a much more reasonable time frame. While I do think it was interesting to listen to, my biggest issue is the lack of a feeling of progression. The idea of a descent into dementia fascinates me, but that feeling just wasn’t strongly evoked in me here. I get that the memory loss in dementia is cyclical and there are moments of lucidity, but I had a conceptual issue with “An empty bliss beyond this World” and “Camaraderie at arms length” (even though both are in my favorite tracks). They removed a lot of my feelings of progression. Worse, though, is that even the most fucked up tracks aren’t that far gone. There are never any that lose their musical quality, the loops just get a little shorter and a little more veiled by snaps, crackles, and pops. Now, I do think I’m being a little unfair; I’m judging this album against expectations I had formed for an entirely different album. There are some genuinely creepy moments on here, particularly “I feel as if I might be vanishing” and “Pared back to the minimal”. The Caretaker’s decision to use ballroom music like this for his project is also just genius. Even before his manipulation, my brain imbues the samples with a wistful, lost quality. It’s not just that the music is super old, but also that it was largely forgettable even at the time it was released. It hasn’t left its legacy as a standard, nor as an important influence on the music to come. It’s just been lost to time, as most people will be. Now, *that* fucks me up. Overall, this is a project with a high conceptual floor, it’s just that *An empty bliss beyond this World* often stays near that floor instead of approaching its ceiling. I hope to listen to *Everywhere at the end of time* in the not-too-distant-future, and I hope that gets closer to this concept’s potential.

“All you are going to want to do is get back there” starts things off very well. The sample was a great choice, particularly for that lonely saxophone. “Moments of sufficient lucidity” already has a sparser sample loop and more white noise. I don’t care for the sample on “The great hidden sea of the unconscious”, making it my least favorite. “Libet's delay” gets rid of some of the fuzz to bring us back to reality and has perhaps the best sample on the album. However, my favorite track has to be “I feel as if I might be vanishing”. That loop is CREEPY. I really have to commend the Caretaker on this one, I’ve been worried that some of my other favorites are largely just because of the quality of sample, but this was a brilliant way to loop this. “An empty bliss beyond this World” has the character of an old, abandoned carousel still spinning through the cobwebs. It’s the exemplary track I would show someone to introduce them to this album. “Bedded deep in longterm memory” begins to play with stereo, and also has the shortest loop so far. “A relationship with the sublime” is not all that interesting. “Mental caverns without Sunshine” is quite atmospheric and the sound starts to get really distant. “Pared back to the minimal” feels like the low-point of the album memory-wise, and I appreciate its conceptual value. Both reprises are good conceptually, but “An empty bliss beyond this World” is the only one that adds anything to its original track. I also wish they had been more spaced out. “Camaraderie at arms length” is good for the same reason the opener is good. I appreciate that the sample in “The sublime is disappointingly elusive” is actually a finale, but I do question the point of this track’s existence.

Strong concept, middling execution. It was pleasant to listen to at points though.

Favorite tracks: All you are going to want to do is get back there, Libet’s delay, I feel as if I might be vanishing, An empty bliss beyond this World (and reprise), Pared back to the minimal, Camaraderie at arms length

Least favorite track: The great hidden sea of the unconscious

# Aidan's review

Fascinating album to read about, interesting enough to listen to the first time, but there's not a terrible amount of depth here. It's more conceptual art than music, and while it's enormously cool as conceptual art, once it reaches the point, it's not super inviting to repeated listens. On its own goals, it's absolutely successful, but as a piece of music I enjoy and get something out of on repeated listens, it fails the goalposts that I set up. Reminds me a lot of Dataplex in that it's doing something immensely cool but lacks anything beyond that. In this case, I think the underlying product is far more interesting, but it's still not good enough to be worthwhile when divorced from the (admittedly brilliant) concept. More cool than good.
