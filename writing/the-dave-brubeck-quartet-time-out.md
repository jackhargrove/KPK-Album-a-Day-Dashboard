# Write-up

The Dave Brubeck Quartet was a jazz band founded in 1951 whose most most well-known lineup (lasting from 1958-1968) consisted of pianist and bandleader Dave Brubeck, saxophonist Paul Desmond, bassist Eugene Wright, and drummer Joe Morello. Dave Brubeck is sometimes known as the first “pop star” of jazz, and not only was *Time Out* the first jazz to album to sell more than a million copies, but “Take Five” remains to this day the bestselling jazz single of all time. Brubeck was born in Concord, California, and raised on a ranch in rural Ione. Hi \\s mother was a classically-trained pianist who nearly went pro. He attended the University of the Pacific in Stockton, originally to study zoology but eventually switched to music. After graduating from conservatory, he was drafted into the army in 1942 at the height of World War II. After performing piano at a Red Cross event, he was spared from war deployment, and instead performed in the US military’s first racially integrated band. He left the military in 1946 and went to study music more at Mills College in Oakland, as well as from classical musician Arnold Schönberg at UCLA. Brubeck’s strong classical background would become a notable theme in his jazz, in particular his experimentation with music theory. In the 1950s, the Dave Brubeck Quartet toured US colleges, earning him enough fame by 1954 to be the second ever jazz musician (after Louis Armstrong) featured on the cover of TIME Magazine. In 1958, the US Department of State sent the Dave Brubeck Quartet on a tour of Europe and Asia, where Brubeck was heavily inspired by the local music styles. In 1959, the quartet released *Time Out*, to this day their best known album. It’s known for its experimentation with time signatures, and it began a series of records based around that theme with “Time” in the title. The Dave Brubeck Quartet broke up in 1968. Dave Brubeck died in 2012, the day before his 92nd birthday. He remains one of the most successful jazz artists of all time, and was particularly popular with early rock musicians.

As previously mentioned, *Time Out* is known for its heavy featuring of nonstandard time signatures. In American popular music and especially jazz, nearly every composition is in 4/4 time, meaning each beat is a quarter note (bottom number) and there are 4 beats per measure (top number). When Brubeck was in Turkey in 1958, he heard a street performer playing in 9/8 (each beat is an eighth note, 9 beats per measure) with the 9 beats subdivided unevenly as 2+2+2+3 (3 short beats and a long beat). Brubeck asked the street performer where he learned such a complex time signature, and the performer responded that 9/8 is Turkey’s version of 4/4. Brubeck composed “Blue Rondo à la Turk” in 9/8 as a result, and the rest of *Time Out* followed. “Take Five” is the only song on the album not written by Brubeck, but instead by saxophonist Paul Desmond; coincidentally, it’s by far the most popular song. It takes its name from its 5/4 time signature. “Everybody’s Jumpin’” and ”Pick Up Sticks” are both in 6/4, “Kathy’s Waltz” is partially in 3/4, and “Three to Get Ready” alternates two bars of 3/4 and two bars of 4/4. The only song that is primarily in 4/4 is “Strange Meadow Lark”. Brubeck’s record label was terrified to release such a nonstandard record, and forced the quartet to record an album of safe hits to release alongside in case *Time Out* was a failure. Brubeck’s output in the late 50s belongs to a genre known as cool jazz, so called because it was mellower and smoother than the frantic bebop that dominated the 40s and early 50s. Miles Davis’ *Kind of Blue*, also released in 1959, is another seminal example of cool jazz.

# Hargrove's review

In some of these reviews, I start by mentioning when I first heard the record, often sometime around my musical awakening when I was 16 or 17. In this case, however, I first heard this record much younger. I couldn’t place exactly when, but this was the first jazz record I had ever heard. My dad introduced me to it, and I started to listen. It’s a very pretty record. Though it’s “experimental”, none of the harshness of Coleman or the abruptness of Coltrane are found here. Instead, everything is led by a master pianist, with a much-smoother-than-average saxophone and a skilled rhythm section. I think “cool jazz” really is the perfect phrase to describe it; everything feels sleek, smooth, clean. Perhaps these qualities are what prevent me from liking it more than I do (and I do, don’t get me wrong). “Blue Rondo à la Turk” is one of my favorite jazz tracks, and its rhythm has been stuck in my head (no joke) for well over a decade at this point. I have found myself tapping the 2+2+2+3 as “pinkie -> index -> pinkie -> index -> pinkie -> index -> pinkie -> ring -> middle” when I’m bored at various points in my life. Fantastic track, and the one that feels most different. “Take 5” is also a great song. I wouldn’t say I have a “fetish” for it, and I certainly prefer “Blue Rondo”, but it’s undeniable how fucking *smooth* “Take 5” is. From the moment it begins, just that cymbal tapping, the slinking of the piano, and then the sax singing the coolest melody you’ve ever heard. I very much “get it”. These are the two songs that have stuck in my head all these years, but my listens this week have shown me that I’ve been ignoring some others I shouldn’t have. In particular, I really have been enjoying “Pick Up Sticks”. I love how you can follow the piano constructing its ultimate solo throughout the song, ever-changing, ever-improving. Once it does come together it’s amazing. “Three to Get Ready” was a really tough nut for me to crack, and it’s the only song where I feel the complex meter actually makes it a challenging listen. The 3/3 + 3/3 + 4/4 + 4/4 results is really uneven phrases that were hard for me to wrap my head around. Ultimately, however, I’ve come to really enjoy it. “Strange Meadow Lark” was another one I didn’t love at first, but the beauty of the opening piano solo eventually won me over. “Kathy’s Waltz” and “Everybody’s Jumpin’” don’t leave too much of an impression, but they’re both nice filler. This album is enjoyable from beginning to end, and just evokes what it feels like jazz is supposed to evoke. At the same time, I’ve never felt as viscerally impressed by it as I have for *The Shape of Jazz to Come* or as I did recently with *A Love Supreme*. A solid, very high B Tier album.

Favorite tracks: Blue Rondo à la Turk, Strange Meadow Lark, Take Five, Three to Get Ready, Pick Up Sticks

Least favorite track: Kathy’s Waltz
