# Write-up

The Doors were an American rock band founded in Los Angeles in 1965. The most famous member of the group was frontman and vocalist Jim Morrison. Born in 1943 in Florida, his father was an admiral in the Navy. Morrison was an extremely bright young child, with an IQ of 149 and read voraciously in high school. He graduated with an 88 average. He first went to FSU for college before transferring to UCLA, where he graduated with a degree in film. After graduating, he spent several months loafing around Venice Beach in LA, doing massive amounts of LSD and writing some songs that would turn into Doors songs. His inactivity ended when he ran into Ray Manzarek, a fellow UCLA film graduate, who invited him to join a band which two of his brothers has formed, called Rick and the Ravens. The rest of the band had never met Morrison and Morrison had never sung before, but he did well enough during his first performance to garner an official invitation to the band. Eventually, Ray's two brothers, Rick and Jim, left the band, leaving it as a five piece outfit, with Morrison, Manzarek on piano, John Densmore on drums, Patty Hansen on bass and Robby Krieger, who was brought in on guitar to replace the departed brothers. Hansen was dropped when Manzarek decided that he could play bass on a cutting edge "piano bass", leaving the group a boys-only quartet. The group then changed their name to "The Doors" after an Aldous Huxley novel titled the "The Doors of Perception", itself a reference to a William Blake quote: "If the doors of perception were cleansed every thing would appear to man as it is, Infinite", which Huxley employed to advocate for LSD usage.

The group got its first gigs at London Fog, a local club, before being poached by the famous club Whisky a Go Go. While playing there, they were signed by Elektra Records president Jan Holzman. Morrison managed to get the band fired from Whisky a Go Go three days later for a particularly raunchy performance of "The End", a pattern which would continue through the band's run. Their debut album consisted mostly of original songs that the band had worked on during their 2 years of playing clubs, but also included two covers, "Alabama Song" by Kurt Weill and Bertolt Brecht (our second song by the duo after "Mack the Knife”) and blues standard "Back Door Man". While Morrison is often seen as the main creative force behind the band, the biggest hit off their debut, "Light My Fire", was written mostly by Krieger. The album was incredibly successful, with "Light My Fire" topping the Billboard charts, and garnering critical acclaim that persists to this day.

Morrison's chief influence was French poet Arthur Rimbaud, who produced all of his work between the age of 15 and 20 before having a long affair with a fellow poet, using drugs heavily, traveling widely and dying of what is now believed to be bone cancer at the age of 37. Morrison engaged in a similar set of self-destructive habits. He would often give provocative concerts. When the band got a gig on the Ed Sullivan show to sing "Light My Fire", he (intentionally or unintentionally) failed to change the lyric "Girl we couldn't get much higher" and the Doors were promptly banned. Later that year, Morrison was arrested live on stage in New Haven after he sung an improv song about an argument he had with a cop backstage, who then came out and arrested him. The charges were later dropped. As the band became more successful, Morrison's drinking began to be a problem. He gained a lot of weight and would show up to sessions and concerts drunk. He then turned to drugs, including heroin, to lose the weight. The concerts themselves sometimes became riots, something Morrison occasionally encouraged. Two years after New Haven, in Miami, he was arrested again, for yelling "You wanna see my cock". Police also alleged he pantomimed fellatio on one band member and exposed his own. Several other incidents resulted in charges pending against him during the last few years of the band's run.

Despite Morrison's issues, the band was incredibly successful. Their followup album, "Strange Days", didn't live up to the debut, but their third album, "Waiting for the Sun", hit number 1 on the charts. "Soft Parade" saw them incorporate the jazz influences of Densmore and Manzarek, and feature brass and strings to their rock sound. Morrison hated the new sound, driving the band to have individual credits on songs, and refused to have the new arrangements on any of his songs. The result is typically seen as their worst album, but still reached number 4 on the charts. They ditched the new sound for their fifth album, "Morrison Hotel", which was seen as a return to form.

The band was slowly breaking apart. Morrison was becoming more interested in poetry than rock and roll, and his drinking was making it difficult to perform with him. The band was also being blacklisted from parts of the country due to their lyrics and raucous performances. During the mastering of "L.A. Woman", Morrison left for Paris to live with his on and off girlfriend, Pamela Courson. Three months later, he died in the bathtub of his apartment. His death has long been viewed as suspicious, and years later, close friend of Morrison Agnés Varda said that she helped cover up the incident. The most likely cause of Morrison's death is a heroin overdose. His death at 27, along with the prior deaths of Brian Jones, Jimi Hendrix and Janis Joplin, sparked the notion of the "27 club", which Kurt Cobain and Amy Winehouse would later become members of when they died at the same age.

Without Morrison, the band was basically dead. The remaining members tried to find a replacement vocalist, including Morrison devotee Iggy Pop, but ended up with Krieger and Manzarek splitting vocal duty. They put out two albums, neither of which matched their output with Morrison, then split up. They would reunite to make one more album, in which they took recordings of Morrison reading his poetry and provided musical backing. It was well reviewed. Since then, the members have reunited with a host of weird collaborations, including Eddie Vedder, Skrillex and Tech N9ne. Manzarek died in 2013 from cancer. Densmore and Krieger are still going strong in their early 80s.

# Hargrove's review

I have had an instinctive aversion to the Doors for no legitimate reason whatsoever. I think I found the idea of them annoying because their instantly recognizable style sounds like self-parody if you’re a lazy dickhead and refuse to dig any deeper than “druggy yells over a dated keyboard”, and also because my dad is a superfan who tried to force the Doors on me at a time when I was a lazy dickhead. Now that I’ve finally given a real listen to the Doors, I’m pretty annoyed at myself for writing them off for such stupid reasons. Though I’ve known “Light My Fire” for over a decade, never once have I given it enough of a shot to realize how orgasmic those solos right in the middle of it are. I of course also knew the ubiquitous “Break on Through (To the Other Side)” but never realized how fun the vocals could be if I’d just wipe the sneer off my face. On top of that, deep cuts like “Soul Kitchen”, “The Crystal Ship”, and “Take It as It Comes” deliver some of the finest 60s pop rock that I just never cared to look into. And “Alabama Song (Whisky Bar)” has to be one of the most bizarre cover choices I’ve ever heard on a major album. While there’s no real weak link in the quartet, Manzarek consistently steals the show for me. That dude can fucking SHRED on the keys. Plus, that particular texture just sets the Doors apart from any of their contemporaries. Krieger holds his own on guitar while Densmore has this swinging jazz sensibility that keeps the music light and refreshing. As for Morrison, though I don’t know that his poetry always lives up to my expectations, he’s one hell of a charismatic frontman. Despite all of that praise, however, *The Doors* as an album falls prey to the same issue as many classic rock albums, especially debuts: it’s just not consistently good throughout! Side one would have been immaculate were it not for the eye roll-inducing “Twentieth Century Fox”; by contrast, boy, are there some duds on side 2. The run from “Back Door Man” to “End of the Night” is just a brutal stretch for any album. And I don’t know that I can get through any classic rock review without a blazing hot take, so here goes: “The End” is only okay. It’s fine. It’s a good track. It’s alright. It does not have nearly the payoff I’d like from an eleven-minute slow burn, let alone an eleven-minute slow burn that concludes the iconic debut album *The Doors* from iconic band the Doors. Still, I could never in good conscience put an album with a moment so enrapturing as the solo section of “Light My Fire” end up below B Tier, allowing the Doors to leapfrog the classic rock wall at the top of C Tier.

I don’t know what it means at all, but, fuck, “You know the day destroys the night/Night divides the day” is an amazing opening line for an album. I just like the way those words sound together. “Break on Through (To the Other Side)” is a serious banger, bringing together searing keys, a fun jazz rhythm, and an intense vocals. It’s hard for me to believe that this wasn’t initially a hit. Manzarek flexes his melodic muscles with a sharp-as-nails riff on “Soul Kitchen”. Morisson further blesses the track with vivid imagery like “Turn me out and I'll wander, baby/Stumblin' in the neon groves”. This is the ideal Doors album cut. I don’t mind at all the slow down on “The Crystal Ship” after the energy of the first two tracks. I love the vocal melody, hitting hardest when Morisson raises his voice at the start of the final chorus. The keys also shine as Manzarek brings in his most complex playing yet. I don’t care for “Twentieth Century Fox” at all. I like the jazzy drums, but it’s just not a strong enough pun to build a song out of. And there’s really little more to it than that. “Alabama Song (Whisky Bar)” fits the weird, dark, druggy vibe of this record so well. It’s an oddball track but it just works. “Light My Fire” is far and away the best track on the album. If it were just the verses and choruses, it would be a decent but unremarkable classic pop rock song, but holy FUCK those solos are positively transcendent. Manzarek goes INSANE and Krieger impressively holds his own in response. I also have to respect the bass vamping too, though I’m not sure whether that’s Manzarek or the session guy. The entire midsection of this track is pure bliss. I mean, it’s as good or better than any jazz solo I’ve ever heard.

“Back Door Man” is a more conventional cover than “Alabama Song”; given that, I’m disappointed they played it so straight. I would’ve preferred they made it their own as Led Zeppelin did with “When the Levee Breaks”. “I Looked at You” is almost comically weak compared to the tracks on side one. It’s not terrible but it has nothing going for it whatsoever. “End of the Night” is similarly unimpressive but with added aimlessness. It lacks any of the energy I appreciate from the Doors. “Take It as It Comes” isn’t quite as strong as anything on side one, but it’s a damn gem within this back half. A great chorus and a stellar keyboard solo? I’ll take it. There’s so much to appreciate about “The End”: the way it builds, the way the guitar and keys both play so delicately under the first verse, the lyrics and delivery from Morrison on that same verse, the ay the drums keep the intensity simmering. It’s undeniably a good track. But eleven minutes at this slow of a build require a lot of buy-in and a lot of pay-off, and it never reaches a moment worthy of its grand presentation. There are moments of pure ecstasy in “Light My Fire” for only half the length. The climax that does exist is decent, but that’s all it is. In a vacuum, this would be on my favorite tracks list, but that would feel wrong given my disappointment. That’s probably unfair, but what are you gonna do.

While *The Doors* escapes the classic rock corridor, I think the perfect place for it in B Tier, is next to *The Stone Roses*, *Red*, and *Power, Corruption & Lies*, all albums with moments of pure inspiration alongside the unremarkable.

Favorite tracks: Break on Through (To the Other Side), Soul Kitchen, The Crystal Ship, Alabama Song (Whisky Bar), Light My Fire, Take It as It Comes

Least favorite track: End of the Night

# Aidan's review

Jim Morrison is the all-American rock star in my head, taking the same role that Mick Jagger does in the UK. Living fast, dying young, mixing a brilliant intellect and good looks with an anarchist temperament and a drug habit. But for most of this album, the star isn't Morrison, but his supporting cast. Their two big hits on this album are enhanced by Morrison's howling, but it's the synth and drums that really attract me to them. Lyrically, the extended "is it drugs or sex" metaphors are clever but thin. "Soul Kitchen" is a catchy song, but most of the supporting songs on here are not that great. Both covers, "Backdoor Man" and "Alabama Song (Whiskey Bar)" don't really land, with the former feeling off for Morrison and the latter feeling too circus-esque for me. The real highlight of this album is "The End", a fantastic sprawling composition in which Morrison does his best vocal and lyrical work. I also love the little guitar lick that runs throughout the song. To me, it embodies the best of what rock music can be, combining the mundane with the mythical and evoking imagery like a surrealist poem. 3-4 good songs don't make a great record, but they make a good one.
