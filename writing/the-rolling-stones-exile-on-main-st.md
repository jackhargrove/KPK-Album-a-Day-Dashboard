# Write-up

Kent's answer to Liverpool's Beatles, the Rolling (née Rollin') Stones were founded in very similar circumstances, as a childhood friendship. Mick Jagger and Keith Richards met at age 7 and formed their first band at 15. They formed the Stones a year later, with Brian Jones, Ian Steward and Charlie Watts. Their first music was entirely covers, mostly inspired by black American musicians such as Chuck Berry and Bo Diddley. From the beginning, the band was influenced by the Beatles. First they tried to imitate them, with their first manager,Andrew Oldham, trying his hand at being Brian Epstein, putting the working class boys in suits. Then, it was the opposite, playing up their rebel image in contrast with the still clean-cut Beatles. As the Beatles began to play originals, the Stones followed. While Jones was the leader of the band at the time, Mick Jagger and Keith Richards were the ones who wrote the songs, and achieved their first big hit in 1965 with "(I Can't Get No) Satisfaction". As the counterculture grew, the Stones became enthusiastic members, taking LSD and heroin recreationally and drawing police to their unruly concerts. At the same time, the band began to break down internally, as Keith Richards stole Jones' girlfriend, and the authorities began to take notice of the band's drug usage, arresting Jagger and Richards. The acrimony eventually drove Brian Jones out of the band, and he soon after drowned in his pool, becoming the inaugural member of the 27 club.

With Jones (and the Beatles) out of the picture, Jagger and Richards were in control of "The Greatest Rock and Roll Band in the World" and soon delivered on their promise, delivering the smash hit albums "Beggers Banquet", "Let it Bleed" and "Sticky Fingers", the latter of which took a page out of the Velvet Underground's book, with a phallic inspired cover designed by Andy Warhol. On the back of their financial success, the Stones confronted a familiar foe to musicians, the taxman. Having set up a legally dubious holding corporation in the Netherlands and Caribbean to dodge taxes, the band was forced to flee their native Britain (aka Main Street) for France in order to avoid paying taxes. At the same time, they had just escaped their contract with their former label owner, Allen Klien, leaving them free of obligation, while Mick Jagger had just married his first (and only legally married) wife, Bianca.

The band rented a villa near Nice and got to recording their next album. With Richards' heroin usage deepening and Jagger busy with his new wife, the initial sessions were the least centralized the band had ever had. Still, all the original songs were composed by Richards and Jagger, and when production relocated to LA, Jagger took control again. Aretha Franklin, John Lennon and William S Burroughs all hung out with the Stones while the album were being made, and several of the songs were shifted to a more gospel-inspired sound thanks to the example of Franklin. The album was seen as middling on release, but has been reappraised as possibly their best work ever and an emblem of rock and roll masculinity such that Liz Phair felt a need to record her own answer to it, the excellent Exile in Guyville. The Stones continued their superstardom for years and years, hitting their commercial peak in the early 80s and releasing new music as recently as 2023.

# Hargrove's review

I have never been a big Stones fan. For some reason they’re always pitted against the Beatles, and I’ve always been a Beatles boy. I really love “Gimme Shelter” (as well as the documentary of the same name), “She’s a Rainbow”, and “Paint It Black”, but other singles of there’s haven’t been my favorite (looking at you “You Can’t Always Get What You Want”) and I’ve never listened to a full album before this. I found my first 3 listens of *Exile on Main St.* interesting but hard to really form an opinion on. The cool rock ’n’ roll atmosphere was apparent immediately, and Jagger positively exudes cool, but the tracks just felt so same-y. Everything ran into each other and it was hard to pick out particular songs I liked. I was ready to throw this record in C Tier. But on listen 4, two things happened. First, I was finally familiar enough with the record to pick out the songs that stood above the rest. I really, really like the opener, “Rocks Off”. The horns are amazing, the melody is so catchy, and the muddled vocals that annoy me sometimes on the album just work. I even like the lyrical content, particularly “The sunshine bores the daylights out of me/Chasing shadows, moonlight mystery”. “Sweet Virginia” is also phenomenal. I love the harmonica and the acoustic guitar tremolo intro, and the vocal melody is beautiful. Jagger does a great job sounding downtrodden, and there’s the perfect amount of extra background stuff to give it a flavor. A well-written, perfectly executed song. It’s followed by the stellar “Torn and Frayed”, perhaps the rawest song on the album, with gritty vocals and nice chord changes. “Happy” is simple but very bright, and it brings me some of its titular emotion. “Ventilator Blues” works well, with fun vocals, a distinctive chorus, and a heaviness absent in other tracks. “Let It Loose” is the standout from disc 2 for me. I love the chords and the guitar arpeggios, which feel meandering and cyclical in the best way. Side 4 is the weakest as a whole, but “Soul Survivor” is a perfect closer, with a superb performance from Jagger, particularly on the delivery of “you’re gonna be the death of me”. The second thing I realized was, with the tracks that still didn’t stand out, it was similar to The Cure’s *Pornography*. Though the songs blend together, they form one strong, unified, cohesive atmosphere. There are some stinkers, in particular I don’t love “Rip This Joint”, “Turd on the Run”, or especially “All Down the Line”. I have other issues as well. I don’t know if it’s just the 2010 remaster, but the vocals are so muddy. I got used to it, but it was very unpleasant on my first few listens. I think the songwriting as a whole is lacking, with many songs not having strong chorus melodies or distinguishing features. And I think it does not need to be a double album. However, I’m really glad I spent so much time with it, because, though it’s not my favorite album, and though I still don’t looooooove the Stones, I’ve come to appreciate it.

Favorite songs: Rocks Off, Sweet Virginia, Torn and Frayed, Happy, Ventilator Blues, Let It Loose, Soul Survivor

Least favorite track: All Down the Line

# Aidan's review

Never been a big Stones guy, but this album is everything I thought they were. The opening trio of songs explode with sex, drugs and rock and roll. The innuendos are skin tight, Jagger's voice just oozes sex appeal and the entire product just sounds so good. I don't think any individual song really stands out for being particularly innovative or catchy, although Rocks Off and Tumbling Dice probably execute the idea of the album the best. After the explosion of the first trio of songs, the album just keeps at it, with tight grooves and Jagger's growl adding panache to songs that would be ordinary if they were played by another band. I can easily see how someone would love this album, but I never got all the way into it. It's great but there's no single thing that really stands out, no true great single, no catchy ear worm of a lyric, no grand choruses. It's just rolling along. The most ambitious song on here is probably Sweet Black Angel, which is a paean to Angela Davis, but unless you google the lyrics, you wouldn't really know it. And that's not an exception, most of the lyrics are pretty forgettable, the guitar licks and tone of the vocals do most of the work, and the album feels not just skin tight, but skin deep at times. The gospel flourishes are great, and it generally sounds good, but it never sounds great. This is proper capitalized Rock and Roll, but it's never anything more. Incredibly solid, never incredible.
