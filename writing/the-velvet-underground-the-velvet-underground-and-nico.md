# Write-up

Story/Fun Facts: The original art rock album, The Velvet Underground and Nico owes the circumstances of its creation to a bona fide fine artist, Andy Warhol. The band itself was a collection of misfits. Lou Reed was the most conventional rock star of the bunch, having just graduated from college and trying to make it as a song writer for Pickwick records, where he met John Cale, a Welsh student with a passion for avant garde composers and droning violas. For a drummer, they found Moe Tucker, who insisted on playing her drums standing, eschewed cymbals and would play on garbage cans. Sterling Morris, a schoolmate of Reed, rounded out the quartet. They played their first paid gig 5 miles from my house at Summit High School before catching the attention of Warhol. Added to the band on Warhol's insistence was the German Nico, who had gotten her start in La Dolce Vita and now was one of Warhol's "Superstars".

The band got their name from a paperback novel that described the culture of BDSM and the attraction to the seedy, squalid and sexually deviant is clear from their first, revolutionary album. At the same time that the Beatles were winking at LSD usage in Sgt. Pepper, the Velvets were full throatily ranting about heroin usage in the appropriately titled Heroin. Warhol enabled their literary creativity, "producing" the album by staying out of their way and making the now famous album cover. The result was a hugely influential dud. Brian Eno famously remarked that the record "sold 10,000 copies, but everyone who bought it formed a band.” While the degree of failure was likely overstated, with contemporary estimates of sales being around 200,000, the record did have a massive influence. The droning guitars and deliberately rough production, the taboo subject matter, the feedback laced "European Son", the aping of literary culture, all of it was new and inspirational to aspiring musicians. The Velvets and Nico split ways after this album, the Velvets oscillating between trying to make conventional hits with "Loaded" and embracing their counterculture roots in "White Heat/White Light", while Nico had a long, successful solo career.

# Hargrove's review

Despite the fact that *The Velvet Underground & Nico* is one of my dad’s all-time favorite albums, and that it’s on every indie essential albums list known to man, and that just about all of my favorite artists love it, I have successfully avoided listening to this album until today. How could it possibly live up to the hype? There’s no way it could be all it was built up to be, and I’d reflexively hate it as a result. But I’m glad this project forced me to finally sit down and listen to the Velvet Underground’s review because…it’s fine? Not exactly my cup of tea or my preferred aesthetic, but a lot of pretty great songs, and some really interesting sounds, particularly for when it was released. I’m never going to see this record in the same light that my dad does, but I’m definitely glad I listened! Certainly some songs going into my rotation, and some better context on the origins of punk rock and underground music in general.

By far the star of this album is Lou Reed’s guitar. It sounds so grungy and grimy in “I’m Waiting For The Man”, and keeps that song constantly driving ahead. It rambles and whines aimlessly in “Venus in Furs”. It carries frantically strummed and chaotic solos on “Run Run Run”. Completely ignoring the rest of the song and finding its own melody on “All Tomorrow’s Parties”. “Heroin” is the masterpiece here, with the guitar part building through each cycle of acceleration until it collapses into a pile of feedback in the last cycle. It complements the vocals with rapid chord changes in “The Black Angel’s Death Song” and helps keep the song structured. It then does the opposite in “European Son”, leading some beautiful, obnoxious chaos.

Not only do I owe this album a debt of gratitude for normalizing weird and harsh noises in rock music, but it uses them pretty well absent any context too. I already discussed how the guitar contributes to this in the last paragraph. The feedback of John Cale’s electric viola is used to create the most incredible drone on “Venus in Furs” and “The Black Angel’s Death Song”, making those my two favorite songs on the album. And there are even non-musical sounds, like the stack of plates crashing in “European Son” or the hissing in “The Black Angel’s Death Song”. You then combine this with the stories told through the lyrics. Tales of crippling drug addiction, prostitution, and sadomasochistic sex. These two aspects of the album combine to give you a vivid, visceral portrait of the New York art scene at this point in the 60s.

Nico is kind of an oddity on this album. She sings lead vocals on three tracks and backing vocals on a fourth, and always feels like an interloper, clearly distinct from the Velvet Underground. I was not surprised to read that she was basically forced on the band by Andy Warhol. However, I think he had the right idea - “Femme Fatale” and “All Tomorrow’s Parties” are two of my favorite tracks. I love it when people sing in their vernacular accent, and Nico sings in this hypnotic manner that almost feels like chanting. I think the stories on both tracks are also better told by a woman, with “Femme Fatale” in particular coming across as dangerous and sinister, rather than whiny like it would have with Lou Reed singing lead. I didn’t care for “I’ll Be Your Mirror” though, unfortunately.

Good album. Important album. Influential album. Album with some banger songs. Album that’s not exactly my style, but that I can still get a lot out of. Album I’m happy to add to my list.

Favorite tracks: I’m Waiting For the Man, Femme Fatale, Venus In Furs, All Tomorrow’s Parties, Heroin, The Black Angel’s Death Song

Least favorite track: I’ll Be Your Mirror

# Aidan's review

I’ve long been a fan of both Warhol and this album. It’s not perfect and its reputation has towered over the actual music as its legend as the original overlooked gem of a rock album has grown its own mythology. Yet the album still manages to transcend at points, with the the fast and slow of Heroin standing out as incredibly cool even today. Like a lot of albums we’ve listened to so far, the album has an unfinished quality to it (listen for Moe Tucker’s drums stopping for a moment on Heroin), and the sound is raw. It lacks some of the humor in later VU albums, but Nico more than makes up for it. The second half is certainly stronger, and I do not understand the popularity of Sunday Morning (maybe it’s the only song from the album you can play to your kids?). The highs carry the lows, and it’s absolutely worth listening to.
