# Write-up

“Everyone who bought on of those 30,000 copies started a band” - Brian Eno on The Velvet Underground and Nico

Unfortunately for the Velvets, 30,000 copies wasn't that many. In reposes to their failure to make it big, Lou Reed fired Andy Warhol as their manager and got rid of Nico, reducing the band down to the quartet of Reed on vocals and lead guitar, Moe Tucker on drums, John Cale on bass/organ/electric viola and Sterling Morrison on guitar/bass. The songs were all written by Reed, with tracks selected from songs that they had already played live. While he was out of managing the band, Warhol still designed the cover and got Reed to "do the 'sucking on my ding-dong' song".  Recording was fast and hard, "Sister Ray" was done in a single take with each of the band's members playing individually due to the level of noise. That extreme noise level was aided by groundbreaking amplifiers provided by an endorsement by Vox. The album was nearly finished in a single day, but due to the boundary pushing nature, ended up taking seven sessions across two weeks. Frank Zappa was in the next recording studio over and encouraged the band to add the final gruesome sound effect to “The Gift”. In making the album, they also managed to alienate their legendary producer, Tom Wilson, who worked on their first two albums, along with 4 Dylan albums, "Sound of Silence", the Mothers of Invention and Sun Ra. Wilson never worked with the band again. This album also marked the last record with Cale, who disagreed with Reed’s pop ambitions and would go on to have his own successful solo career (check out Paris 1919!). Nobody was really happy with the final product. The recording engineer walked out of the single take of Sister Ray. Tucker thought that Reed ruined "I Heard Her Call My Name" by boosting his vocals, and Morrison nearly walked out over the same thing. The boundary pushing nature of their sheer volume ended up being impossible to transfer over to tape, and the end result was compressed and distorted from what the band wanted their sound to be.

The album continues the transgressiveness laid out in the VU's debut, with drug use (White Light/White Heat and Sister Ray), "trans-sexuals" (Lady Godiva's Operation and Sister Ray) and explicitly sexual lyrics (Here She Comes Now and Sister Ray). Sister Ray is probably the most notable song on the record, not only for its lyrical content, but also for its jazz influences (Ornette Colman specifically) and length. The album's title comes from the novelist Alice Bailey's work, "A Treatise on White Magic" which was a favorite of Reed's. The album sold even less than the Velvet Underground and Nico, prompting the band to adopt a much poppier sound for their next 2 records (both also on the list).

# Hargrove's review

I used to listen to “The Gift” a lot as a little kid (I don’t remember it being so explicitly sexual!) but other than that I hadn’t heard any song from this album. I was really excited to listen to it though because of how cool *The Velvet Underground & Nico* was and because of its reputation as their most out-there record. I ended up listening to this around 10 times over the last three days and…I’m still not sure if it’s good! What I do know, however, is that it absolutely kicks ass. It’s awesome how much creativity there is, how much fun they’re having, and the moments of sweetness that seep through the noise. Man is it a complete fucking mess though. By far my biggest gripe is the sequencing. I think “The Gift” is so cool, but putting it as the second song, immediately after how hype the opener is, really kills momentum. I get 2 minutes of “fuck yeah!” from “White Light/White Heat”, and then 10 minutes of John Cale deadpanning. Further, “Here She Comes Now” is sandwiched between two much better songs and buried deep in the middle of the record, which ends up making me forget it. My proposal is the following: “White Light/White Heat” -> “Here She Comes Now” -> “Lady Godiva’s Operation” -> “The Gift” -> “I Heard Her Call My Name” -> “Sister Ray”. Still not perfect, but as good as you’re gonna get with this set of songs. My other issue has to do with the songwriting. Only two of the tracks (“White Light/White Heat” and “Lady Godiva’s Operation”) feel like fully-formed song ideas. “The Gift” is two ideas stapled together, “Here She Comes Now” is incredibly simple, “I Heard Her Call My Name” exists for the improvised guitar solos, and “Sister Ray” is “Sister Ray”. This is a little infuriating just because songs like “Sunday Morning”, “Femme Fatale”, “All Tomorrow’s Parties”, and “Heroin” off their debut, as well as “Perfect Day” and “Walk on the Wild Side” from Reed’s solo career, show that he can write a damn good song, music that can be covered for eternity.

But still, there’s something God damn alluring about this album. I couldn’t stop listening to it, and I was excited every time. “White Light/White Heat” is a bolt of electricity straight down your spinal column with great sing-along potential. “The Gift” is an amazingly unique idea and the short story itself is amazingly well-written and paced, though the instrumental in the right ear leaves a little to be desired. “Lady Godiva’s Operation”, my favorite track, has a catchy-as-hell melody with a perfect vocal delivery from Cale. I especially love the back half, where he and Reed sloppily trade lines back and forth and they have those DIY sound effects. Great song. “Here She Comes Now” would be my least favorite were it not for the frustrating position of “The Gift”, but still it’s a nice simple song with a good chorus. The guitar solos in “I Heard Her Call My Name” are genuinely head-splitting, adapting jazz solos to rock in a way I didn’t think was possible. And “Sister Ray” is both blistering and laugh-out-loud funny (I’m sorry Carms for ruining it for you :/). Despite its length, I don’t tire of it, and the interplay between Reed’s guitar and Cale’s organ is something to behold. And the lyrics throughout are wonderful, I love Reed’s fascination with the bizarre and the degenerate. I’m on my final listen of “Sister Ray” as I’m writing this and I’m sad to move on. When I listen, I feel a considerable mix of enjoyment and irritation, though thankfully the former is stronger. I’m putting this right below their debut, as I imagine that will have more tracks I’ll want to return to, and right above *Surfer Rosa*, which I have similar but less intense feelings on. B Tier feels weird given how high the highs are, but it reflects these mixed emotions.

Favorite tracks: White Light/White Heat, Lady Godiva’s Operation, I Heard Her Call My Name, Sister Ray

Least favorite track: The Gift

# Aidan's review

The most provocative of the VU's discography, White Light/White Heat is a supremely interesting record. I really don't like judging an album by the sum of its songs, but this album is so disjointed and short that I don't think there's really any other way to approach it. The opener is a nice song, and picks up with the lyrics of drug usage where the previous album left off. The call and response is neat, and Lou Reed's patter is really cool. Then it's "The Gift", a song that manages to stay transgressive after over half a century. I winced every time at the ending, Cale's vocals are a really good fit with the sudden shift from whimsical to horrifying when Waldo Jeffers is given a SAW-esque death. The song does exactly what it sets out to do, but I would never call it an enjoyable listen, it's extremely long, the guitar is interesting but not enough and part of the shock at the end comes from how boring tedious Cale's narration gets. "Lady Godiva's Operation" is similar, as another transgressive spoken word piece, but Reed's singing makes it feel closer to their debut. Again, fine song, but not as innovative as "The Gift" and when both turn on a similar moment of horror, it's clear that "The Gift" does shock better. Then it's the two songs that were pretty clearly written for Nico. "Here She Comes Now" is a nice bit of innuendo, and manages to stay charming through its 2 minute run time. It's the most conventionally enjoyable song on the album, and I really hope there's a Nico cover out there somewhere. "I Heard Her Call My Name" is definitely the least enjoyable song on the ablum, the song isn't driving enough to make up for the extremely long free jazz guitar solo, which is about as annoying as it sounds. I do think that a better mix with more drums like the band wanted could have made it work, but alas. And then it's "Sister Ray". "Sister Ray" is nothing if not stylish. Fuck you it's 17:30, fuck you it's the same guitar riff for the entire running time, fuck you Lou Reed is going to sing the exact same melody over and over again in monotone about doing drugs and having sex with individuals of various genders. God damn is it cool. Like "The Gift", I can't profess to enjoying the entire 17 minutes, but I have to appreciate the style.

My issue with this album is twofold. One, it really does not feel like an album. Hargrove is right about the track ordering being horrendous and impossible to really fix. It's a conglomeration of good ideas that are loosely tied together by sheer transgression. Secondly, it's a victim of its own influence in that the good ideas have been ripped off and done better. "Roadrunner" by the Modern Lovers and "Drunk Girls" by LCD Soundsystem do most of the same things that "Sister Ray" and "White Light/White Heat" do, but they're just catchier songs. It's an album that's more stylish and influential than it is enjoyable. I absolutely respect Hargrove for having the shirt, and it's the type of album I might buy to flex my taste, but it's just not that enjoyable. Probably my least favorite VU album but also probably the coolest VU album.
