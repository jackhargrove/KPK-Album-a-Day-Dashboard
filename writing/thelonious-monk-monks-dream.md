# Write-up

Thelonious Monk was an American jazz pianist based in NYC. He was one of the major figures in developing bebop in the 1940s, alongside Charlie Parker, Dizzy Gillespie, and others. He was born in North Carolina in 1917 but moved with his family to Manhattan at age 5. He started to learn piano at age 9, receiving two years of classical training. However, a neighbr was also teaching him stride piano, a very early jazz style popular in the early 1920s, on the side. Stride piano developed out of ragtime, one of the first truly American forms of popular music and an important precursor to jazz; listen to “Maple Leaf Rag” by Scott Joplin, one of the artists who brought ragtime to prominence in the 1890s, as you will certainly recognize it. Ragtime’s signature piano style featured the left hand (bass) acting as a rhythm section, keeping a strict but syncopated pulse, while the right hand plays melody. Stride piano takes this left hand style and makes it more difficult to play. On beats 1 and 3, the left hand plays a single bass note on the far left of the piano, while on beats 2 and 4 the left hand plays chords in the middle of the piano. This requires the left hand to frequently move up and down the piano, hence the name “stride”. Though this was not the style Monk would always play, it was the style he was first taught, and would go on to influence his playing. He attended Stuyvesant High School but dropped out.

In the 1940s, Monk became the house pianist at Minton’s Playhouse in Manhattan. Here, he developed his own style from stride and became a central figure in the development of bebop. Bebop was an artsy and technical form of jazz that replaced the swing music that was so popular in the 1930s. It focused on speed, improvisation, and technical ability. In 1947, Monk met Lorraine Gordon and her husband Alfred Lion. Lion has produced 8 of the jazz albums on our list and, more importantly, was cofounder of Blue Note Records. Lion believed Monk was a genius and started recording his music. Monk’s records sold very poorly, but Blue Note was fine losing money on them because Lion was so certain Monk was a genius. Monk was extremely shy, so Gordon began to act as his mouthpiece to the press. In 1951, Monk lost his license to perform in NYC nightclubs after a drug bust, seriously hampering his ability to perform and grow. In the mid-to-late 50s, the hard bop jazz scene began to grow, and Monk made records with people like Sonny Rollins, Art Blakey, Max Roach, and Miles Davis. Pannonica de Koenigswarter, a member of the then-richest family in the world, the Rothschilds, who was a major jazz patron in NYC. She would be extremely close to Monk for the rest of his life. By the late 50s, Monk was extremely respected by his peers, but critics and audiences still weren’t interested. He began recording albums of original compositions and, after having his nightclub license restored, performing a residency at the Five Spot Cafe with John Coltrane in 1957.

By 1962, Monk had finally gained enough of a reputation to be signed to major label Columbia Records. The next year, he released *Monk’s Dream*, which became a top seller thanks to Columbia’s promotion. Monk was now famous enough that in 1964 he appeared on the cover of Time Magazine, one of only five jazz artists to ever do so However, his music output began to slow down significantly afterward, and by the 70s he had more or less disappeared. Monk had always struggled with mental health issues, and they had gotten really bad. He spent the last 6 years of his life living alone in Koenigswarter’s house in New Jersey. He died of a stroke in 1982. He is remembered today as a central figure in jazz and for his unique, highly percussive style of piano playing.

# Hargrove's review

A big part of my hold up this week is that I’ve found it hard to review this album. It’s been hard for me to form an opinion on it at all because it’s just so forgettable. I really did not think that would be the case. I had high expectations for Thelonious Monk. He’s a huge name and his name so often gets thrown around as a musical genius. I think maybe this album just doesn’t capture it, but I feel so empty. In my reading for the write-up, I saw a lot of talk about Monk’s percussive style of piano playing, his whole note scales, his use of dissonance. All of that is true. I could hear all of those things in this album. But every time I spotted them, I thought to myself, “what does this add? Does this make the music better?” and too often the answer was “No.” The dissonance did on occasion, but I just didn’t care about how hard he was hitting the keys in his solos. The two best songs on this album are “Body and Soul” and “Just a Gigolo”, the solo piano tracks. The former has elite propulsion thanks to the left hand in the stride technique, and the latter is so expressive for a single-instrument solo piece. I thought both were pretty great. I will also note that both were covers of standards. Imaginative reinventions of songs that weren’t meant for solo piano, sure, but the melodies are nothing new. The best original composition is “Bye-Ya”, with a pretty catchy head melody and a mercifully short run time at just over five minutes. Now, of course there’s nothing wrong with playing standards in jazz, but I’m just confused at everything I read that called Monk such a notable composer when the original composed pieces are not all that exciting. “Five Spot Blues” is my least favorite because after 6 listens, including one with note-taking, I still couldn’t tell you what it sounds like. Outside of the piano, I thought the saxophone was largely replacement level. It stood out as good in the opening of “Bright Mississippi”, but otherwise was just standard hard bop sax. The bass had some nice walking lines. The drums existed. This album unfortunately did not leave a huge impression on me. I feel similar to how I did after the Cannonball Adderley album, but I had much higher expectations for Monk. I hope I’m not getting bored of jazz. The last three jazz albums have all been hard bop, so perhaps I’m just weary of the style. We will see.

Favorite tracks: Body and Soul, Just a Gigolo, Bye-Ya

Least favorite track: Five Spot Blues
