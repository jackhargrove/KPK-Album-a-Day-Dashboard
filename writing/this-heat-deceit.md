# Write-up


# Hargrove's review

I originally heard of This Heat as one of the bands mentioned in LCD Soundsystem’s “Losing My Edge”, but I’ve never listened to any music from the band before. Like with Pere Ubu, this has resulted in the band occupying a space in my brain as something that was cool and too challenging for me. However, when I finally listened to Pere Ubu, I learned that it wasn’t actually all that challenging, and also it kind of sucked. Additionally, I’ve heard This Heat mentioned as making particularly unusual music, and have seen comparisons to the Pop Group, whose album *Y* is currently my lowest rated album on the list. All of this is to say: I was deeply apprehensive going into this album. And while there are some interlude tracks that lose themselves in sound collage experimentation, I found myself digging this album straight from the get go. In particular, I like all five of the tracks that are fully realized songs: “Paper Hats”, “S.P.Q.R.”, “Cenotaph”, “Makeshift Swahili”, and “A New Kind of Water”. I think what made them so work so immediately for me was the essentially krautrock drumming. I don’t know what This Heat did to make drums sound so crisp and forceful and downright LOUD while recording from a meat locker, but the drums blew me away. On top of that, while much of the album is obviously a deconstruction of post-punk, it still allows the angular, dissonant, spindly guitar rhythms a chance to shine, unlike The Pop Group’s total deconstruction. The result is five damn good tracks surrounded by experiments that create a truly terrifying atmosphere. And it’s interesting because it’s not terrifying in a gothic way like Nico or Tim Buckley or the Cure, nor does it sound like a spooky forest as in black metal outfits like Ulver. Instead, there’s a truly modern evil lurking in the foundation of each and every track. It makes the whiny British vocals sound resigned, hopeless, lost. Unfortunately, the album dedicates just a little too much time to the experimental interludes that feel somewhat redundant given how good the fully formed songs are at instilling the atmosphere. But there’s more than enough standout moments for me to unreservedly say that I like this album. And I’m always glad to add another original-era post-punk album into my rotation.

“Sleep” begins the album with a lullaby as creepy as it is catchy. It sets the tone in both actual aural qualities as well as in unsettling nature. The voices sound thin and spindly and British. I don’t like it in the way I like the tracks with drums, but it serves its purpose as an opener. I love the texture of the guitar in the beginning of “Paper Hats”. It then shifts to dissonant twangs, one of my favorite elements of post-punk. The drums kick into gear after around two minutes and the remainder of the song falls into a drum jam. The cymbals sound like large glass objects shattering. I don’t hate “Triumph”; I certainly appreciate that it introduces a recognizable melody even as an interlude. But I’m not crazy for the kazoo (?) and it doesn’t stand out overall. “S.P.Q.R.” starts with roaring drums and tons of energy. I love the harmonized vocals, particularly the contrast between the depth of the lower voice and the thinness of the higher voice. My favorite part of the track, however, is how the tremolo guitar radiates anxiety to build tension for the periodic drum explosions. This Heat pulls off hypnotic tricks with the cymbal on “Cenotaph”. It’s like a new kind of motorik. My favorite part of this track is when it shifts into a bridge that’s…vaguely lovely? The chords are comforting, but the vibe remains deeply unsettling. “Shrink Wrap” and “Radio Prague” follow as two formally experimental interludes. The former sounds like deconstructed Gang of Four in the way it goes about critiquing consumerism. The latter is basically musique concrète. Neither does much of anything for me. “Makeshift Swahili” is my least favorite of the fully formed tracks, but I still quite enjoy it. The guitar takes center stage at the beginning with that angular post-punk guitar sound. Then, the drums pick up and the instrumental changes completely. Finally, it shifts into my favorite section, a blistering rocker. It’s a bit too schizophrenic, but that last part is just too good.The dub production that begins “Independence” sounds really cool. Like, really really cool. However, I don’t get a ton out of the vocals at all. The sining is just so tuneless and arhythmic. The albums final fleshed-out track is “A New Kind of Water”, which might be my favorite. The guitar shimmers in a sickening way, completely out of step with the drum beat in a way reminiscent of Radiohead’s “Weird Fishes/Arpeggi”. It creates a great effect, but somehow it’s outclassed by the track’s second half, a more straightforward post-punk thriller with an insanely powerful melodic bass line. The album ends with “Hi Baku Shyo (Suffer Bomb Disease)”, an ambient outro that I take as some kind of nuclear wasteland. Like “Sleep”, it serves an important purpose to the album, but it doesn’t stand out on its own.

This was a solid high B Tier for me. I could see myself liking it even more with time. It’s really cool to see just how influential krautrock was on these bands of the post-punk era in the UK.

Favorite tracks: Paper Hats, S.P.Q.R., Cenotaph, Makeshift Swahili, A New Kind of Water

Least favorite track: Radio Prague
