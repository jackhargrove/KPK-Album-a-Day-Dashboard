# Write-up

Three 6 Mafia are a hip-hop group formed in 1991 in Memphis, Tennessee. Though their crew and affiliates create a truly massive collective of rappers, only six have ever been considered full-fledged members: DJ Paul, Juicy J, Lord Infamous, Koopsta Knicca, Crunchy Black, and Gangsta Boo (female rapper! 4chan has gone woke!). Three 6 Mafia have been incredibly influential throughout its existence; their early work popularized the style known as Memphis rap, while their middle-period work helped create and spread crunk, a style that would become dominant in the early 2000s hip hop landscape. The founding members of Three 6 Mafia were DJ Paul, Lord Infamous, and Juicy J. Paul and Infamous were half-brothers who both trained as musicians as teenagers and formed their first rap duo in 1989. Juicy J grew up on the other side of Memphis and began rapping independently. All three were inspired to make rap by the West Coast gangsta rappers that blew up in the late 80s like NWA. Memphis is not a huge city and it didn’t yet have an established hip hop scene, so Juicy J inevitably crossed paths with Paul and Infamous in 1991. They formed a group called The Backyard Posse and began making music with all three rapping and Paul making beats. They also continued making their own projects. One of those was a mixtape from Paul and Infamous in 1992 that included songs on today’s album like “Tear Da Club Up” and “Porno Movie”. They changed the name of the group to Triple 6 Mafia, evoking the devil in reference to the horror movie themes of their music. Koopsta Knicca joined in 1994, as did Crunchy Black and Gangsta Boo in 1995. In these early years, the crew released a lot of music, much of which was later put on compilation albums.

In 1995, they tweaked their name to Three-6 Mafia and released their debut album *Mystic Stylez*. At the time, hip hop was defined by the feud between the East Coast and the West Coast, and the South was largely ignored. As a result, the album was released on their own independent label and made with lo-fi equipment. It is by far the Mafia’s most horrorcore work, with cartoonish violence, Satanism, and ominous beats. They were also involved in a (mostly one-sided) beef with Bone Thugs-n-Harmony, a s similar group from Cleveland that they thought had stolen their style. “Live By Yo Rep” is a diss track against the Bone Thugs. The album cemented them as the largest group in Memphis and also made them known across the South. Atlanta duo Outkast had just released their debut the previous year, and the South was beginning to make its mark on hip hop.

After their initial success, the Mafia began their journey to becoming a massively successful group. They began to shift their sound toward a more clubbing and dancing focused direction on their third album in 1997, helping to establish “crunk”. Crunk was the first style of Southern rap to become massive nationally. It was defined by its use of drum machines (instead of drum samples), vocals that shouted directions for dancing, and a club vibe. Lil Jon is the most famous practitioner of crunk. Crunk would eventually evolve into trap in the mid 2000s, and trap became the most important genre of hip hop in the 2010s. Three 6 Mafia had become one of the biggest hip hop artists in the world by the early 2000s. They had a platinum album, multiple gold albums, and even became the first hip hop artists to ever win an Oscar (for Best Original Song). They split up in 2012 so Juicy J could pursue his solo career. Infamous died of a heart attack in 2013. Koopsta Knicca died of a stroke in 2015. The surviving members got back together in 2019 and they’ve been performing ever since. Gangsta Boo died of a drug overdose in 2023.

# Hargrove's review

This was kind of like Wu-Tang meets Black Sabbath meets Dirty South. I didn’t really know what to expect going into this other than that it would generally take the shape of 90s rap. I had heard the name before but I didn’t know anything about them. When I looked them up, I recognized the names Juicy J and Gangsta Boo, although I couldn’t have told you anything they had done. My primary context for Memphis rap was when we listened to Lil Ugly Mane a couple months ago, and I really enjoyed that album. Listening to this, I honestly felt more or less the same about it as I did LUM. Both albums are too long, but they’re also pretty consistent throughout without any real stinkers or heavy front-loading. Both are pretty smooth, although LUM is much smoother (both a good and bad thing). Both had multiple standouts with simple but amazing hooks that got stuck in my head. The primary difference was that LUM had tons of bars that I thought were awesome and have memorized, whereas I wasn’t super impressed with the rapping from Three 6. I was impressed by the rapping though. It’s a good mix of like lethargic slurred styles and frantic triplet styles. I think the members distribute their talents more evenly than Wu-Tang, but they do a worse job of differentiating themselves. I much prefer this album stylistically to Wu-Tang though. Koopsta Knicca was by far the standout to me, I got super excited every time I heard his high-pitched voice. Lord Infamous and Gangsta Boo were also impressive rappers in my opinion, although the former was very hit or miss. DJ Paul and Juicy J are decent, and Crunchy Black is basically a nonentity. I don’t think I have enough data on the affiliates. One other thing that I think is notworthy is how intensely lo-fi this album is. A lot of the best 90s rap has a lo-fi, low tech vibe, but it sounds like they made this on an abacus. This does add something to the atmosphere, especially given how relentless the description of hood poverty is in the bars. However, it’s a bit much, and the rapping gets really muffled and hard to make out. One final high-level observation from me before I move on to the tracks is that it’s really interesting to hear heavy metal-style Satanism in hip hop. You would think it would be much more common given the subject matter in hip hop, but this is pretty much the first time I’ve heard this level of occult reference. I think black people are just a very religious demographic, so it’s a tougher sell. It stood out to me, and I thought it was pretty cool and complemented their observations of gangsta life.

I think it would do a disservice to this album to go through each track individually like I usually do. There are an awful lot of them, and they all kind of blend together. There are cons to this approach, but the upside is that the album feels like a continuous, real experience. Like I’m going through life in the ghettos of Memphis. One thing I found really great on the LUM album was how effective those simple hooks were, and there are a few tracks on here that have some stellar hooks in that style. “Break Da Law ’95’” is the first real song on the album, and the hook brings the energy right away. Much later on the album, “Tear Da Club Up” has a very similar hook that is just as good, if not better. I think the choral chanting brings the energy to a fever pitch, and I think that song is uncanny in the best way, an existentially terrifying club banger. “All or Nothin” has a the slickest non-sampled hook on the entire album and also does some cool things with the flute. I think the single best hook on the album, and unironically one of the best hooks I’ve ever heard in rap music, comes from the closer, “Porno Movie”. It combines two samples seamlessly and is unbelievably catchy. Will be listening to that one a lot. On the other hand, you have a few great posse cuts that eschew the hook altogether. That can be really hard to pull off, but they do a great job off keeping my attention and transitioning from verse to verse. “Live By Yo Rep” is a great example, it just flows perfectly. I also find their style of dissing to be very funny, just cartoon-style violence with no justification. Another example of a great hookless posse cut is “Mystic Styles” near the end. Infamous’ verse here is maybe the best verse on the entire album, and Gangsta Boo and my boy Koopsta also have some serious standouts. The beats also keep things simple but can really make the song. The use of drum machines ironically makes this sound super modern when it would have made it sound dated at the time. I enjoy the beat on “Gotta Touch ‘Em - (Pt. 2)” as much as I do the exact same beat on “Like That” by Future and Metro Boomin feat. Kendrick Lamar. “Now I’m Hi Pt. 3” has such a sleazy, hazy atmosphere, I just love it. And the last archetype for songs I liked here is the type that leans heavily into the horrorcore. “Sweet Robbery” for example uses that sample of Chucky laughing to an extent that I would expect to find annoying, but it fits into the song so well. Freaky, creepy rap song. “Fuckin Wit Dis Click” is the closest I’ve seen to a song replicating the feeling of horror movies since “Black Sabbath” on Black Sabbath’s *Black Sabbath*. It even uses super similar sound effects. Koopsta’s verse sounds staright up terrifying (and a New Order reference!) and Paul has his best showing.

That’s approximately half of the songs. Those are the ones that particularly stood out to me. The ones that I didn’t mention I thought were fine. There’s really not a bad song on this album. I wouldn’t have minded seeing a few of them cut to get this album down to a more reasonable length, but I can’t single any out as bad. I do have some gripes. The lyrics do a good job setting a mood but are thoroughly unremarkable otherwise. I thought the rappers outside of Juicy J and DJ Paul were far more interesting. When Infamous doesn’t nail a verse, it can become a real drag. And at some points things got a little too smooth, and I got a bit distracted. But overall, I was really happy with this album. I’m putting it just below LUM because he gave me similar vibes but with better bars.

Favorite tracks: Break Da Law ’95’, Live By Yo Rep, Now I’m Hi Pt. 3, Sweet Robbery, Fuckin Wit Dis Click, All or Nothin, Gotta Touch ‘Em - (Pt. 2), Tear Da Club Up, Mystic Styles, Porno Movie

Least favorite track: Big Bizness
