# Write-up

Tim Buckley was a folk and experimental musician born in 1947 and raised in upstate New York. See the write-up I did for his album *Starsailor* two weeks ago for the first half of his story. *Lorca* was Buckley’s fifth album, releasing only five months before *Starsailor* in 1970 and seventh months after Buckley’s fourth album *Blue Afternoon*. *Lorca* was recorded at the same time as the latter record but sounds almost nothing like it. It was Buckley’s first foray into fully experimental music inspired by free jazz, abandoning standard tonality and defying rhythm. The lyrics are also challenging and opaque, inspired by Spanish poet and the album’s namesake Federico García Lorca. In addition to Buckley’s 12-string acoustic guitar and vocals, the album features Lee Underwood on electric guitar and electric piano, John Balkin on bass (both upright and bass guitar) and pipe organ, and Carter C. C. Collins on conga drums. Underwood and Balkin would both appear on *Starsailor* as well. *Lorca* was Buckley’s first album to not appear on any Billboard chart, a reflection of its challenging nature.

After the commercial failures of both *Lorca* and *Starsailor*, Buckley got really into drugs and booze. For some reason, he formed a funk band in 1970 and released three funk albums with sexually explicit lyrics. Unlike *Lorca* and *Starsailor*, these albums have decidedly not been positively reappraised in retrospect. He also tried his hand at acting but, of course, it was in an experimental and low-budget film. By 1975, Buckley had reached rock bottom, and he started performing his pre-*Lorca* material live in a last-ditch attempt to regain his popularity and make some money. This effort was actually moderately successful, leading to a tour that ended with a crowd of 1,800 people in Dallas. To celebrate, he went home to LA to drink a bunch of booze and snort heroin. He got too drunk at a friend’s house, so his buddies took him home and his second wife (not Jeff’s mom) put him to sleep in his bed. A while later, she checked in on him and found that he had stopped breathing. Tim Buckley was declared dead on June 29, 1975, due to a combined overdose of heroin, morphine, and alcohol. He was 28 years old. Virtually no one in Buckley’s life was surprised that he died young in this manner. The friend who owned the house where Buckley overdosed was convicted of involuntary manslaughter.

Today, Tim Buckley is perhaps best known as the father of musician Jeff Buckley. Though Tim abandoned Jeff and his mother before he was even born, and the two only met once when Jeff was eight, he was proud of being Tim Buckley’s son. Jeff became involved in the music industry in 1990 with the help of Herb Cohen, Tim’s old manager. He released one studio album, *Grace*, in 1994. A singer-songwriter take on alternative rock that included a cover of Leonard Cohen’s “Hallelujah”, it was a modest success upon release. In 1997, Jeff decided to go swimming in the Mississippi River outside Memphis on a lark. A passing tugboat pulled him into its wake and Jeff Buckley drowned. He was 30 years old, only two years older than his father. Buckley’s tragic death spurred a massive resurgence of his album from three years prior; in particular, Jeff Buckley’s version of “Hallelujah” has become the most famous version of the song and is very likely the first version any of you ever heard. *Grace* is now commonly considered one of the best albums of all time. Jeff often referenced his father and famously performed at a tribute concert for him in 1991, helping keep Tim Buckley’s legacy alive. Even though staying alive was not something either of them were particularly good at.

# Hargrove's review

Though I liked *Starsailor* far more than I expected to, I was still deeply skeptical that Tim Buckley needed two records on this list, particularly given that both come from the same era of his career. When I started listening to *Lorca*, I found that it had many of the same characteristics that I enjoyed about *Starsailor* but with less interesting compositions, seemingly confirming my fears about the record. This judgment was especially based off of the long, atmospheric opener “Lorca” and, to a lesser extent, the similarly experimental “Anonymous Proposition”. My ears had glazed over and largely ignored side 2. I then talked to Aidan, who claimed that side 2 of this record was the first time he felt he enjoyed any of the Tim Buckley music we had listened to. Since then, I’ve listened to *Lorca* with that in mind, and it has actually shifted my viewpoint on the record. I still maintain that I most enjoy Tim Buckley’s atmospheric jams. In that sense, I prefer what side 1 of this record tries to do more than I do side 2. However, I just don’t think that “Lorca” and “Anonymous Proposition” do a very good job of it. “Lorca”, at least, is pretty cool, but Nico’s *Desertshore* categorically outclasses its gothic folk horror atmosphere. “Anonymous Proposition” does little for me at all. Side 2 of this record has some decent compositions. I don’t love “Driftin’”, but “I Had a Talk with My Woman” and “Nobody Walkin’” are both enjoyable listens. They’re mostly bluesy and even a little bit funky, with just enough of the long-form experimentation I know Buckley for to keep them interesting. However, as much as I do enjoy them, they’re just not what I want out of a Tim Buckley album. There are numerous other folk artists with voices I prefer, lyrics I prefer, melodies I prefer to any of the three tracks on side 2 of *Lorca*. As a result, this record slips between the cracks just a bit and I don’t have much of a place for it. It’s fine, but I don’t find it likely I come back to it often.

“Lorca” is the track that most resembles *Starsailor*, albeit with a more plodding atmosphere. The descending riff terrifies me; in addition to it being a chromatic riff, the notes come in sets of five instead of four, rendering it deeply off-putting. It’s all very horror movie. At nearly 10 minutes, though, it gets repetitive, and I’d just much rather listen to *Starsailor* or Nico’s *Desertshore*. “Anonymous Proposition” is strictly worse, though I like the line “Love me as if someday you’d hate me”. It abandons the grim and scary atmosphere, something I respect about the previous track, in favor of something more…Spanish? I just wish these tracks had more of a hook. At least the opener had the descending riff, but here, there’s nothing for me to grasp. “I Had a Talk with My Woman” is quite nice. I love the hand drum, the soft strumming, and the way Buckley channels the expressivity of his voice into something more powerful than pure acrobatics. “Driftin’” starts off much folksier than any other track before transitioning into a slow burn blues jam. I like it at first, but the burn is too slow and it loses my interest quickly. With the knowledge that Buckley had just abandoned his wife and unborn child, the lyrics of “Nobody Walkin’” are kind of horrifying. But sometimes you gotta turn your back/Ah, sometimes you gotta turn your back/Just to show that little girl/That there is something she's gonna lack/Just to show that little girl/There's something more that you desire”? Jesus. Unfortunately, it’s a banger. The chord change at the start of the chorus in particular gets me going, and the hand drum continues to rule. It’s easily the best track on the album.

C Tier is the perfect place for this album. It’s decent enough, but it doesn’t carve out any real space for itself. *Starsailor* remains my preferred Buckley album.

Favorite tracks: I Had a Talk with My Woman, Nobody Walkin’

Least favorite track: Anonymous Proposition
