# Write-up

Tim Buckley was a folk and experimental musician born in 1947 and raised in upstate New York. At age nine, Buckley and his family moved to LA, where he began his interest in music. He was first introduced to the early 50s cool jazz of Miles Davis, the vocal jazz of Billie Holiday and Frank Sinatra, and the country music of Johnny Cash and Hank Williams. As the 60s began, a teenage Buckley became swept up in the spirit of the folk music revival and began his career as a folk singer. A high school football injury resulted in permanent damage to Buckley’s left hand, affecting his guitar playing by preventing him from playing barre chords. Buckley attended college for two weeks in 1965 before dropping out to pursue music full time. The next year, Jimmy Carl Black of Frank Zappa’s group the Mothers of invention discovered Buckley and got him set up with Zappa’s manager Herb Cohen, who had Buckley temporarily move to NYC. Buckley’s debut album, *Tim Buckley*, released in 1966 and was full of straightforward folk compositions. Critics found the result middling but were enamored by Buckley’s voice. Also in 1966, Buckley’s wife became pregnant; he chose to abandon her to pursue his career a month before she gave birth to his son, Jeff. *Goodbye and Hello*, Buckley’s sophomore album, released in 1967. It reflected influence from the growing psychedelic rock scene such as uncommon time signatures and political lyrics. Some began to compare him to Bob Dylan, which Buckley immensely disliked.

Buckley slowly increased his profile as the 60s ended, including appearances on TV shows *The Monkees* and *The Tonight Show*. In late 1968, Buckley’s friend and lyrical collaborator Larry Beckett was drafted into the military, precipitating a shift in Buckley’s music toward avant-garde jazz. His third album, 1969’s *Happy Sad*, pushed deeper into psychedelic folk territory. Still, it became his highest charting album yet, hitting number 81 on the Billboard 200. He followed it up that same year with *Blue Afternoon*, his strangest album to date. Now, Buckley was incorporating influences from the European classical avant-garde. This was his first album to cross the event horizon into territory that was too weird, only hitting number 192 on the Billboard 200. It would be his final album to chart in his lifetime. Buckley released two albums in 1970: *Lorca* and *Starsailor*. The first of these, *Lorca*, is also on our list and will be covered in its own write-up.

*Starsailor*, Buckley’s sixth album, is often remembered as his masterpiece. In addition to Buckley’s guitar and vocals, it features percussionist Maury Baker, bassist John Balkin, guitarist and pianist Lee Underwood, flautist and tenor saxophonist Bunk Gardner, and trumpeter Buzz Gardner. The latter two are brothers who were both members of the Mothers of Invention and played on *Uncle Meat*. The album released on Straight Records, Zappa’s personal record label. Given its eclectic nature, *Starsailor* received little critical or commercial attention upon release. However, it contains “Song to the Siren”, which ultimately became Buckley’s most famous song. It has been covered by countless artists, most famously British dream pop supergroup This Mortal Coil in 1983 with Elizabeth Fraser and Robin Guthrie of Cocteau Twins on vocals and guitar, respectively. This version of the track peaked at number 3 on the UK Independent Singles Chart and number 66 on the overall UK Singles Chart. It has also been covered by Cocteau Twins, John Frusciante of Red Hot Chili Peppers, Sinéad O'Connor, and Tim Buckley’s son Jeff Buckley.

Tim Buckley’s story will continue in my write-up for his other album on the list, *Lorca*.

# Hargrove's review

Given what I know about my own taste and preferences, I strongly feel that I shouldn’t like this record. I like all kinds of weird voices, sure, but Buckley’s kind of expressive, operatic, wide-ranging voice usually only works for me with women (see: Joanna Newsom, Kate Bush). Combine that with the loose, inconsistent rhythms in the instrumentals, and on first listen I was sure it would end up in D or even F tier. But then, weirdly, I grew to actually like it a decent bit. And I don’t mean that as like “I learned to tolerate it enough to stick it in low C Tier”. I’m actually, genuinely, really enjoying it now. It’s far from perfect, but wow does it have some scorching banger tracks. Buckley’s voice remains hit-or-miss for me, but when he hits, he knocks it out of the park. The story of this album is that a song can be good so long as Buckley doesn’t make a conscious decision to ruin it. When Buckley tempers himself just a little bit, even vocal performances as adventurous as on “I Woke Up” and “Down by the Borderline” can be boons. Something truly remarkable about this album is how varied my reasoning is for liking each track that I like. I like “I Woke Up” for its atmosphere and commanding grandeur, “Moulin Rouge” and “Song to the Siren” for their understated beauty, and “Jungle Fire” and “Down by the Borderline” for their seriously heavy riffs. What a weird spread! Additionally, given the capacity for this kind of music to become extremely grating, I was also impressed by how limited the misses were. “Starsailor” is the exception, indulging every one of Buckley’s worst impulses, but “Come Here Women” and “Monterey” only miss out on being favorite tracks because Buckley took things just a tiny bit too far, while “Healing Festival” is decent but not standout. All of this is to say, damn solid record.

I quite enjoy the percussive guitar intro to “Come Here Woman”, as well as the organ that traces the vocals. The first minute or so of this track establishes such a deeply unsettling atmosphere, almost gothic in nature. Unfortunately, it shifts into jamming around 1:40 which I appreciate less than the atmospherics. Buckley’s voice also goes too far. There’s a lot to like here, but too much to dislike for me to name it a favorite. By contrast, “I Woke Up” sets up a similar atmosphere and sticks to it for the duration of its runtime. Buckley keeps his voice in check without sacrificing expressivity, matching the energy of that grand atmosphere. Much like the opener, “Monterey” starts out strong, pairing a driving ride cymbal with a bluesy guitar riff and a stellar vocal melody. And, much like the opener, it shifts into a grating gear around 1:20. “Moulin Rouge” introduces a pretty melody and breezy rhythm that provide a nice contrast to the madness of the rest of the album. Plus, let’s be real: was there any way I wouldn’t love the track sung largely in French? It’s gorgeous and sweet. “Song to the Siren” is similarly gorgeous, but this time more delicate. The lyrics have been passable so far, but holy fuck is this track well-written. Where did that come from? I particularly like the phrasing of Now my foolish boat is leaning/Broken lovelorn on your rocks”. Great track. “Jungle Fire” takes some time to find its footing. Like, around half of the track. However, the second half is so Goddamn good that I’ll take it. It’s the best realization of this album’s bizarre jazz fusion yet. There’s this driving guitar riff in 5/4 time, orgasmic drum insanity, absolute vocal acrobatics from Buckley and terrifying atmospheric effects. What a wonderful, scorching banger. I respect Buckley for making “Starsailor” but FUCK is it a hard listen. It’s genuinely hellishly terrifying. Usually, I just want it to be over the second it begins. Just thinking about it makes me shudder. “Healing Festival” has this seriously uneven 10/4 bass line that I like a lot. There’s also a ton of sax. It’s decent, but not amazing. “Down by the Borderline” is funky, jazzy, bluesy, and overall just a great track to end on. These riffs have been amazing down the stretch, truly. I also love the trumpet. Buckley tries to ruin it a little bit, but the vocals end up mostly fine.

Beyond just my general enjoyment, I have to give it extra credit for sounding very little like any of the other records I liked. It’s going up in mid-to-high B Tier. I didn’t like it quite as much as Henry Cow, but I was pleasantly surprised by it in a similar way as I was by that record.

Favorite tracks: I Woke Up, Moulin Rouge, Song to the Siren, Jungle Fire, Down by the Borderline

Least favorite track: Starsailor
