# Write-up

Tim Hecker is an avant-garde electronic musician and composer from Montreal who is often regarded as the finest ambient producer of the 2010s. He earned a Ph.D. in urban noise from McGill University in 2016. Hecker released his first album, the glitch and post-rock inspired *Haunt Me, Haunt Me Do It Again*, in 2001, immediately catching the eye of Pitchfork. By 2009, he had released 5 albums of ambient music, each album receiving good reviews and getting progressively harsher while remaining ambient. He began to experiment with the pipe organ (the type of organ that they have at church) and with drone music around this time as well. In 2011, he released his 6th solo album *Ravedeath, 1972* to immense critical acclaim. In 2012, he released a collaboration album with Daniel Lopatin (Oneohtrix Point Never) to significantly less acclaim. 2013’s *Virgins* expanded on the sounds of *Ravedeath, 1972*, with many fans considering *Virgins* his masterpiece (only *Ravedeath, 1972* is on the list). He has released 4 studio albums and 2 film soundtracks since. Hecker has also made music for multiple art installations, as well as contributions to pieces of classical music. He has gone on tour with post-rock bands Godspeed You! Black Emperor and Sigur Rós, and collaborated with artists including Arca, John Cale, Oneohtrix Point Never, and Interpol.

*Ravedeath, 1972* was Hecker’s to receive critical acclaim beyond just underground critics, and is arguably his most well-liked album today. Hecker wrote the base compositions in Canada in 2010. He then recorded them live using a pipe organ in a historic church in Iceland. He returned to Montreal, where he used his laptop to surround the droning organ with harsh, distorted, and apocalyptic electronic production. The album is a true drone record, with all of the compositions being based around sustained tones from the organ with little by way of rhythm or melody. It’s the second album on this list that I would classify as entirely outside the popular music tradition (depending on how you classify jazz), after Brian Eno’s *Ambient 1: Music for Airports*. The “ravedeath” portion of the title comes from a thought Hecker had while attending a rave, while the “1972” portion refers to the album’s cover, which depicts a group of MIT students in 1972 pushing a piano off a roof. Like many of Hecker’s other projects, *Ravedeath, 1972* uses much harsher sounds than typical ambient albums, and aims to instill disease in the listener rather than relaxation.

# Hargrove's review

Not only do I think the term “ambient” does this album a disservice, I think it’s just plain incorrect. I certainly think that this comes out of the same minimalist school as ambient music does; in particular, the many tracks that feature tones that vibrate both in volume and in pitch remind me a lot of my favorite composer Steve Reich. But I truly believe that this is music that is meant to have your full attention and, in fact, I can’t imagine how you could listen to this as background. That’s what I tried to do in my first listen, and I just didn’t get it at all. I did some reading and saw that Tim Hecker’s next album *Virgins* was also highly acclaimed but not on the list, so I tried it out. *Virgins* is the same general idea but much more dynamic, much more in-your-face. I liked it immediately. It was an album I had to focus on and, when I did, it blew me away. After a couple listens, I returned to *Ravedeath, 1972* and gave it the same focus I gave *Virgins*. And I am really, really glad I did. Holy shit does this album rule. It is absolutely relentless in its droning, its atmosphere-building, its roughness, its beauty, its pursuit of the sublime. Something I found really cool is how apt the titles are. “The Piano Drop” feels like its constantly slipping away from you, the “In the Fog” suite features this murky pool of hazy distortions, “No Drums” has no drums, the “Hatred of Music” duo is dripping with blood and guts and viscera, “Analog Paralysis, 1978” feels frozen both in texture and in the guitar that goes nowhere, “Studio Suicide, 1980” sounds like it was a bitch to produce, and the “In the Air” suite features more open space than anything else on the album. It’s so singular in pursuing its vision and always knows exactly where it’s going. Really, really cool piece of art.

“The Piano Drop” throws you into the deep end immediately, acting as a stripped-back thesis statement for the album with its naked, vibrating drones. Every tentacle of sound feels like it’s leaving you, drifting off into oblivion and never to be seen again. All three “In the Fog” tracks are great and form a cohesive 15 minute piece. Part I starts so ethereal, and it slowly introduces new errant sounds into its primordial soup, every bit glitching out. It gives little tastes of melody here and there, but the background static oppresses everything, and the lack of rhythm creates a really cool unpredictability in how long the different tones last. This track is how I imagine it would feel to wake up from a serious traumatic head injury. Part II introduces a vibrato drone that shifts back and forth between two pitches but moves forward as though it were one. I love the interspersed heaviness of the organ’s bass tones. The more abrasive aspects of the track make me think of disintegration, of entropy. There’s also a grandiosity about it that makes me think of *2001: A Space Odyssey*. Part III synthesizes the first two parts to bring this suite to a finale, combining the murky stew of part I with the vibrato drone of part II. It eventually dissipates into nothing for one of the most minimalist sections of the album. “No Drums” is a shift into a smoother gear than “In the Air”, but it begins a shift to an even darker, gloomier atmosphere. It’s unfortunately a little static for my taste, but it’s a great atmospheric interlude between “In the Air” and “Hatred of Music”.

Part I of “Hatred for Music” is genuinely hateful. This track is the greatest burst of energy in the album, but it’s a snarling, destructive energy. We fade into a complete cacophony; instead of the hazy gumbo of “In the Air”, we get a puddle of bubbling corrosive purple acid that’s trying to eat everything it comes into contact with. At first you get a single synth that cries out for help, periodically announcing itself as it rises above the din. Eventually, it disappears, and is replaced with a rumbling and dull guitar sound, as if its had its skin eaten clean off. This track is INSANE. Part II is much simpler, as the acid has left but still the drone remains. It’s even louder than before, defiant to whatever damage it took. All the while, theres a disembodied piano in the background, playing aimlessly but unperturbed. “Analog Paralysis, 1978” begins static and atmospheric, but the last third features a quiet guitar that’s a highlight of the album for me. There’s something so wistful and downtrodden and just plain broken about it. “Studio Suicide, 1980” is the most textural piece on the album, with this layering of sounds that feels completely 3D. Instead of the drone being a line through the center, it’s a cylinder of sound traveling through space. Although everything is blended together, you can occasionally pick out individual flourishes. It just feels *full*.

Part I of “In the Air” is a shock to the system, replacing the density of the last track with more open space than you know what to do with. The piano in the first half is aimless, but when the loud notes hit it’s absolutely *jarring*. In the second half, the piano is replaced with the two-pitch vibrato drone from earlier that gives some forward motion. I love how unpredictable the track is, as it feels like it slips into the lower drone without warning. Part II trades the more pleasant sounds of part I for some serious electronic harshness. Despite that, there’s still a very open-air feeling, with the drone as the land and the static as the sky. There’s still those loud notes that are amazing when they hit, and the piano just floats on through the air. With part III, the piano dominates and we enter our first calm in a long while. There are these ethereal voices with a very reflective quality; everything about the track invites you to sit back and ponder. There’s a cool motif in the voices too that grows more frequent as we approach the end, giving the shape of a worn-out and tired old melody. Excellent closer.

It took a few listens and a little effort on my part, but I was eventually blown away by this record. One of the coolest that I hadn’t heard before. As I said earlier, I really don’t think this is ambient, and I think it’s far better than any of the true electronic ambient stuff we’ve heard so far. I think this demands attention, and I think it deserves it. I became mesmerized, and it eventually felt like time wasn’t passing at all. Just masterful.

Favorite tracks: The Piano Drop, In the Fog I, In the Fog II, Hatred for Music I, Hatred for Music II, Analog Paralysis 1978, In the Air I, In the Air II, In the Air III

Least favorite track: No Drums

# Aidan's review

Really cool album, had no clue what it was going into it and really enjoyed it both ambient and actively. Strangely beautiful, evokes a lot of the same feelings as Godspeed, but via a completely different method. It’s the rare record that’s incredibly interesting to think about and listen to. Virgins was also great. It’s hard to drill down into specifics because this album just floats above your perception for a lot of it, but it’s never not beautiful.
