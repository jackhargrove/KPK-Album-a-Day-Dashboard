# Write-up

"Your favorite artist's favorite artist" has been used to the point of cliché, but it's an apt descriptor for Tom Waits. To one critic, "he's more Dylan than Dylan", he's been covered by everyone from Bruce Springsteen to Scarlett Johansson. You hear his music at the beginning of the Wire and at the end of the latest Knives Out film. Legend has it he was born in the back of a truck in Pomona, California (Waits is known for his persistent fibbing and stonewalling about his personal life, preferring to direct the focus to his stage persona). He fell in love with music in his early teens, and has a laundry list of influences, starting with his grandfather and extending out to Motown, Dylan, the beatniks, Lenny Bruce and Roy Orbison. He got his start singing at the Troubadour in LA, where he met record executive David Geffen (who would go on to found his eponymous label), who signed the 22 year old. His first album was most notable for having a song covered by the Eagles. He soon managed to date Bette Midler and open for Frank Zappa and the Mothers of Invention. His first big album was his fourth, Small Change, which grazed the Billboard Top 100 and garnered critical praise. The list of celebrities who crossed paths with him around this time boggles the mind. He dated Rickie Lee Morgan, hung out with and was casted in the director debut of Sylvester Stallone, was asked to write a soundtrack by Francis Ford Coppola and had his song "Jersey Girl" become a concert staple in the hands of Jersey's own Bruce Springsteen. It was at this point, with his career picking up steam, he met his wife, Kathleen Brennan, who would come to be a major influence on his future artistry. They got engaged within a week and are still together to this day.

Brennan took over as his manager and introduced him to new influences, particularly Captain Beefheart, who would shape the next few albums that Waits would release. He began writing more in imagery and left his longtime home in LA for Ireland, then NYC. His next three albums formed a loose trilogy and are his most critically well regarded works. First, he released the self produced Swordfishtrombones, and was showered with praise, with one outlet calling it the second greatest album, full stop in 1989. For Rain Dogs, he holed up in a studio on the corner of Washington and Horatio Streets in NYC and started recording the ambient sounds of the street. His backing band included Keith Richards of the Rolling Stones and was given considerable leeway to improvise. It drew from an even more eclectic range of influences: German socialist composer Kurt Weill, the American blues tradition and New Orleans funeral marches. The result was somehow outshined Swordfishtrombones, counting among the best albums of the year and becoming an inspiration to artists such as Thom Yorke and Elvis Costello. Soon after its release, his first son was born. He then wrote a musical and the songs in it would be released as the final album in the trilogy.

Waits' music is characterized by his wide range of instrumentation, vivid imagery of the urban poor and his gravelly voice. He draws lyrical inspiration from authors including Jack Kerouac, Charles Bukowski, Dashiell Hammett and John C. MacDonald. His voice was originally inspired by his grandfather and Waits himself has described it as "Louis Armstrong and Ethel Merman meeting in Hell". While his early albums mostly stuck to a jazz rock sound, starting with Swordfishtrombones, he began to vary more widely, eventually reaching instruments as diverse as the the singing saw and the Stroh violin.

Waits has continued releasing albums, with his last release in 2011, and all of them well received. He's a fairly prolific actor, appearing in several Coppola films as well as a fairly random assortment of mid-budget movies, from Licorice Pizza to the Book of Eli to The Ballad of Buster Scruggs. He's written poetry, and been inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame.

# Hargrove's review

Speaking of artists I want to like more than I actually do: Tom Waits! Although in reality, I enjoy Tom Waits’ music much more than I do Fugazi’s. My trepidation with Waits is more that he constantly walks a line between delightfully weird and frightfully irritating. I tried *Rain Dogs* for the first time as a teenager, and have listened to it more than 10 times this week, and yet I still find myself having trouble articulating an opinion on it. There are some really cool songs on this thing that you just can’t get anywhere else: “Singapore”, “Clap Hands”, “Jockey Full of Bourbon”, “Tango Till They’re Sore”, “Diamonds & Gold”, “Rain Dogs”, “Anywhere I Lay My Head”. Tracks like these, heavily skewed toward the album’s first half, create this dark, sleazy, cinematic, almost cartoonish atmosphere that’s an absolute joy to listen to. The marimba, one of God’s great gifts to mankind, factors heavily into many of these tracks. They also generally feature a subtler, more measured vocal style. Unfortunately, this album also has quite a few tracks that abandon this dark funhouse freak show club atmosphere for more generic country folksiness. Some of these tracks even directly ape Springsteen, an artist I like but whose style can be unbearable without his particular charm. There are a few gems within this set of tracks, namely “Time” and “Downtown Train”, but they make the back half of this album noticeably weaker than the front. I was also generally underwhelmed by Waits’ lyrics across nearly every song, regardless of whether the instrumental worked. Still, the highs are damn high on this album, and they’re not few nor far between.

“Singapore” is such a cool fucking track. Its atmosphere is simply unimpeachable. It weirdly reminds me a lot of *The Marvelous Misadventures of Flapjack*, giving visions of cartoonishly grotesque sailors. The percussion rules, and “While making feet for children’s shoes” is a great euphemism for sex. “Clap Hands” kicks ass too. From the catchy and sleazy vocal line, to the exotic marimba, to the tribal undertones, this track makes me feel like I’m living in a funhouse mirror, and I just love it. “Cemetery Polka” keeps up the atmosphere, but it is kind of annoying as an individual track. It cancels out. “Jockey Full of Bourbon” rocks though, containing one of the better guitar solos on the album and a sweet Spanish surf rock intro. I love the poorly played piano of “Tango Till They’re Sore” and the interplay between the trumpet and the vocals. The start to “Big Black Mariah” reminds me of Howlin’ Wolf’s “Smokestack Lightning”, an all-time great track; unfortunately, this song completely blows. Waits goes way overboard with his vocals. Jesus Christ! “Diamonds & Gold” makes up for it with the beautiful marimba. I don’t even care if the songwriting is lackluster, I just adore listening to it. “Hang Down Your Head” features the first Springsteen impression for a pretty bleh track. From a pure singer-songwriter perspective, “Time” is the best track. While I found this album’s lyrics generally disappointing, the third verse here just destroys me: “Well, things are pretty lousy for a calendar girl/The boys just dive right off the cars and splash into the street/And when they're on a roll/She pulls a razor from her boot/And a thousand pigeons fall around her feet/So put a candle in the window and a kiss upon his lips/As the dish outside the window fills with rain/Just like a stranger with the weeds in your heart/And pay the fiddler off till I come back again”. I also love the swelling accordion under the chorus.

“Rain Dogs” kicks off side 2, but it’s more the last demonstration of what made side 1 so good. You gotta have that marimba, and now there’s some angular guitar in the mix. I find the central metaphor of the “rain dog”, a dog lost on the street after the rain has washed away its scent trail, to be deeply affecting. “Midtown” is a whatever spy music interlude. “9th & Hennepin” has some good lines, particularly “Ah, there's nothing wrong with her $100 won't fix” and “And the clock ticks out like a dripping faucet/Till you're full of rag water, bitters and blue ruin/And you spill out over the side to anyone who'll listen”, but it has that quality of Patti Smith-style spoken word poetry that I do not like one bit. “Gun Street Girl” marks the moment when the album shifts from unique atmospheres to more pedestrian folk sounds. It’s a decent track that tells a coherent story, but I’m not crazy about it either. “Union Square” is a true throwaway, finding Waits at his most vocally annoying atop a bog standard blues. “Blind Love” is the nadir of side 2, fading into background musically as Waits loses his mind vocally. “Walking Spanish” is certainly an improvement, but it finds Waits singing bad Dylan lyrics in a bad Dylan voice. Now, to get the elephant out of the room: yes, “Downtown Train” is the Springsteeniest of all the Springsteen tracks. But he actually pulls it off. It sounds as grand an important as any proper Springsteen anthem. It’s a great track. “Bride of Rain Dog” is a good interlude that reminds us of the first side’s incredible atmospheres. I very much enjoy how there’s really no other song in existence that sounds like “Anywhere I Lay My Head”. Whether the solemnity of the vocal section or the raucousness of the dixieland ending, this track closes the album in style on a high note.

This album will find itself somewhere in the middle of B Tier, a placement that accurately reflects its inconsistency but does not accurately reflect its singular weirdness. I think that even if you absolutely hate it, anyone who listens to this album is better for having listened to it. But that doesn’t mean I connect with it or cherish it in the way I do my favorites.

Favorite tracks: Singapore, Clap Hands, Jockey Full of Bourbon, Tango Till They’re Sore, Diamonds & Gold, Time, Rain Dogs, Downtown Train, Anywhere I Lay My Head

Least favorite track: Big Black Mariah

# Aidan's review

This is one of those albums that I would love to love, because a bunch of people I think are really cool hold it in high esteem. And there are some great moments, especially once you get into Waits' voice. There's some beautiful lyrical moments on tracks like Time and Rain Dogs, the street sound influences are interesting and I especially love how well his rough voice can express emotion on tracks like Hang Down Your Head and Downtown Train. Still, after all my listening I'm not enthralled by the rougher blues sound and the more exotic instrumentation on some of the earlier tracks. It feels kind of true to who Waits is that this album is so uneven, but that's still what holds it back for me.
