# Write-up

Vince Guaraldi was a jazz pianist from San Francisco born in 1928. He was born in 1928 and served in the Korean War as a cook. He began releasing music in the 50s and formed his first trio in 1954. This trio, like others that followed for Guaraldi in the 50s, had no drummer. Guaraldi signed to a label in 1956 and went solo in 1959. Though he would play in groups with other musicians for the rest of his career, he had a habit of seeing the session musicians he played with as technicians rather than artists, often not even keeping track of which drummers and bassists played on which recordings. Guaraldi was a part of the West Coast jazz scene that pioneered cool jazz, in contrast to the frantic and stressful bebop in New York. His 1962 album *Jazz Impressions of Black Orpheus* was his breakthrough and made him a well-known name in jazz nationally. The music was all inspired by the Brazilian film *Black Orpheus* and, as such, featured fusion of jazz with bossa nova music. This style would become popular in the US in the early 60s, peaking in 1964 with *Getz/Gilberto* (on our list). Guaraldi’s song “Cast Your Fate to the Wind” became a surprise hit, winning a Grammy and catching the attention of television producer Lee Mendelson. Mendelson was planning a documentary of Charles Schultz and his comic strip *Peanuts*, which began in 1950 and had become popular. Mendelson thought Guaraldi’s music evoked the same feelings as *Peanuts* and requested Guaraldi write the score to the documentary. Guaraldi accepted, and not long after he phoned Mendelson to play his new piece for him. The piece was “Linus and Lucy” and would forever serve as the theme song for *Peanuts*.

Mendelson couldn’t find a network to air his documentary, and it was shelved. Guaraldi chose to release the music he had written for it anyway as *Jazz Impressions of a Boy Named Charlie Brown* in 1964. *Time* magazine featured the characters of *Peanuts* on its cover in April 1965, making them more famous than ever. It inspired Mendelson to develop a half-hour *Peanuts* special that Christmas. He and Schulz pitched it to Coca-Cola, who agreed to sponsor its production. Despite the time crunch, the special was successfully made and proved quite influential. It didn’t include a laugh track, featured untrained child actors, referenced religion bluntly, and had a jazz soundtrack from Guaraldi. Much of the music consists of Christmas standards like “O Tannenbaum” (“Oh Christmas Tree”), “What Child Is This”, “My Little Drum” (“Little Drummer Boy”), “Hark, The Herald Angel Sings”, and “The Christmas Song” with jazzy improvisation from Guaraldi. It also includes the original compositions “Linus and Lucy”, “Christmas Time Is Here”, “Skating”, and “Christmas Is Coming”, the first two becoming Christmas standards in their own right. The tracks “Christmas Time Is Here - Vocal” and “Hark, The Herald Angel Sings” both feature singing from a children’s choir in San Rafael, CA; the take chosen for the latter song was picked because it was slightly out of key, giving a more authentic and childlike feel.

The *A Charlie Brown Christmas* special was a massive and unexpected success, with the soundtrack in particular receiving acclaim. It is now the second best-selling jazz album of all-time, after Miles Davis’ *Kind of Blue*. It introduced jazz to a new generation of children just as rock was taking over in popularity. Guaraldi would go on to do music for 16 *Peanuts* TV specials and movies. His brand of cool jazz has become synonymous with the franchise, both combining whimsy and melancholy in equal measure. As the 60s turned into the 70s, Guaraldi remained cutting edge and experimental, moving into jazz fusion genres. He never toured outside of Northern California. In 1976, he died suddenly of a heart attack immediately after a performance at the age of 47. Although he waned in popularity after, he has had a major resurgence and is more popular than ever today.

# Hargrove's review

It’s really hard to separate the music here from my nostalgia. My mom loves the Charlie Brown TV specials and religiously makes us watch them at the appropriate holiday every year. They’re really good specials, even outside of the music. They are consistently more thoughtful and profound than the other specials. And the music just fits. It hadn’t ever even occurred to me before this list that the guy who did the music for the Charlie Brown specials was probably a real musician who did real stuff beforehand. It all just feels like a natural outgrowth of the *Peanuts* universe. Like it spontaneously began playing when they finished the special. Unfortunately, others have already taken all the words I want to say about how closely the music matches *Peanuts*, how they both combine whimsy with melancholy (not my words). It is what it is. Those people are completely right, and I’ll say it even if I’m being unoriginal. Both the specials and the music somehow capture an overbearing wistfulness without losing a strong sense of hope in the slightest. So many songs are in a minor key (not my observation) but keep a certain buoyancy. It captures what it was like to be a kid better than almost anything else (not my comparison). This is a frustrating review to write because every last thing I want to say has been said. But the rub is this: it deserves it. This is a gorgeous album that captures something almost nothing else does. It’s not something I want to listen to every day (luckily, it has an element of seasonality built into it), but wow can it hit you like a ton of bricks if you’re in the mood. It’s the pinnacle of cool jazz I’ve heard so far, and I really like Brubeck.

“O Christmas Tree” is not one of my favorite Christmas standards, but Guaraldi does the melody justice in “O Tannenbaum”. I think it was a really good idea to start the album off with a standard played straight before breaking out into the jazzy fun. Though it’s my least favorite of my favorites, well, it’s still one of my favorites. “What Child Is This” is just a banger. I’m biased because “Greensleeves” is one of my favorite traditional melodies, but man Guaraldi knocks this one out of the park. So nice and smooth, and the chord changes are pure sex. Great track. “My Little Drum” has a nice bossa nova rhythm and introduces the first vocals. I love the little sound effect vocals on this track. It’s fun and playful. The piano sounds at once incredibly delicate and incredibly warm. “Linus and Lucy” is just iconic. The counterpoint is stellar. It’s so danceable. Banger, of course. “Christmas Time Is Here - Instrumental” is such a beautiful Christmas song. I don’t know how someone can just pull a song like this out of his brain. It feels absolutely timeless. “Christmas Time Is Here - Vocal” adds lyrics that, while simple, feel achingly earnest. The children’s choir sells that feeling so hard. “Skating” continues the run of all killer no filler. The piano melody is just INSANE. It sounds like an icy waterfall, one note cascading over the next. Like an ice skater crossing one leg behind the other again and again. Perhaps the best song on the album. “Hark, The Herald Angel Sings” and, later, “Für Elise” are both shorter tracks that work very well in the special but function more as interludes in the scheme of the album. I don’t love how close together they are, but ultimately it’s harmless. “Christmas Is Coming”, like “O Tannenbaum”, is one of my weaker favorite tracks. The left hand in the piano carries it. Still, it’s good. “The Christmas Song” sadly ends things on a bit of a weak note and is my least favorite track. It’ just a little too downtempo and barren for my liking. It doesn’t balance out the melancholy quite enough.

Just good stuff. So pretty. So smooth. So nice. I’m glad this list made me listen to it again. Now I just want the holiday season to hurry up and start…

Favorite tracks: O Tannenbaum, What Child Is This, My Little Drum, Linus And Lucy, Christmas Time Is Here - Instrumental, Christmas Time Is Here - Vocal, Skating, Christmas Is Coming

Least favorite tracks: The Christmas Song
