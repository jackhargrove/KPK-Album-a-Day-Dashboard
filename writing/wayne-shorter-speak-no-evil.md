# Write-up

Story/Fun Facts: I imagine many of you guys have very little context for jazz and subgenres (I have a tiny bit of background), so I’ll give some background here. Jazz developed in New Orleans in the 1900s and 1910s, and then developed further in Harlem in the 1920s. Up through the 1930s, jazz was typically a dance-focused genre, with easygoing tunes that people could enjoy during a night out. In the 1940s, however, a new generation of jazz musicians emerged on the scene, led by saxophonist Charlie Parker. They played a more abstract form of jazz that was meant to be enjoyed artistically (as opposed to background music for dancing), with a focus on extremely fast tempo, improvisation, frequent key changes, and high-level musicianship. This new form of jazz was referred to as “bebop” or “bop”. In the 1950s, bebop continued to evolve with a new group of jazz musicians known as the “cool” school, led by Miles Davis and John Coltrane.  Miles Davis’ 1959 album *Kind of Blue* pioneered **modal jazz**, a genre that experimented with musical modes (the stuff Carms is talking about when he says phrygian, mixolydian, dorian, etc.), while John Coltrane’s 1960 album *Giant Steps* took jazz to a new level of virtuosity and music theory complexity. In the 1960s, artists began making music that would be called **hard bop**, which simplified the song structures of bebop and introduced influence from blues and gospel music. There was also music known as **post-bop**, a broad and amorphous term that described jazz music combining bebop, hard bop, modal jazz, and free jazz (an avant-garde style). Charles Mingus was one of the defining artists of post-bop, as was Wayne Shorter, the artist of today’s album.

Wayne Shorter was born in Newark in 1933 and earned a degree in music education from NYU in the 50s. His primary instrument was the tenor saxophone until 1968, when he switched to the soprano sax. After serving in the military, he joined Art Blakey’s group the Jazz Messengers, where he eventually became the primary composer. In 1964, he joined the Miles Davis Quintet, where he was the only composer who could keep up with Davis musically. While playing with Miles Davis, he also began recording solo records for Blue Note Records, a legendary jazz label known for supporting hard bop, post-bop, and the avant-garde. He recorded *Speak No Evil* in 1964, but it was not released until 1966. Today, it is typically regarded as his most essential and identifiable solo album. Legendary jazz musician and fellow member of the Miles Davis Quintet Herbie Hancock plays piano on *Speak No Evil* and served as Shorter’s sideman for a time. Shorter stayed with the Miles Davis Quintet until it broke up in 1968, and he continued to play as Davis’ sideman through 1969’s *Bitches Brew* (which is on this list). In later years, he would continue to collaborate with artists such as Joni Mitchell and Carlos Santana, as well as with Steely Dan, whose album *Aja* (also on the list) features saxophone solos from Shorter. He died in 2023.

# Hargrove's review

I have limited experience with jazz, but the other jazz albums that I have listened to are primarily in the hard bop and early post-bop eras, so this was a relatively easy transition for me. All the songs follow the formula I’m used to of opening composed section, series of improvised solos, closing composed section. Shorter’s tenor is really the star of the show here, but every instrument is being used well. Hubbard’s trumpet comes in for a few really nice solos, Hancock’s piano constantly feels like it’s doing its own thing without regard for the rest of the song which gives kind of an avant-garde flair, Jones’ drums are phenomenal and a consistent highlight for me, and Carter’s bass isn’t flashy but it gets the job done. I lack the capacity to truly “get” some of the modal stuff, but I do enjoy how the scales ground the improvisation; it feels like they all start ordered and safe, and they slowly deconstruct as the solo gets further and further away from the original scale. So I disagree with Aidan there. For me, this album encapsulates a sleazy, late night jazz bar vibe that really resonates with me, but I don’t have the proper jazz context to know if that’s unique to this album.

“Witch Hunt” is really great and it hooked me immediately. The noodling at the beginning got me into that jazzy mood, and the composed sections at the beginning and end have one of the better tenor sax melodies on the album. It really gets loud and overblown, and the drums (particularly the cymbal work) complement it so perfectly. “Fee-Fi-Fo-Fum” opens with another elite sax melody, with this one in particular feeling the most like nightlife, and also kind of inquisitive. Hancock goes crazy on the piano in this one, it’s just constantly noodling around in the background, and when it gets the final solo it feels great to finally hear it in the foreground. The trumpet solo really gets up there and it sounds great, but the sax solo is probably my least favorite on the album. The piano is again a star on “Dance Cadaverous”, where I find the sax melody a little more lackluster. However, Shorter makes up for it with his solo, which feels abrupt and I really like it when it gets fast at the end. The drums are also just perfect and unpredictable here.

“Speak No Evil” feels like it’s constantly going up and down stairs. I like the crescendoing sax in the beginning and end sections, and the drums and sax combine so beautifully in the solo (although it does go a little too long unfortunately). The trumpet and piano solos are well done also. “Infant Eyes” has the sleaziest night club vibes of any track. I can really see myself sitting at a table, zonked out on heroin, lamenting everything with a drink held loosely in my hand as the rain pours into the night outside. It stays a little too consistently mellow for me though, and I found it a bit of a slog on repeat listens. “Wild Flower” is far and away my favorite track on the album; the sax melody is so damn catchy, it’s been stuck in my head all day. I just really like the long notes converging into staccato notes combining back into long notes. During the sax solo, the drums and piano work together to destabilize the whole thing, and it comes off wild and frantic. A beautiful but wild flower.

I really enjoyed listening to this and I’m super looking forward to digging into more jazz in the future. I’m excited to see if this record is just quite good, or if I just like jazz more than I thought. Listened to this quite a few times throughout the day and it just kept me in a great mood.

Favorite tracks: Witch Hunt, Dance Cadaverous, Speak No Evil, Wild Flower

Least favorite track: Infant Eyes (sorry Dan)
