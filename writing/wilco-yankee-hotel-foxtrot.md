# Write-up

Wilco's story began in Belleville, Illinois, a midsized suburb of St. Louis. Jeff Tweedy, the future frontman of Wilco, was invited by Jay Farrar to join his rockabilly band, the Plebs, which consisted of Farrar and his two brothers. Tweedy wasn't a good guitarist at that point, but they needed him to be eligible for a battle of the bands contest that they ended up winning. Tweedy tried to push the band towards the punk rock that he was a fan of, but there was no real audience for it in the St. Louis area, so the band settled on playing uptempo blues-y garage rock. Jay's two brothers left the band, one due to disliking Tweedy's push towards punk and another to college. In their place, Mike Heidorn was brought in on drums, leaving Tweedy on bass and Farrar on lead guitar, with Tweedy and Farrar splitting vocal duties. The band renamed itself to The Primatives and then Uncle Tupelo, before getting enough attention to get singed with indie label Giant Records. Their first album, "No Depression", is sometimes credited with founding the alt-country genre. The title track was a cover of a Carter family song about dying to escape the Great Depression, while other songs talked about drinking whiskey, drinking wine, working at factories and getting drafted to fight in the Gulf War. The album blended the lyricism of Woody Guthrie with the punk/indie rock instrumentation of bands like the Minutemen. The album sold well enough for an underground record to merit a followup, which was also well received.

In 1992, with the USSR in pieces, and Clinton about to enter office, the underground suddenly underwent its own transformation as Nirvana's success sent every record label scout into dive bars from Seattle to Atlanta looking for the next underground hit. Uncle Tupelo was aware that any sort of alternative album would get them signed with a major label, but the group decided to go in the exact other direction. Their third album, the modestly titled "March 16-20 1992" was produced by Peter Buck of R.E.M. and expelled all tinges of alternative rock from the band's style. The band had brought on Brian Henneman on as a multi-instrumentalist for their second album and he took on an increased role here, teaching himself to play mandolin and boutouki (a Turkish lute) for the acoustic folk album. Still, despite wanting to reject the major labels, Giant records hadn't paid them any royalties and the big labels came knocking, so the band signed with Sire, a Warner Music subsidiary. With Heidorn settling down with a steady job and a wife, and Henneman starting his own band, the Bottle Rockets, the band brought on Ken Coomer on drums, Max Johnston on mandolin and violin, and John Stirratt on bass, shifting Tweedy to guitar. Their first album on a major label and as a five man group, "Anodyne" featured more personal lyrics, increased songwriting duties for Tweedy and was recorded in a single take for every song.

Anodyne managed to graze the charts and the executives were happy, but internal tensions between Farrar and Tweedy began to drive the band apart.  Tweedy was expanding his creative role in the band from just the music to now writing his own songs, leaving Farrar feeling like Tweedy was taking over. Seeing Tweedy possibly hit on his girlfriend didn't help either. Farrar told their manager that he was done, who told Tweedy, leaving Tweedy feeling disrespected as well. Still, the label wanted a tour, so the moribund band went on tour, and Farrar refused to sing harmony on any of Tweedy's songs. The band even went on Conan (and played a Tweedy song, further annoying Farrar). The band's last show jokingly promoted the band as "the 4th best country band in St. Louis".

In the aftermath of Uncle Tupelo, both Tweedy and Farrar looked to prove themselves and outdo the other. Tweedy managed to keep the entire supporting cast with him, and while he considered keeping the Uncle Tupelo name, ended up changing it to Wilco, military slang for "Will Comply". Farrar formed a whole new band in Son Volt. Each band signed to a major label, Son Volt to Warner and Wilco to Reprise, a Warner subsidy, and each put out a debut, Son Volt with "Trace" and Wilco with "A.M.". Tweedy tried to make Wilco into a more collaborative band, asking each member to try to write songs, but only a single song from Stirratt made it onto A.M. and every other song ever from Wilco has been totally or partially written by Tweedy. Unfortunately for Tweedy, "Trace" was more commercially and critically successful than A.M. and received acclaim matching or exceeding what Uncle Tupelo had garnered.

Regathering, Wilco added multi-instrumentalist Jay Bennett on keyboard and guitar, and started recording a second album that would shift away from the alt-country style. While recording the album, titled "Being There",  Max Johnston left due to role clashes with Bennett, and Bob Egan joined on guitar. The band was so productive that their 19 songs couldn't fit into a single album, but Tweedy was concerned that a double album wouldn't sell well. The album agreed to sell the double album at the price of a single album in exchange for Wilco's royalties, which would total $600,000. Still, the album was extremely well received, managing to chart and gaining the critical approval that A.M. had lacked.

"Being There" also garnered the attention of Billy Bragg, a British singer known for his left wing political views. Bragg had just been asked by Woody Guthrie's daughter to record unreleased songs that her father had written and Bragg wanted Wilco to record with him. Bennett was particularly enthusiastic about recording with Bragg, one of his idols. The project set out to take Guthrie's lyrics and put them next to more modern instrumentation. To this end, Bragg took the more political songs like "Another Man's Beauty Undone" while Tweedy took more eclectic songs like "California Stars". Squabbles started over production, with Bragg preferring his typical heavy production while Bennett seeking a more stripped down song. Eventually, the two groups pretty much just made their own halves of the album, alternating songs. Despite the conflict, the album was another critical success, while Son Volt's next two albums fell flat.

At the same time, the band was working on their third album, Summerteeth. The album was the first to be electrically produced, something that caused issues within the band. Outside of the band, the label was also unhappy, as belt tightening caused by Time Warner's acquisition of AOL had reached the Warner Bros music division, who now needed commercial sales, not just critical approval. The label demanded a radio single against the wishes of Tweedy, who agreed to remix one song for radio, which flopped. Despite continued critical acclaim, Summerteeth did not sell well, leaving the label unhappy.

While in the process of writing the music for their fourth album which would become Yankee Hotel Foxtrot, Tweedy performed with Jim O'Rourke at a festival in Chicago, and ended up forming a side group with him and drummer Glenn Kotche called Loose Fur. This is the second time we've seen O'Rourke, after he brought John Fahey back to music. O'Rourke is a noted guitarist  whose work has touched indie rock, post rock and experimental music. At the time, he was also a member of Sonic Youth. When Wilco had finished recording their fourth album, which would become Yankee Hotel Foxtrot, Tweedy was unhappy with the drumming and production. He brought in Kotche and O'Rourke to rerecord several of the drums parts and remaster the entire album, causing Coomer to leave the band, to be replaced by Kotche full time. Bennett would follow after the album was released. O'Rourke stripped much of the supporting backgrounds, at times leaving only the parts played by members of Loose Fur, and added the shortwave radio samples that would give the album its name among many other changes. At the same time, Warner Bros was still looking to cut costs and when news that Wilco was making an experimental album with no single and jettisoning members hit the ears of executives, they were ready to drop the band. In order to avoid bad publicity, the label agreed to let the band keep the masters for no charge, a remarkably generous offer.

While looking for a new label, the band released the album for free on their website. As the reviews began to flow in, the album accrued hype. Pitchfork gave it a 10. "Masterpiece" and comparisons to Radiohead were thrown around. On the back of the hype, the band signed with Nonesuch Records, who were themselves a Warner Bros subsidiary. Despite being written before 9/11, songs like Jesus Etc. and Ashes of American Flags drew attention for seemingly reflecting the realities of a post-9/11 America, and the album sold well even after being available for free. While Being There, Summerteeth and Yankee Hotel Foxtrot was a fantastic run, the band would never release an album as good as those three and still putters along to this day, releasing pretty good indie/art rock. Tweedy released a well received solo album a few weeks ago. Jay Bennett died in 2009 from a fentanyl patch overdose.

# Hargrove's review

*Yankee Hotel Foxtrot* is the fourth of five great albums from last week. Spotify says I first listened to this album in early June of 2017, which lines up with my memories. It was one of the first albums I got into after I decided I wanted to get more into music and chose indie rock as my primary genre. My dad is a big fan of Wilco’s earlier alt-country work and an even bigger fan of Uncle Tupelo, but *Yankee Hotel Foxtrot* would never have been the album he would recommend. Instead, it was Pitchfork I have to thank for me trying this album out. I remember being blown away by “I Am Trying to Break Your Heart” on first listen. The sound collage elements were unlike anything I had ever heard before and the cryptic lyrics defied my attempts to interpret them (look, “aquarium drinker” is much easier to parse for a 24 year old than a 16 year old). I listened to it a lot that summer, sticking “Jesus, Etc.” and “Heavy Metal Drummer” onto my regular rotation playlists. “Heavy Metal Drummer” even made it onto my basement playlist as music chair in college. During COVID, I revisited the album and got really into “Pot Kettle Black”, still one of my favorite songs. More recently, I spent a lot of time with the record the week after the 2024 election, right after quitting my job; I became obsessed with “Ashes of American Flags” and added that to my GOATed *Yankee Hotel Foxtrot*s songs collection. While I like most of the songs on this album, those are the ones that have really stuck with me at different points in my life. An album having five songs that make it that far is a rare thing. As I continue to revisit and evolve my feelings on this album, I’m no longer as impressed by the noisiness of the musique concrète collages. I also grew out of a lot of indie rock a few years ago; I still love a lot of indie rock, but it’s far from my primary genre these days. And yet, every time I come back to this album I still find something to love. Tweedy’s lyricism is almost romantic in its self-deprecation and certain lines pack more folksy wisdom punch than entire country albums. The melodies are simple but infectious, the perfect complement to Tweedy’s stark sincerity. Though what remains of Wilco’s early country sound is largely vestigial by this album, its country soul remains. Tweedy is a singer whose home isn’t on the range, but rather in the Sears Tower. It’s an album that finds the roots growing out of the cracks in the sidewalk and wraps them around any disaffected yuppie with the heart to accept a little authenticity. It makes me feel seen.

“I Am Trying to Break Your Heart” is such a phenomenal album opener. It’s nearly 7 minutes long, but not one second feels wasted. Every time I hear “I am an American aquarium drinker/I assassin down the avenue”, I feel at home. There’s so much that I love about this song that I could talk about, but, to keep it brief, I love the way it chugs along constantly like a steam engine. No matter what is going on in the surroundings, that drum beat will connect each and every verse. And the dissonant piano is a personal favorite. And every time I hear “I’d be lying if I said it wasn’t easy”, fuck, what a line. “Kamera” has a great chorus but I’ve always been underwhelmed by it as a song. I just don’t love the verses. It’s a catchy ditty with nice psychedelic qualities, but it just feels insubstantial coming after the opener. “Radio Cure” feels like a song I should be iffy on for similar reasons, but I’ve always liked it. I love the cadence of the verses. I’m not quite sure what the lyrics mean, but they always make me sad. The slow burn is great and leads into such a restrained, tasteful, aching explosion at the end. “War on War” is a simple avant-pop bop. It’s nothing flashy, but man is it fun to hear. “Jesus, Etc.” takes the cake as best song on the album. It’s just so pretty, from the fiddle to the other country elements to the subdued vocals to the heartbreaking lyrics to the Nashville melody. “last cigarettes are all you can get” guts me every time.

“Ashes of American Flags” is also just fantastic. “All my lies are always wishes/I know I would die if I could come back new” might be the hardest any lyric on this album hits. I’m also partial to “I shake like a toothache”. Those unexpected strings on the final chorus give me shivers every time. “Heavy Metal Drummer” rounds out an immaculate three track run as this album’s best banger. It’s crazy that *Yankee Hotel Foxtrot* had a song that could be consistently played in the basement. The drums are a highlight, but overall it’s just a fun, sunny track. “I’m the Man Who Loves You” is unfortunately a pretty bad miss and the absolute low point on the album. I actively dislike this song. The verses sound like total word vomit that Tweedy can’t eject fast enough. The horns near the end are laughable. The Lou Reed “I Heard Her Call My Name’ guitar solo is not needed. I just don’t much care for it. “Pot Kettle Black”, though, is as good as any song here. “It’s become so obvious/You are so oblivious to yourself”. What a line. I love singing along to every word but the chorus in particular build so much energy out of nowhere. In addition to “I’m the Man Who Loves You”, this album’s biggest weakness is that it ends on kind of a whimper. Though I’m torn on “Poor Place”, it’s the better of the final two tracks. The chorus is so good, but it takes three minutes of a total slog to get there. I would be more willing to tolerate that slog if this were the closer, but no, you have “Reservations” to end things on 8 minutes of ambience. It’s not catastrophic, but it’s a bummer closer to an album that has been so good up to this point.

This album isn’t quite perfect, but man is it so endearing. It’s simply one of the best documents of pure, unadulterated indie rock from that decade’s heyday. I love its simplicity, its pop catchiness, and, frankly, the amount I relate to Jeff Tweedy. I hope to continue revisiting and rediscovering this album as I grow older.

Favorite tracks: I Am Trying to Break Your Heart, Radio Cure, War on War, Jesus, Etc., Ashes of American Flags, Heavy Metal Drummer, Pot Kettle Black

Least favorite track: I’m the Man Who Loves You

# Aidan's review

I could gush for pages about how good this album is, but what really interests me is how it compares to the rest of Tweedy's work. The album is infused with the emotion that he barely showed a sign of in Uncle Tupelo or in earlier Wilco albums. Part of that is the drumming, which felt too often taken for granted when done by Coome. Under Kotche's command, the drums introduce hesitation to Tweedy's voice where it never was before. Tweedy himself also explores new avenues of songwriting to explore, where he had always been simple and definite in previous songs, he finds beauty in nonsense like "You are not my typewriter" and "I think you have no motive/ I know you have no home". It's just insane to watch a guy that was so consistent in his output suddenly find genius after 7 albums that ranged from ok to pretty good. O'Rourke's stripped down production is another piece of the puzzle, as he slowly introduces the radio static over the earlier songs on the album that builds to a crescendo in the titular sample in Reservations. He's stripped Wilco of all of the annoying quirks, the refusal to end with a sad song, the weird overproduced Elvis Costello wannabe songs, and given the band the weight it had always lacked. Sure, there's some echos of what they would achieve here on songs like "Via Chicago", "Understood" and "Red Eyed and Blue" on earlier albums, but this is the mature expression of those ideas like never before.  The mythos of this album also doesn't hurt, as songs like "War on War", "Ashes of American Flags" and "Jesus Etc." are given new meaning to the audience of hippies who suddenly find the nation in the grips of jingoism that Tweedy sings about as some sort of hipster Cassandra. Great, great album, never disappoints, I could listen to it for hours on end, perfectly finished project, probably would be a desert island disc for me. Can't praise it enough.
