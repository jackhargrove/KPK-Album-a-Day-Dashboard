# Write-up

William Basinski is an experimental musician and sound artist born in 1958 and raised in Houston. He was classically trained in clarinet as a child and attended the University of North Texas to study jazz saxophone. There, he discovered minimalist composers like Steve Reich and ambient musicians like Brian Eno, inspiring him to make music with tape loops. He spent the 80s making music from tapes and shortwave radio sounds, but did not release any of it until much later. He did, however, begin playing saxophone professionally in 1989, once opening for David Bowie. Basinski created his debut album *Shortwavemusic* in 1982, but would not release it until 1998. He released his second album, *Watermusic*, in 2001.

While living in New York City, Basinski decided to transfer his archival tapes from the 80s and 90s to digital. Unfortunately for him, magnetic tape doesn’t last forever. Even professionally made VHS and cassette tapes degrade on a relatively short time span, showing noticeable wear within a decade or two. His home tapes didn’t stand a chance. As he played these old tapes, he could hear the sound quality get worse each loop as they disintegrated piece by piece. He was moved by the sound of his own music slowly but surely turning into dust, and he decided to turn it into a project. Basinski worked on this project from his home in Brooklyn through the lovely month of September 2001. He woke up early in the morning on the 11th, the final day of the project. It was nice out, so he decided to go up on the roof of his building to record the last of his tapes crumble. As he was doing so, 19 terrorists hijacked four commercial planes, flying two of them into the Twin Towers just across the East River to kill over 3,000 people. He took photos of the aftermath from his roof that were used as covers for the music made during this project, forever tying the disintegration of his tapes with the disintegration of the World Trade Center and the post-Cold War calm of the 90s.

The project, titled *The Disintegration Loops*, was released in four installments in 2002 and 2003. Each is approximately 75 minutes, making the full project total around 5 hours. Today, we are only listen to the first installment, as it’s the one indicated on our list (and the most acclaimed). The same melody plays throughout this installment, beginning quite clear but becoming a tattered and broken shadow of itself by the end. *The Disintegration Loops* has been acclaimed as one of the best conceptual ambient projects of all time, receiving particular glaze from Pitchfork over the years. Its legacy is permanently entwined with the September 11 attacks, soundtracking MoMA’s 10 year remembrance in 2011 and a major artistic memorial of the event in 2022. Basinski has continued to have a successful career, consistently appearing on the Billboard New Age chart and receiving acclaim for his 2017 tribute to Bowie, *A Shadow in Time*.

# Hargrove's review

I listened to “dlp 1.1” one time in like 2018 because I was a real diehard Pitchfork guy at that time and I saw they had glazed the living hell out of this album. I did think the concept and the context were both pretty cool, but clearly it’s not the kind of thing that especially grips me if I never went back to it. I do love telling its story to people though, because it is genuinely pretty crazy. Overall, purely conceptual albums are rarely my thing. Something like the Caretaker’s *An empty bliss beyond this World* can get me to go “hmm, cool”, but it’s rare that I’ll find it particularly resonant. I think *The Disintegration Loops* is about as well thought out and well executed as a concept can be. Which is probably good to get it like C tier. It’s genuinely super cool to hear the lifecycle of a tune, from full and bright to dead and decayed. And I think that just letting it play for over an hour is the only way to really get this effect across. Unfortunately, it just doesn’t lend itself to a particularly gripping album experience. I do think it would be really cool in some kind of sound art exhibit at a museum, but that’s just a different kind of listening entirely. I like “dlp 1.1” better than “dlp 2.1” for three reasons. First, I think the loop is just better. It’s a heavenly sound with lots of moving parts that both make it sound better and provide a greater contrast between the beginning and the end. The loop in “dlp 2.1” is super eerie, which is cool in its own way, but I think the dreamy, ethereal loop of the first track works better for the concept. Secondly, though I complained about the length a little bit earlier, if I’m going to listen to this album, I want the full effect. That requires the punishing length. The hour I spend on “dlp 1.1” feels more worthwhile than the 10 minutes I spend on “dlp 2.1”. Finally, I prefer how the loop in “dlp 1.1” disintegrates more completely. It goes from this grandiose sound at the beginning to truly just four clicks and some reverb by the end. If nothing else, “dlp 1.1” is the perfect proof of concept for this project. Which, I imagine, is why it’s “dlp 1.1” in the first place. I don’t have much more to say than that. Cool concept, not something I would go out of my way to listen to. But maybe I should test it out sometime in a truly ambient manner, like while I’m reading. I also would like to do all four installments in one sitting someday. Maybe during the break.

Favorite tracks: dlp 1.1

Least favorite track: dlp 2.1

# Aidan's review

As Ambient 1 established, I'm a sucker for tape loops and this is another brilliant ambient album incorporating them. What really unlocked Ambient 1 for me was hearing it performed live and feeling the tenuousness of the music as the environment actively rebelled against it. Here the result is quite similar, as Basinski places his simple melodies in conflict with the ravages of time in the form of the mechanical limits of magnetic tapes. It's strikingly haunting and beautiful to hear flaws start to whisper and then slowly advance to take over the entire melody.
