# Write-up

The only album from the first wave of punk on our list, Pink Flag was Wire's debut album, coming a mere month after the Sex Pistols released their provocative classic "Never Mind the Bollocks...". While the Sex Pistols were working class and favored sheer anger over any artistic flourish (one of their members, Sid Vicious, could barely play), Wire were middle class art school kids. The original lineup was a 5 piece band, but after guitarist George Gill broke his leg, they dropped him. The remaining 4, Graham Lewis, Colin Newman, Bruce Gilbert and Robert Grey, put out Pink Flag in November of 1977, after the breakneck emergence of punk in 1976. The mood in Britain was dire, with stagnation and unemployment coming over the country, and punk put that anger into simple, guttural music. Newman's vocals display that sheer hatred that punk was known for, but Lewis's lyrics are more wry than the hatred that the Pistols put out in songs like "Bodies", which was referred to as being "effectively anti-abortion, anti-woman, and anti-sex." In comparison, the "Punk Floyd" (as the British press referred to them) were more nuanced in their criticism, mocking the tabloids, the 9-5 lifestyle and in true punk fashion, love itself. The band's influence was limited in the brief explosion that was the first wave of punk, with Pink Flag struggling to sell well. However, both hardcore punk (Black Flag and Big Black were fans) and post-punk were enormously indebted to the innovations on this "punk suite". The band went on to record 2 more albums that went post-punk and beyond, "Chairs Missing" and "154", the former of which is also on the list, and after breaking up in 1980, has reformed twice and is currently making new music.

# Hargrove's review

One of the better truly punk albums that I’ve ever heard, even if it’s not truly punk. It certainly sounds punk. For the most part. Except maybe a few of the songs in the final quarter. But it definitely has a certain artistic quality, a level of cerebral detachment, that you don’t get from the Sex Pistols. The vulgar British accent certainly makes it sound punk. The guitars are a wall of fuzz from beginning to end, sometimes even leading melodic duties to the bass. And the bass is free to take up the melody sometimes because the drums are absolutely *killing* it rhythmically. It’s insane how good the drumming on this album is. When I sat down to do my note-taking listen, I left “great drums” as a note for early every song. The choice of percussion for every song is perfect, and something in the production highlights them so perfectly. They sound so incredibly crisp, a little higher in the mix than I would expect, and there’s just a brilliant mastery of the hi-hat. A complete standout of the album for me. Rhythm in general gets a little more attention than you would expect for a punk album made by 4 whities in England. Nothing is danceable, we’re not yet to Gang of Four and the dance punks, but the seeds of it are very, very clear. The drums and bass really elevate this record above what it should have been. There’s some pretty decent songwriting too, and a lot of personality in the vocals.

“Reuters” doesn’t do anything special as an opener, but man is it a good song. It’s a great example of the lyrics and the music complementing each other well. I really enjoy the lyrical story of a reporter in a war-torn country, and the overwhelming fuzzy guitars build an oppressive and apocalyptic mood to match. This song is up there with The Cure in its ability to craft its own world through the music. “Three Girl Rhumba” is one of my absolute favorites on this album, thanks in no small part to its guitar riff. The guitar is so fuzzy but at the same time bright and defiant, I think it’s one of the best on the album. I would be remiss not to mention how great it is when Colin Newman says “the impossible”. “Pink Flag” reminds me of “Reuters” in the way that the guitar dominates everything else (although the drums remain crisp). Once the accelerando begins, we get one of the most musically interesting parts of the album, and the drums go DUMMY. “106 Beats That” starts out as a pretty typical song for this album, but the second half has a complete freakout from the guitar and what I imagine has to be a cold, driving keyboard or synth line even though neither appear in the album’s credits. “Strange” is the longest track on the album at nearly 4 minutes, and it heralds the beginning of this album’s greatest segment in the final quarter where the songs get more rhythmically intricate and dare I say funky? The guitar on this track is reminiscent of “Three Girl Rhumba”. I don’t really know what’s happening with the background noises, but they create a thick atmosphere. “Fragile” has some of the finest guitar work on the album, as well as stellar vocals. In particular, I enjoy how the vocals build from verse to chorus so seamlessly. “Mannequin” is far and away the best song on the album. The bass is walking up and down and all around the town. The chorus is crazy catchy, and I love the “ooohs” and “la la las”. The closer “1 2 X U” is great. I think the relative calm of the vocals contrasts greatly with the explosive instrumentation in between.

List of songs where I noted how good the drums are: “Field Day for the Sundays”, “Lowdown”, “Start to Move”, “Brazil”, “It’s So Obvious”, “Pink Flag”, “The Commercial”, “Straight Line”, “106 Beats That”, “Fragile”

For how good so much of this album is, a lot of it is also quite rough. I appreciate the brevity of the songs for the fast-paced effect it creates, but I would love to sit with an idea for a little longer, and when you do get normal length tracks like “Pink Flag” or “Strange” it’s somewhat of a relief. A few of those shorter tracks left very little impression on me even after numerous listens, like “Brazil”, “It’s So Obvious”, “Straight Line”, and “Different to Me”. There were a couple of songs I just outright disliked as well. I enjoyed the poppy-ness of “Ex Lion Tamer” but the lyrics just rubbed me the wrong way. If you went off of the punk of the late 70s and early 80s, you would think TV was the greatest threat to society in history. “Lowdown” just bugged me for some reason and I couldn’t put my finger on why. The instrumental is kind of static for so much of it, and the almost spoken word vocals don’t do it for me. “Surgeon’s Girl” was my least favorite track, it felt a little too heavy on the punk and a little light on the art. The vocals also get super irritating by the end. Overall though, the good points far outweigh the bad.

Favorite tracks: Reuters, Three Girl Rhumba, 106 Beats That, Strange, Fragile, Mannequin, 1 2 X U

Least favorite track: Surgeon’s Girl

# Aidan's review

Apparently the secret to making a good punk album is to cram as many songs as possible into a sub 40 run time. Wire sprints through 21 songs in under 36 minutes, hitting their top speed on my personal favorite, the 29 second Field Day for the Sundays. The opening few tracks are pure punk, drums, guitar and wry lyrics buried under a thick British accent. When the lyrics do come through they’re great, and cover the usual punk topics. These guys hate love, they hate TV and they hate jobs. Thatcher only gets saved by the virtue of the album coming out a year before she becoming PM. The last third of the album is the most impressive to me as the group runs through the styles of the future, starting with Fragile’s Dunedin sound, and continuing with the indie rock of Mannequin, the hardcore punk of Different to Me and the post punk of Champs and Feeling Called Love, and rounding out with the return to classic punk in 1 2 X U. It’s a tour de force of everywhere punk would go before the Pistols had even broken up. It’s punk at its most creative, embracing the freedom from the cliches of rock to make something new. You can claim it’s more influential than enjoyable, but that’s just because how insanely influential it is. There are forgettable songs among the 21, but punk was never about staying perfect, just being there for a cut or two, and Wire does that many times over.
