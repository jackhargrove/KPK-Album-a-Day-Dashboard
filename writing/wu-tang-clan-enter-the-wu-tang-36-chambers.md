# Write-up

Hip hop was created in New York City in 1973, and for about 15 years it was dominated by the city that birthed it. But in the late 80s, the West Coast snatched the crown and monopolized the rap radio airwaves. The East Coast was in the wilderness for years with no idea of how to compete with the funky gangstas of California. Enter: The Wu-Tang.

Wu-Tang Clan is a collective of hip hop artists from Staten Island (referred to as “Shaolin”) unofficially led by RZA, who produces all the beats. The original 9 members of the Wu-Tang Clan are producer RZA (Robert Diggs) and rappers GZA (Gary Grice), Ol’ Dirty Bastard (Russell Jones), Method Man (Clifford Smith), Raekwon (Corey Woods), Ghostface Killah (Dennis Coles), Inspectah Deck (Jason Hunter), U-God (Lamont Hawkins), and Masta Killa (Elgin Turner). The first predecessor group to Wu-Tang was formed in the late 80s and consisted of RZA, GZA, and Ol’ Dirty Bastard, who are all cousins. This early work built their reputation in NYC, but didn’t catch on broadly. RZA then met Ghostface Killah, and the two decided to put together a group that was based on “Eastern philosophy picked up from kung fu movies, Five-Percent Nation teachings picked up on the New York streets, and comic books.” They chose the name “Wu-Tang” from RZA’s favorite movie, *Shaolin and Wu Tang*. Their low-budget and gritty debut album *Enter the Wu-Tang (36 Chambers)* was a surprising success, and it provided a way forward for East Coast hip hop after years of West Coast gangsta rap hegemony. In the years that followed, every individual member of Wu-Tang put out solo albums that featured RZA production, 3 of which are also on this list. Wu-Tang Clan as a collective put out 6 more albums between 1997 and 2015, but the solo albums are the real successors to the original Wu-Tang record. All original members of the Wu-Tang Clan are alive except for Ol’ Dirty Bastard, who died of a drug overdose in 2004. Just last month, Wu-Tang announced that they would embark on a farewell tour this Summer before putting the group to rest for good.

*Enter the Wu-Tang (36 Chambers)* is named after the movies *Enter the Dragon* and *The 36th Chamber of Shaolin*. The production of the album was truly lo-fi, with everything being recorded and produced in a tiny NY studio with shitty equipment. With 9 rappers all fighting for space on 1 record, RZA would force the other members to have rap battles with each other, with the winners getting to put verses on his beats. RZA’s production was innovative in that his samples pull from broader sources than the standard funk and soul of most other hip hop, as well as the fact that he uses “the sampler more like a painter’s palette than a Xerox.” Kanye West has called RZA the single biggest influence on his production. Many fans cite the varied personalities and styles of each Wu-Tang member as one of the best parts of the record (from Pitchfork: “Half the charm is in the cast's idiosyncrasies: ODB's hovering sing-song, Raekwon's fake stutter, Ghostface's verbal tics, Method Man's hazy, dusted voice.”). The album’s success, minimalist production, hardcore rapping, and sense of humor revitalized the East Coast scene, paving the way for rappers like Nas, Biggie, and eventually Jay-Z to become superstars.

# Hargrove's review

Another album I’m deeply familiar with, another short late night review from a “man” who has an increasingly concerning relationship with alcohol. I’ve never loved this album. There’s just something about it that isn’t smooth in the way that I generally love my hip hop, and there aren’t particularly interesting bouts of lyricism that make up for it. That being said, I had a lot of fun listening to this record today. I really like ODB. I really, really do. His style of rapping is genuinely unique in a genre that often rewards technical ability. He’s almost more of a singer than a rapper, and his lyrics are fucking gross. In terms of technical ability, I don’t think you can beat Ghostface Killah. Everyone says GZA is the man to beat, but I’ve never agreed with that conventional wisdom. Method Man has some moments that are transcendent, and Raekwon delivers some consistent bars, Inspectah Deck is largely forgettable, and U-God shouldn’t be on it at all. Thankfully, the competitive capitalist method of verse allocation generally. meant that the better rappers rapped more. I don’t know how this isn’t a more popular system. “C.R.E.A.M.” is genuinely deserving of its stature, the hook couldn’t be better. “Method Man” is my personal favorite track, the intro skit is just iconic and his verses are hilarious; just the right amount of pop culture references. My favorite Method Man bar comes from “Shame on a Nigga”: "I sever, the head from the shoulders, I'm better than my compet'ta/You mean competitor, whatever, let's get together”. Love that meta shit. My favorite line from GZA is “I gamed Ella, the bitch caught a Fitz like Gerald-/-ine Ferraro, who's full of sorrow” off “Clan in Da Front”. My favorite verse from Ghost is on “Can It Be All So Simple”. I think “Da Mystery of Chessboxin” has the best verses of the album, but the production is lacking. Speaking of production, I really do love RZA. I think he’s the exact same type of autistic that I am. But I do sometimes find his beats a little repetitive, a little static. I think *Illmatic* jut does this style of production better. A deserved classic though, if not my personal favorite.

Favorite tracks: Bring Da Ruckus, Wu Tang: 7th Chamber, C.R.E.A.M., Method Man, Tearz

Least favorite tack: Da Mystery of Chessboxin’
