# Write-up

XTC was a band formed in Swindon, England in 1972 whose lineup at the time of this album was vocalist and guitarist Andy Partridge, bassist Colin Moulding, and multi-instrumentalist Dave Gregory. Although Partridge was the primary songwriter, Moulding wrote some tracks as well, and *Skylarking* had the highest proportion of Moulding tracks of any of their albums with 5/15. XTC have had considerable influence on a wide range of music, but they never found the level of success that you would expect because of their refusal to conform to the fashionable trends of every era in which they played. Partridge and Moulding were childhood friends who were enamored with bands like The Beatles and The Monkees as teenagers in the 60s. After forming various bands together and with guitarist Dave Gregory in the early 70s, they were blown away by American glam rock and proto-punk outfit New York Dolls. They started XTC in 1975 playing in a roughly glam rock style. Punk broke in 1977 and, although XTC did not play punk music at all, and in fact Partridge actively disliked the Sex Pistols, their unclassifiable attitude got them lumped in with the punk scene. Similarly, when New Wave became a thing, they got lumped in there too. Partridge disliked the terms “punk” and “New Wave”, preferring to just be called “pop”. XTC released its debut album in 1978, and would release four more by 1982. Their two biggest hits in that timeframe were 1979’s “Making Plans for Nigel” and 1982’s “Senses Working Overtime”. For one reason or another, their music was never particularly big in the UK, but they amassed a significant fanbase in the US.

By 1982, touring was beginning to take a huge toll on Partridge, and he basically completely lost his mind for a while. He finally had a panic attack shortly before a show, and he locked himself away in his house for so long that people speculated he died and bands started having XTC tribute concerts in his honor. They became a purely studio-based band afterward. Between no touring and years of mismanagement by their criminal manager, XTC essentially became slaves to Virgin Records. When they released their next album in 1983, people began to think they were behind the times. To re-center his mind, Partridge began a side project called The Dukes of Stratosphere, which was a band that pretended to be a lost group from the 60s. The resulting record was a big hit in the US. By 1986, however, Virgin had become dissatisfied with XTC’s performance and threatened to drop them if the next album wasn’t a hit. They hired legendary American musician Todd Rundgren to produce the album. The result was *Skylarking*, which was a big success on its own, but the b-side “Dear God” became a huge (and controversial, for its atheist themes) hit in the US. They were now a regular on the American college radio stations that would solidify the indie rock scene. They continued releasing music, and in 1989 played a fully acoustic recorded concert for MTV in New York; this would become the first of a series called MTV Unplugged, which Nirvana famously played in 1994. In 1993, XTC went on strike from their label, and were eventually released from their contract. XTC ultimately released 12 albums before disbanding in 2006. Despite never fully breaking through into massive success, XTC was very influential on a bizarrely broad range of artists; U2, R.E.M., and INXS all styled their names after XTC. Kurt Cobain cited XTC alongside The Beatles as an example of good pop music. Various hardcore punk groups including Minor Threat have said they’re big fans, as have music legends Brian Eno and Peter Gabriel. XTC was the primary forerunner to Britpop, a mid-90s genre in the UK where bands like Oasis and Blur sought to adopt the 60s pop aesthetics that XTC kept alive through the 80s. And the UK side of the early 2000s post-punk revival scene also consider XTC one of the biggest influences. Not bad for a pop group!

# Hargrove's review

XTC, my favorite Beatles rip-off band (and there are many). This is only the third album we’ve done so far that I had actually listened to in a meaningful way before I intentionally got into music at age 16. When I was 12 or 13, my dad gave me 5 CDs for my new 5 disc rotation CD player. This was the first one in the rotation. I would go to sleep every night for months hitting play on the first CD and letting them play in order. Of course, that means I was really only listening to this one. However, my dad gave me the names in the wrong order, so I thought they were Jane’s Addiction for a long time! I really do love this record, and yes there’s some nostalgia involved but it is just such a beautiful, natural, warm pop record. The sound just floats all around you, caresses you softly, envelops you in an embrace of green grass and yellow sun. The lyrics are impressively evocative for how simple they are. The hooks are snappy and memorable. The vocals are deep and expressive. Just a wonderful album. “Summer’s Cauldron” begins with an orchestra of chirping crickets that I find really inventive and fun, and the line “Please don’t pull me out, this is how I would want to go”. The explosive drums in the chorus are a highlight as well. It forms a perfect couplet with track 2, “Grass”. I sometimes think songs flowing one into the next is a bit overrated, but it complements the free-form atmosphere so well. “Grass” is perhaps even nicer and dreamier than “Summer’s Cauldron”, with the description of a perfect summer day. The couplet idea is done again (but conceptually even better) with “Ballet for a Rainy Day” and “1000 Umbrellas” pair a lovely rainy day with a depressing one. The chorus of the former is so buttery smooth, and the verses so colorful. The sudden downer ending is a great segue into the latter, where the string orchestra soundtracks wonderful melodrama. Strings are just *perfect* for melodrama, always. I love the theatrical flourish in the lyrics, particularly at the “misery” part. “Season Cycle” is another with wonderful nature imagery, and another with a wonderful chorus of ecstasy. My favorite song overall is “That’s Really Super, Supergirl”, a psychedelic take on New Wave. The sarcastic lyrics are positively dripping with spite, rivaling Dylan at his most venomous. He is NOT happy with his ex. The extended analogy to Supergirl is great and funny, and wow is it catchy. Speaking of catchy, the other uptempo banger on here is “Earn Enough for Us”, the only song to ever make me feel a shred of empathy for poor people. I really end up feeling for the situation of the narrator and his family, he’s so hardworking and ambitious. And I have not been able to get that chorus out of my head for a decade now.

You might have noticed that all but one of the songs I’ve discussed so far are on the first half. With the exception of “The Meeting Place”, which is only pretty good, the first half of this record is flawless. The second half is quite good too, and has some serious standouts, but it’s not quite on the same level. To start with the standouts: “Another Satellite” is the most psychedelic song on the album, and also one of the best. Partridge stretches his voice in the most enjoyable way, and I’m impressed with how seamless the space references are. Great track. I was shocked at how moved I was by the lyrics of “Dying”, which I had never paid much attention to before. It’s an extremely sincere and frank look at death. The description is honestly terrifying. “Sacrificial Bonfire” is a very nice would-be closer, with one of my favorite lines: “Disguises wore thin/With less and less skin/And the clothes that were draped/Was all that told man/From ape”. The orchestration that should close the album is perfectly executed as well. There is, however, “Dear God” left. A very good track, but one that’s not supposed to be on the album. Still, I can’t imagine myself wanting to listen to a version without it. It’s witty, acerbic, and honestly just cathartic to listen to someone rage against God. The Beatlesy violin is great, and “I can’t believe in/I don’t believe in/I won’t believe in” is a stellar turn of phrase.

Of the remaining songs: “The Meeting Place” is fine, but it’s a step down from the rest of side 1. “Big Day” is my least favorite song by far, it’s honestly kind of annoying and it’s nowhere near as smooth as the rest of the album. “The Man Who Sailed Around His Soul” has a similar problem, but I enjoy the more psychedelic and progressive aspects. “Mermaid Smiled” is a pretty folk song, but overall forgettable.

This is simply one of my favorite albums. So lush. So gorgeous. So warm. It’s accessible too — anyone can enjoy it. What a treat.

Favorite tracks: Summer’s Cauldron, Grass, That’s Really Super Supergirl, Ballet for a Rainy Day, 1000 Umbrellas, Season Cycle, Earn Enough for Us, Another Satellite, Dying, Sacrificial Bonfire, Dear God

Least favorite track: Big Day

# Aidan's review

My background for XTC was very much on the opposite side of Hargrove’s. I had heard the singles “Dear God” and “Making Plans for Nigel”. I figured that the saccharide tones of the former were a one off, a la “Boys Don’t Cry” and that the true sound of the band was more like the latter, somewhere between new wave and post punk, firmly within the zeitgeist of the 80s. I was dead wrong. Skylarking is as sweet as they come, so much that I was mildly nauseated at first with the mix, which flattens the traditional radio friendly wall of sound into something that sounds rather simplistic. But after another listen or two, I grew to appreciate this as just a sparkling example of pure pop, with the blithe vocals and synths to match. It’s wonderful in its simplicity, owing as much to Eno’s Another Green World as to the Kinks, Beatles and Beach Boys that it wears on its sleeve. There’s not really a single song that stands out besides “Dear God” (originally relegated to a B-side before the Americans got involved), but over the 14 prior songs, there’s just enough variety to make this a throughly pleasant listen, even on repeat.
