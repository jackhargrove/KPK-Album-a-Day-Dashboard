# Write-up

Yes is an English prog band with a sizable discography and a sprawling history, with 23 studio albums, 18 live albums, and 20 different official members. "Close to the Edge" is the third of a four album run from 1971 to 1973 which they are best known for, along with "The Yes Album", "Fragile" and "Yessongs" (which is a live album). The band emerged from the wreckage of a psychedelic band, Mabel Greer's Toyshop. MGT was founded by Clive Bailey and Robert Hagger, who were later joined by Chris Squire, Peter Banks, Tony Kaye and Jon Anderson. After kicking out Hagger for Bill Bruford, Banks left to join another band, only to return and bump out Squire. The band decided that they needed a new name, and arrived on "Yes". Bruford quit for a bit, was replaced by Tony O'Reilly and Ian Wallace (later of King Crimson) at different times, only to be brought back. The band was so wowed by seeing their peers King Crimson that they realized they would have to up their game to compete, so they signed with Atlantic and started woodshedding, releasing an eponymous debut in 1970. Next shuffle of the band saw Banks leave after their second album, "Time and a Word", was recorded, with Steve Howe replacing him. Howe provided a huge creative spark for the band, and with him they began their legendary four album run. The first of the four was "The Yes Album", after which Kaye was out in favor of Rick Wakeman. "Fragile" was next and contains their hit song "Roundabout" whose opening section has become a popular meme (you'll know if when you hear it). In 1972, "Close to the Edge" was released, after which yet another shuffle saw Bruford to leave for King Crimson, to be replaced by Alan White. This marked the first peak of the band's success, after which their sixth album, "Tales from Topographical Oceans" failed to measure up commercially or critically, causing Wakeman to leave the band. The band released 4 more albums, with the last one fittingly titled "Drama", as the band began to break apart. They had reached the heights of commercial and critical success and now were seen as the old thing, while punk rock was all the rage. They whole absorbed the Buggles (of "Video Killed the Radio Star" fame) but were hemorrhaging members faster then they could replace them, and eventually the band officially ended in 1981, only to be reformed in 1983 following some half hearted projects by its members. At this point the core was now Squire, White and Trevor Rabin. The band's 1983 comeback album, 90125 was their biggest hit. While there is another 40 years of history during which the band managed to sustain its breakneck pace of changing members, it's not particularly relevant to today's album, "Close to the Edge". "Close to the Edge" features a wide range of influences, including the novel Siddhartha, Lord of the Rings and composer Jean Sibelius. It features a cover deigned by Roger Dean, who worked on many of the band's covers, and inspired by mountain lakes in northern England. Eddy Offard produced, Anderson and Howe did the main work writing the music, assisted by Squire and Offard. If you were somehow keeping track of the lineups, you would know that Wakeman and Bruford were also there, but they were far less involved in the creation of the album. Despite the difficulties recording, especially for Bruford, the album was a huge hit, drawing comparisons to Sgt Pepper and garnering accolades in addition to commercial sales. It has been rereleased 3 separate times, the latest time being this March, in a 7 disc edition.

# Hargrove's review

I don’t know exactly why, but I’ve been having a ton of trouble reviewing this album. I’ve given it close to 10 listens at this point, as well as two re-listens of Jethro Tull’s *Thick as a Brick* and one of King Crimson’s *In the Court of the Crimson King* for comparison. I think the trouble stems from the fact that this entire album is dripping from top to bottom with the everything about prog that I don’t like: the vocals are whiny and annoying, the compositions are grand and complex but without much meaning, and the instrumental parts are technically challenging in a way that borders on masturbatory. However, there are some really high highs on this album that find a way to shine despite all of that. The opener, “Close to the Edge”, is a very well-regarded prog track that I liked more than I expected to given my biases but that certainly didn’t live up to its stature. The vocals are annoying throughout, especially during the whisper duet in the “I Get Up, I Get Down” section. I also don’t think it justifies its 18 minute length, with the middle section in particular dragging. That being said, the primary chorus (“Close to the edge, down by a river”) is insanely catchy. I can’t stop playing it in my head. That has to count for something. I think the song is well-constructed melodically. I also think Wakeman’s various keyboard parts are a highlight, particularly the icy organ solo starting around 12:20. The overall structure reminds me of Bowie’s “Blackstar”, although I like that song much better. Still, I would say I like this opener more than I want to. The second track, “And You and I”, is unfortunately a bummer to me though. It’s a major step down from the opener, and I like it less every time I listen to it. I like the folky guitar parts, but I don’t think the underlying composition is very strong at all. In an album where the strength of composition has made me look the other way when it comes to annoying atmospheric elements, “And You and I” just doesn’t work for me. I did rather like “Siberian Khatru” though, perhaps even more than the opener. I think that synth line that appears at the beginning and then again about 3/4 of the way through the song is really good, and I wish they had built around it a little bit more. The song gets less interesting when they get into the main verses, but I do really like the chorus, particularly the vocal harmony on the last line of the chorus. Overall, I wanted to hate this album a lot more than I was able to. *Wish You Were Here* still stands as easily my favorite prog album. I’m putting it right below Type O Negative, which was similar in that it had a lot of overarching elements that I really disliked, but there were very strong moments that made their way through.

Favorite tracks: Close to the Edge, Siberian Khatru

Least favorite track: And You and I

# Aidan's review

Probably the biggest name in prog other than King Crimson, this edges out Fragile as the token Yes album. Hargrove has made his distaste for prog clear with our previous reviews, and I think this album is full of the cliches. Annoying vocals singing nonsensical lyrics, busy-ness for its own sake, and songs that feels like they meander rather than going anywhere. Nothing is that wrong about it, it's just not my bag, and there's nothing that raises it above the stereotype for me. The first track is by far the worse offender in my book, I don't like the opening nasally guitar and when the organ comes in, it manages to go from annoyingly simple to annoyingly knotty within 5 minutes. The other tracks are far less annoying. I really like the acoustic section on "And You and I" and they manage to get the right balance with the organ, and "Siberian Khartu" does the impossible and keeps a really nice drum beat going throughout the entire song. After how much I fell in love with "Thick as a Brick", this too often lacks the driving grooves that made me think I could like prog. Beyond the half of the album that is wasted on the title track, it's pretty solid, but the title track is among my least favorite prog songs so far. As a whole, it's below WYWH and above Court of the Crimson King for me. Completely unrelated to the music, but also the single most unenjoyable write-up I’ve had to do because they do nothing interesting besides change members every album.
