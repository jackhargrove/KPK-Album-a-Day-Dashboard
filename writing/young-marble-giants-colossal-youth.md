# Write-up


# Hargrove's review

Between Broadcast and now Young Marble Giants, the most unexpected niche I’ve discovered I enjoy from this list is the far post-punk side of indie pop with a female singer. I liked *Colossal Youth* a lot better than I expected to on first listen. The record truly sounds sparse, made up only of guitar stabs, echoing bass, icy electronic organ, and Alison Statton’s reserved but heartbreaking vocals. This is an absolutely frigid album, standing in stark contrast to the overbearing warmth of the last two records we listened to. Every element supports the overall effect, but I would be lying if I said that Statton’s vocals weren’t the highlight for me. Wow does she kill it on this record. She conveys with piercing honesty the experience of a quarter-life crisis, the terrifying reality of the end of youth, all without so much as a quiver in her cool, detached delivery. It’s striking how well this style of voice works on me between Statton and Broadcast’s Trish Keenan. More than just the voice, though, is the writing in the lyrics. Statton’s percentage of lines that are absolutely gutting stands far above most lyricists. As for the instrumental, I enjoyed the way the minimalism complemented the icy vocals. Some songs feature a super cool interplay between brief stabs of guitar and echos of bass that sound like “Marquee Moon” cut down to its barest form. This album’s biggest weakness is the number of shorter tracks that don’t leave an impression. While the minimalism in every other facet worked great, I would have liked a chance to explore more of some of these tracks. Still, this was a delightful listen. I can now add a 22 year old Welsh woman to the list of lyricists that get me.

*Colossal Youth* starts with one of its best songs, “Searching for Mr Right”. The clicky bass and simple guitar epitomize the minimalist “Marquee Moon” thing I was talking about right away. Statton’s whole deal on this track captures the uncertainty at the end of youth about as well as *The Graduate*. And of course, there’s that sweet, sweet dub bass. The lyrics of “Include Me Out” feature a cool punk take on the 60s. Statton kills it on vocals over a Wobble-esque bass line and a country rock guitar solo. Another great track. “The Taxi” never really makes much of itself and ends up largely forgettable. I like the clever juxtaposition of mundane activities with horrors in the lyrics of “Eating Noddemix” and especially adore the spoken word part at the end. However, the underwhelming instrumental just barely keeps it out of my favorites. “Constantly Changing” features perhaps the coldest line on the album: “I cannot fix you in a position/Where I would lose you out of my vision/For you are movement/And that is nothing”. Welcome back, Bob Dylan! Statton sings a bit above her range but I find that straining endearing. “N.I.T.A.” uses a haunting two chord organ drone as a backdrop to just crushingly insightful lyrics: “It's nice to hear you're having a good time/But it still hurts cause you used to be mine/This doesn't mean that I possessed you/You're haunting me because I let you“. That one really got to me. I love it when an artist can analyze herself like that. “Colossal Youth” includes more lines that I like than I can feasibly include here. Let’s just say that an anti-narcissist sentiment like “If you think the world is a machine with one cog/And that cog is you or the things that you do/Then you are not in this world/The world is not you” or If you think the world is a balloon in your head/When it goes bang only you will be dead” will always make me jump up and down. On top of that, I love the bouncy organ line.

“Music for Evenings” has my favorite guitar line on the album, a sputtering motor in the heart of this song. The verse melody is lovely, and I get a kick out of “Now I'm not a neurotic/Or my business spasmodic”. I don’t care for “The Man Amplifier”, which has uninteresting lyrics, a grating organ line, and a frustrating vocal melody. It’s not the worst thing in the world, but it’s my least favorite from the album. “Choci Loni” brings back the country rock guitar, this time in a uniquely heavy atmosphere. “Wurlitzer Jukebox” contains an absolutely crazy bass line, especially jarring (in a good way) given the melancholy of the guitar and Statton’s vocals. “Salad Days” is nice but a bit on the unsubstantial side. The album saves two single-quality tracks for the end. The first, “Credit in the Straight World”, brings back the sparse “Marquee Moon” quality and has a great vocal melody. The second, “Brand - New - Life”, has to be the album’s masterpiece. The guitar riff sounds fully fleshed-out, the vocal melody is transcendent, the bass line chugs along beautifully, and the male vocals pair nicely with Statton’s. The line “So bad when you went away/Nothing I could do or say/And now we are a lonely two/Sit at home and watch the tube” hits hard. The album closes on “Wind in the Rigging”, a mechanical instrumental that I might otherwise criticize for being a weak ending, but I actually think it fits this album’s vibe.

I like Broadcast’s *Tender Buttons* ever so slightly better because I think it takes this sound and adds a noise element that I really enjoy. But I ended up liking this a LOT better than I expected to. Post-punk was just, like, the best, man. UK had an immaculate run from 1978-1983.

Favorite tracks: Searching for Mr Right, Include Me Out, Constantly Changing, N.I.T.A., Colossal Youth, Music for Evenings, Wurlitzer Jukebox, Credit in the Straight World, Brand - New - Life

Least favorite track: The Man Amplifier
